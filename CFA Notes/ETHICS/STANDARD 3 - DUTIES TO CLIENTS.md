## III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care

> **Client first. Best interest. Beneficiaries matter.**

## Core Concepts

1. The exam keeps hammering one thing: **client interests come before your own interests and before your employer's interests**.
2. “Loyalty” means you act for the client’s benefit. “Prudence” means you act with caution and sensible judgment. “Care” means you do not handle client money casually.
3. The first question is often **who exactly is the client**. In a pension plan or trust, ==the real client is usually the beneficiaries, not the sponsor or trustee who hired you.==
4. This standard is broader than formal legal fiduciary duty. Even if local law uses a narrower label, CFA still expects client-first conduct.
5. If you control client assets, your responsibility gets heavier. ==You should keep assets separate, provide proper reporting, and stay inside the governing documents and client mandate.==

### Violation Traps

1. **I thought** the person paying the fee is always the client. **Wrong logic:** the pension sponsor hired me, so I should do whatever helps the sponsor. **Correct logic:** ==when managing a pension or trust, my loyalty usually runs to the beneficiaries.== **Tested angle:** the exam often asks who the real client is, and the answer is usually the ultimate beneficiaries.

2. **I thought** employer pressure can sit beside client interest. **Wrong logic:** if my firm wants to push its own product, I can lean clients that way if the product is not terrible. **Correct logic:** I must recommend what is best for the client, not what is best for firm revenue. **Tested angle:** selling a house product is not automatically wrong, but favoring it over a better client option is.

3. **I thought** trade execution people are outside this standard. **Wrong logic:** I do not give advice, so my only job is to process the order. **Correct logic:** even execution professionals must act prudently and seek the most favorable terms reasonably available. **Tested angle:** "I only execute" is not a defense if the execution harms the client.

4. **I thought** I can follow the trustee's wishes even if beneficiaries lose. **Wrong logic:** the trustee is my contact person, so their instruction controls. **Correct logic:** I cannot knowingly take action that benefits the intermediary at the expense of the real client. **Tested angle:** instructions from an agent do not override duty to the actual client.

5. **I thought** diversification is optional if the client wants a concentrated bet. **Wrong logic:** if one stock looks amazing, loading up is just strong conviction. **Correct logic:** prudent management usually requires diversification unless the mandate clearly permits otherwise and the client understands the risk. **Tested angle:** concentration can be allowed only when it fits the stated objectives and constraints.

6. **I thought** custody issues are just back-office details. **Wrong logic:** as long as returns are fine, asset location and reporting are operational matters. **Correct logic:** when I control or can access client assets, reporting, segregation, and documentation become part of my ethical duty. **Tested angle:** weak controls over client assets can itself show lack of care.

7. **I thought** I can fix a bad client conflict with disclosure alone. **Wrong logic:** if I tell the client about my conflict, I may still proceed however I want. **Correct logic:** ==disclosure helps, but I still must act in the client's best interest.== **Tested angle:** disclosure does not excuse taking a clearly client-harming action.

### Not-a-Violation Traps

1. **I thought** this standard makes every member a legal fiduciary. **Wrong logic:** if CFA says client first, then every role becomes a full fiduciary under law. **Correct logic:** the standard sets a high ethical baseline, but whether a legal fiduciary duty exists depends on law and facts. **Tested angle:** ethical duty can apply even when the legal label is different.

2. **I thought** acting for all fund investors means I must personalize each investor's needs. **Wrong logic:** an index fund manager must tailor trades to each unit holder. **Correct logic:** when managing to a stated mandate, loyalty is shown by staying faithful to that mandate. **Tested angle:** pooled fund managers owe duty through the mandate, not through individualized suitability for each investor.

3. **I thought** prudence means never taking risk. **Wrong logic:** the safest portfolio is always the most prudent. **Correct logic:** prudence means appropriate risk for the client's objectives, horizon, and constraints. **Tested angle:** low risk is not automatically prudent if it fails the client's actual goals.

4. **I thought** a client loss proves disloyalty or imprudence. **Wrong logic:** if performance is poor, the manager must have violated III(A). **Correct logic:** a loss alone does not prove a breach if the process was careful and client-focused. **Tested angle:** the exam separates bad outcome from bad conduct.

5. **I thought** I must always obey the client's exact instruction immediately. **Wrong logic:** if the client says "buy now," my duty is blind obedience. **Correct logic:** I still must act with care and within the account's objectives, restrictions, and governing documents. **Tested angle:** client instruction matters, but it does not erase prudence.

6. **I thought** quarterly reporting is just a legal formality. **Wrong logic:** if the law is silent, detailed reporting does not matter ethically. **Correct logic:** regular, clear reporting supports the duty of care, especially when assets are under my control. **Tested angle:** good reporting is evidence of loyalty and care, not just paperwork.

7. **I thought** client approval cures every doubtful choice. **Wrong logic:** if I get a signature, the action becomes ethical. **Correct logic:** client approval helps when there is uncertainty, but it does not make an obviously harmful or improper action acceptable. **Tested angle:** consent matters, but client-first judgment still controls.

## III(B) Fair Dealing

> **Fairly to everyone. Not equally in every second. No favoritism.**

## Core Concepts

1. “Fairly” does **not** mean every client gets the information at the exact same second. It means you do not play favorites.
2. Fair dealing covers **investment analysis, recommendations, trade allocations, and other professional activities**.
3. Big exam trap: ==**fairly** is not the same as **equally**.== Different clients may receive different service levels if that difference is disclosed and legitimate.
4. Once a recommendation changes, you should have a process that gives all eligible clients a fair chance to act on it.
5. Allocation is a major test area. ==Hot issues, partial fills, and limited liquidity should not quietly go to favored clients.==

### Violation Traps

1. **I thought** fair dealing means I can reward my biggest clients first. **Wrong logic:** larger fee-paying clients deserve first access to good ideas. **Correct logic:** I cannot favor one client or group when disseminating recommendations or allocating trades. **Tested angle:** premium clients may pay for more service, but not for unfair access to recommendations or trades.

2. **I thought** my friends and family accounts can be handled first if they are small. **Wrong logic:** their orders are tiny, so no one is hurt. **Correct logic:** personal preference is still favoritism if it changes who gets access or execution first. **Tested angle:** even small favored accounts can create a fair dealing violation.

3. **I thought** a recommendation is private until I finish telling my best clients. **Wrong logic:** I can slowly roll out a new rating over several days to selected accounts. **Correct logic:** I need a distribution process designed to treat all clients fairly. **Tested angle:** controlled batch communication can be okay; selective tipping is not.

4. **I thought** hot issue allocation can follow relationship value. **Wrong logic:** clients who bring more assets should get the scarce initial public offering shares. **Correct logic:** ==scarce opportunities must be allocated by a fair method, not by favoritism.== **Tested angle:** oversubscribed offerings are classic fair dealing fact patterns.

5. **I thought** verbal hints are not recommendations. **Wrong logic:** if I quietly tell one client "take a look at this name," I have not really distributed research. **Correct logic:** soft, early hints can still unfairly advantage one client. **Tested angle:** the exam often hides unfair dealing inside informal conversations.

6. **I thought** partial fills can be fixed after the fact however I want. **Wrong logic:** I can give the best prices to the accounts I like and average the rest loosely. **Correct logic:** allocations should follow a fair, pre-established approach. **Tested angle:** trade allocation mechanics matter, not just the final recommendation.

7. **I thought** I can let large clients front-run the publication because the public note will come later. **Wrong logic:** as long as everyone eventually gets the report, the sequence does not matter. **Correct logic:** timing matters when early access changes investment opportunity. **Tested angle:** "everyone got it eventually" is not a defense when one group got a meaningful head start.

### Not-a-Violation Traps

1. **I thought** fair dealing means identical service for every client. **Wrong logic:** all clients must receive the same depth of review, same frequency of meetings, and same product menu. **Correct logic:** service levels may differ by contract as long as recommendations and opportunities are handled fairly. **Tested angle:** fairly does not mean mechanically identical treatment.

2. **I thought** batch dissemination is automatically unfair. **Wrong logic:** unless every client receives the alert at the same second, III(B) is violated. **Correct logic:** a reasonable dissemination system can still be fair even if messages go out in sequence. **Tested angle:** process quality matters more than literal simultaneity.

3. **I thought** different account suitability means different actions are unfair. **Wrong logic:** if not every client buys the same stock, I must be discriminating. **Correct logic:** clients can be treated differently when their mandates and constraints differ. **Tested angle:** suitability differences can justify different portfolio actions without violating fair dealing.

4. **I thought** a waiting list for an oversubscribed deal is always unfair. **Wrong logic:** only pro rata allocation is ethical. **Correct logic:** several allocation methods can be fair if they are objective and consistently applied. **Tested angle:** the method matters less than whether it is neutral and consistently used.

5. **I thought** one client can receive a recommendation first if they specifically requested research on that sector. **Wrong logic:** any early delivery is favoritism. **Correct logic:** individualized service may be acceptable if it is part of the normal service relationship and does not unfairly disadvantage others. **Tested angle:** context matters; favoritism is the problem, not every timing difference.

6. **I thought** a recommendation change must always be sent through one single channel. **Wrong logic:** using email for some and portal alerts for others is automatically unfair. **Correct logic:** different communication channels are acceptable if the overall process is fair and reliable. **Tested angle:** communication medium is less important than equal opportunity to act.

7. **I thought** clients harmed by market movement during dissemination prove unfair dealing. **Wrong logic:** if one client trades later and gets a worse price, III(B) must be violated. **Correct logic:** fair dealing requires a fair process, not guaranteed identical outcomes in a moving market. **Tested angle:** fairness of procedure matters more than perfect outcome matching.

## III(C) Suitability

> **Know the client. Know the portfolio. Match the mandate.**

## Core Concepts

1. Before advising, you must make a **reasonable inquiry** into the client's experience, objectives, risk and return goals, and financial constraints.
2. ==Suitability is judged in the context of the **total portfolio**, not one shiny investment viewed alone.==
3. Client information must be **updated regularly**. An old investment policy statement can become dangerous if the client's life changes.
4. When managing to a mandate, strategy, or style, the test becomes simple: ==**stay consistent with the stated mandate and constraints**.==
5. High return does not rescue an unsuitable investment. A good product for one client can be a terrible fit for another.

### Violation Traps

1. **I thought** age decides suitability by itself. **Wrong logic:** older client means conservative, younger client means aggressive. **Correct logic:** suitability depends on the actual facts, including wealth, liabilities, risk tolerance, and goals. **Tested angle:** the exam loves two clients of different ages where the older client can actually take more risk.

2. **I thought** a great investment can be recommended to everyone. **Wrong logic:** if expected return is high enough, suitability is obvious. **Correct logic:** no investment is suitable in the abstract; it must fit the client's profile and portfolio. **Tested angle:** "good investment" does not equal "suitable investment."

3. **I thought** the client's written objective is enough forever. **Wrong logic:** once the investment policy statement is signed, I can rely on it for years. **Correct logic:** I must update client circumstances and constraints regularly. **Tested angle:** marriage, retirement, inheritance, illness, and job loss are classic update triggers.

4. **I thought** each position can be judged alone. **Wrong logic:** a risky security is fine if the client agrees to that one trade. **Correct logic:** ==I must judge suitability in the context of the total portfolio.== **Tested angle:** the exam often hides unsuitability in overconcentration or correlation with existing holdings.

5. **I thought** client enthusiasm proves suitability. **Wrong logic:** if the client begs for private credit, options, or crypto, I should just facilitate it. **Correct logic:** I still need to determine whether it fits the client's objectives, constraints, and capacity for loss. **Tested angle:** client demand does not erase the suitability duty.

6. **I thought** a pooled mandate can drift if the manager has conviction. **Wrong logic:** a value fund can buy growth names if the manager thinks they are temporarily cheap. **Correct logic:** ==when managing to a stated style or mandate, actions must stay consistent with that mandate.== **Tested angle:** style drift is a standard violation pattern.

7. **I thought** diversification alone makes an investment suitable. **Wrong logic:** if it helps diversify, it must be appropriate. **Correct logic:** diversification is only one part of suitability. Liquidity needs, tax issues, legal restrictions, and risk tolerance also matter. **Tested angle:** one positive portfolio effect does not rescue an overall mismatch.

### Not-a-Violation Traps

1. **I thought** any losing suitable investment proves bad advice. **Wrong logic:** if the client loses money, the recommendation must have been unsuitable. **Correct logic:** suitability is judged when the recommendation is made, not by hindsight. **Tested angle:** a sound process can still lead to a loss.

2. **I thought** periodic review means constant unnecessary trading. **Wrong logic:** unless I change holdings often, I am not updating suitability. **Correct logic:** review means reassessing the client's facts, not forcing trades. **Tested angle:** updating the investment policy statement does not require automatic turnover.

3. **I thought** one client can hold a concentrated position and still be suitable only in rare legal exceptions. **Wrong logic:** concentration is always unsuitable. **Correct logic:** concentration may be suitable when it matches the client's objectives, wealth, risk capacity, and informed preferences. **Tested angle:** concentration is risky, but not automatically a violation.

4. **I thought** mandate management requires personal knowledge of each pooled investor. **Wrong logic:** a manager of a small-cap fund must learn each unit holder's finances. **Correct logic:** the manager's duty is to run the fund consistently with its stated mandate. **Tested angle:** pooled vehicle suitability works through mandate consistency.

5. **I thought** a client's speculative side account is always unsuitable. **Wrong logic:** any aggressive position violates if the main goal is conservative. **Correct logic:** a limited aggressive sleeve can be suitable if the total portfolio and client constraints support it. **Tested angle:** the full portfolio context may make a seemingly risky holding acceptable.

6. **I thought** the client's sophistication removes the duty to inquire. **Wrong logic:** wealthy or experienced clients do not need fact-finding. **Correct logic:** even sophisticated clients require a reasonable suitability inquiry. **Tested angle:** sophistication may change the answer, but not the need to ask.

7. **I thought** a written mandate lets me ignore new verbal information. **Wrong logic:** until the paper is formally amended, I can disregard a major life change I know about. **Correct logic:** if I know the client's circumstances have materially changed, I must address that mismatch rather than hide behind stale paperwork. **Tested angle:** known facts can make an old mandate unreliable.

## III(D) Performance Presentation

> **Fair. Accurate. Complete. No cherry-picking.**

## Core Concepts

1. Whenever you communicate performance, the standard is simple: ==**fair, accurate, and complete**.==
2. This rule covers both **how performance is measured** and **how performance is shown**.
3. Past performance and expected performance can both be misrepresented.
4. Brief presentations are allowed, but supporting details should be available on request and the summary should not mislead.
5. The Global Investment Performance Standards, often called the **Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS)**, are ==the best practice benchmark for firms presenting managed performance histories.==

### Violation Traps

1. **I thought** one strong account can represent the strategy. **Wrong logic:** showing my best portfolio gives prospects a fair sense of my skill. **Correct logic:** using a cherry-picked account can mislead; similar portfolios should generally be shown as a composite. **Tested angle:** representative account stories are a favorite exam trap.

2. **I thought** terminated accounts can be dropped because they are no longer current. **Wrong logic:** once a client leaves, that history is irrelevant to the strategy. **Correct logic:** ==excluding terminated accounts can distort the performance record.== **Tested angle:** bad historical results do not disappear because the client relationship ended.

3. **I thought** simulated results look fine if the model is smart. **Wrong logic:** back-tested results speak for themselves. **Correct logic:** simulated performance must be clearly identified and not presented like actual live results. **Tested angle:** the exam is strict about model performance masquerading as real history.

4. **I thought** gross return is close enough to net return. **Wrong logic:** fees are small, so no need to state the basis clearly. **Correct logic:** ==I must clearly disclose whether performance is gross of fees, net of fees, or after tax.== **Tested angle:** return labeling is a core tested detail.

5. **I thought** expected performance can be sold with certainty words. **Wrong logic:** if my process is good, saying clients "will earn" the strategy return is acceptable. **Correct logic:** expected outcomes must not be presented as guaranteed future performance. **Tested angle:** certainty language around future returns is dangerous here too.

6. **I thought** a short marketing deck excuses missing context. **Wrong logic:** because the slide has little space, I can leave out the important assumptions. **Correct logic:** a brief presentation still must not mislead and should point to fuller supporting data. **Tested angle:** "limited space" is not a defense for incomplete performance claims.

7. **I thought** prior-firm performance becomes mine automatically. **Wrong logic:** if I was involved in the old track record, I can present it without explanation. **Correct logic:** prior entity performance needs clear disclosure so prospects know whose record it is and under what conditions it was earned. **Tested angle:** inherited track records must be labeled carefully.

### Not-a-Violation Traps

1. **I thought** a short summary always violates. **Wrong logic:** unless every calculation detail appears in the main presentation, III(D) is breached. **Correct logic:** a summary can comply if it is fair, accurate, complete in context, and backed by available detail on request. **Tested angle:** brief does not mean misleading.

2. **I thought** not claiming full Global Investment Performance Standards compliance means automatic violation. **Wrong logic:** if the firm does not claim Global Investment Performance Standards compliance, the presentation is unethical by default. **Correct logic:** compliance with the Global Investment Performance Standards is best practice, but a non-claiming firm can still satisfy III(D) if the presentation is fair, accurate, and complete. **Tested angle:** best practice is not identical to minimum ethical compliance.

3. **I thought** using a composite instead of one account hides information. **Wrong logic:** prospects deserve a single real account, not an aggregate. **Correct logic:** composite performance is usually more fair because it reduces cherry-picking. **Tested angle:** aggregation can improve fairness rather than reduce it.

4. **I thought** past underperformance must be removed from a short brochure to keep it simple. **Wrong logic:** weak years are just distracting detail. **Correct logic:** brevity does not justify omitting material performance history. **Tested angle:** simplification becomes a violation when it changes the true picture.

5. **I thought** analysts are outside this standard if they are not portfolio managers. **Wrong logic:** only discretionary managers need to worry about performance presentation. **Correct logic:** analysts making claims about the success of their recommendations also need fair, accurate, and complete presentation. **Tested angle:** recommendation track records can trigger III(D) too.

6. **I thought** simulated data is banned in every case. **Wrong logic:** any model result automatically violates. **Correct logic:** simulated results may be shown if clearly identified and not mixed up with actual performance. **Tested angle:** disclosure and clarity decide the case.

7. **I thought** a strong footnote cures a misleading headline return. **Wrong logic:** I can put the full truth in tiny print. **Correct logic:** the overall communication must be fair and not create a false first impression. **Tested angle:** the exam judges the whole presentation, not just whether a disclaimer exists somewhere.

## III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality

> **Keep client information private. Three exceptions. Use judgment carefully.**

## Core Concepts

1. ==Client information stays confidential for **current, former, and prospective clients**.==
2. ==There are three classic exceptions: **illegal activity, disclosure required by law, or client permission**.==
3. If law requires disclosure, follow the law. If law forbids disclosure, you may have to stay silent even when the facts feel uncomfortable. That is why legal and compliance advice matters here.
4. Confidentiality applies inside the firm too. ==Information should be shared only with authorized colleagues working on that client's matters.==
5. Modern exam twists include social media, electronic storage, accidental disclosure, vulnerable investors, and cooperation with CFA Institute Professional Conduct investigations.

### Violation Traps

1. **I thought** former clients lose privacy rights. **Wrong logic:** once the relationship ends, their data becomes old business information. **Correct logic:** ==confidentiality survives the end of the relationship.== **Tested angle:** old client records remain protected.

2. **I thought** good intentions justify disclosure. **Wrong logic:** if sharing client information helps a charity, friend, or family member, it is harmless. **Correct logic:** good motives do not override confidentiality. **Tested angle:** helpful intent does not excuse revealing client facts.

3. **I thought** internal sharing is always fine. **Wrong logic:** anyone at my firm may see client data because we work for the same employer. **Correct logic:** I should share confidential information only with authorized colleagues who need it for the client's work. **Tested angle:** same firm does not mean unlimited access.

4. **I thought** suspected illegal activity lets me immediately call outsiders. **Wrong logic:** once I become suspicious, disclosure is automatically required. **Correct logic:** I need to check the applicable law and seek legal or compliance advice before disclosing. **Tested angle:** illegal activity is an exception area, but the law still controls the path.

5. **I thought** family members may override the client. **Wrong logic:** adult children who sound worried can direct me to freeze or discuss the account. **Correct logic:** ==without client permission or legal authority, family concern alone does not erase confidentiality.== **Tested angle:** sympathetic relatives do not automatically become authorized contacts.

6. **I thought** social media mistakes are the client's problem only. **Wrong logic:** if a client posts private facts in a group, I have no duty to respond. **Correct logic:** I should take reasonable steps to protect confidentiality and remove exposed information promptly when possible. **Tested angle:** accidental electronic disclosures test process and response.

7. **I thought** vulnerable client concerns let me tell anyone I trust. **Wrong logic:** if I fear exploitation, I can freely discuss the account with relatives or friends. **Correct logic:** disclosure must stay within what the law and prior client permission allow. **Tested angle:** vulnerability concerns do not create unlimited disclosure rights.

### Not-a-Violation Traps

1. **I thought** every disclosure to authorities violates confidentiality. **Wrong logic:** speaking to regulators is always disloyal. **Correct logic:** if disclosure is required by law, compliance with that law is allowed and may be required. **Tested angle:** legal duty can override silence.

2. **I thought** possible client illegality must always stay secret unless the client agrees. **Wrong logic:** confidentiality always beats suspicion of wrongdoing. **Correct logic:** illegal activity is one of the recognized exceptions, but the exact response depends on the law and professional advice. **Tested angle:** the exception exists, but it is not a free-for-all.

3. **I thought** cooperation with CFA Institute Professional Conduct automatically breaches confidentiality. **Wrong logic:** I can refuse cooperation because client information is private. **Correct logic:** cooperating with a Professional Conduct investigation is not barred by III(E) unless law prevents it. **Tested angle:** CFA investigations are an explicit carveout area.

4. **I thought** asking for a secondary contact is itself a privacy breach. **Wrong logic:** naming a backup contact means I am preparing to violate confidentiality. **Correct logic:** obtaining advance permission for a trusted contact is best practice, especially for potentially vulnerable clients. **Tested angle:** preventive planning supports confidentiality rather than weakens it.

5. **I thought** accidental client self-disclosure automatically means I violated the standard. **Wrong logic:** if the client posts private facts in a group forum, I am automatically at fault. **Correct logic:** if I used reasonable procedures, warned clients properly, and responded promptly, the accidental post may not be my violation. **Tested angle:** reasonable safeguards matter a lot in electronic communication cases.

6. **I thought** prospective clients are not protected because they never signed. **Wrong logic:** until the client relationship is official, their information is fair game. **Correct logic:** prospective client information is also protected. **Tested angle:** the duty starts before the final hiring decision.

7. **I thought** discussing a client with an authorized coworker always needs separate written permission each time. **Wrong logic:** no internal sharing is allowed without fresh client consent. **Correct logic:** sharing with authorized colleagues working on that client's matter is generally allowed. **Tested angle:** necessary internal sharing is different from improper disclosure to outsiders.
