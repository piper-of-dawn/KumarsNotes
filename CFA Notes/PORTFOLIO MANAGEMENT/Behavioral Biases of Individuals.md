
> [!tip] SEE THIS BEFORE EXAM
> - Behavioral finance starts from one brutal fact: people do not invest like calm computers.
> - Cognitive errors usually mean bad reasoning, so the first instinct is **moderate**.
> - Emotional biases usually mean fear, pride, comfort, attachment, or inertia, so the first instinct is **adapt**.
> - Belief perseverance biases: conservatism, confirmation, representativeness, illusion of control, hindsight.
> - Information-processing biases: anchoring and adjustment, mental accounting, framing, availability.
> - Emotional biases here: loss aversion, overconfidence, self-control, status quo, endowment, regret aversion.
> - If the line sounds like "I only like evidence that agrees with me," think confirmation.
> - If the line sounds like "I cannot sell until it gets back to breakeven," think loss aversion.
> - If the line sounds like "I already own it, so it is special," think endowment.
> - If the line sounds like "change is too much effort," think status quo.
> - If the line sounds like "I do not want to feel stupid later," think regret aversion.
> - Market anomalies tied to behavior here include momentum, bubbles, crashes, value-growth mispricing, and home bias.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> - Behavioral biases are systematic patterns, not one-off mistakes.
> - Cognitive errors are usually easier to fix than emotional biases.
> - Belief perseverance means clinging.
> - Information-processing means mishandling.
> - Emotional bias means feeling is driving the decision.
> - Market anomalies can be seen as crowd-level expressions of individual bias.

> [!tip] HOW TO ANSWER BIAS QUESTIONS FAST
> - First ask: bad reasoning or feeling?
> - Then ask: what exact sentence gives the bias away?
> - Then ask: moderate or adapt?
> - Do not classify by outer behavior alone. Classify by the **inner reason**.

1. Behavioral finance studies how people and markets actually behave once real psychology enters the room.
2. What is behavioral finance: the study of decisions distorted by shortcuts, feeling, memory, ego, and social pressure. Why is it used: because traditional finance is cleaner than real life.
3. A behavioral bias is a systematic tendency to think or act in a way that pushes decisions away from rational risk-return logic.
4. What is systematic: repeatable, not accidental once. That is why these biases matter so much in portfolio management.
5. The curriculum cares more about recognizing whether a bias is present than about measuring how strong it is.
6. Financial market participants in this reading include both investors and financial professionals.
7. The first big split is cognitive errors versus emotional biases. What is the difference: cognitive errors come from bad thinking, while emotional biases come from feelings taking the steering wheel. Why is this split important: because the fix depends on the type.
8. Cognitive errors come from faulty reasoning, bad updating, weak information handling, or memory problems. How do you handle them: usually by moderating them with better process, better evidence, and slower thinking.
9. Emotional biases come from impulse, fear, pride, attachment, comfort, temptation, or inertia. How do you handle them: often by adapting the portfolio or decision process around the bias, because emotion usually does not disappear just because you gave a lecture.
10. That split matters because cognitive errors can often be moderated, while emotional biases are often better adapted to. So when the exam gives you a bias, do not just name it. Also ask, "Is this bad reasoning, or is this feeling?"
11. Cognitive errors split again into belief perseverance biases and information-processing biases. What is the difference: belief perseverance means you cling to the old view, while information-processing means you mishandle the data in front of you.
12. Belief perseverance means clinging to an old belief even when new information should move you. Why is it dangerous: because the market keeps moving while your mind stays parked in yesterday’s story.
13. What is cognitive dissonance: the discomfort of new facts fighting old beliefs. Why is it important: many belief-perseverance biases are just attempts to escape that discomfort.
14. Conservatism bias means updating too slowly when new information conflicts with the old story. What is happening: the facts moved, but your belief barely moved. Think about Nokia and BlackBerry when the smartphone shift started. Apple was clearly changing what a phone was, but many investors kept acting like old market share and keyboard loyalty would save the leaders.
15. Why is conservatism bias dangerous: because the world can change fast while your forecast changes like a sleepy turtle. How do you handle it: force yourself to ask, "If I did not already believe the old story, what would this new evidence make me believe now?"
16. Confirmation bias means hunting for agreement and downplaying contradiction. What is happening: you are not looking for truth; you are looking for emotional backup. Imagine a Tesla investor who watches only bullish videos, follows only pro-Tesla accounts, and treats every weak delivery number as temporary noise while never reading the bear case.
17. Why is confirmation bias dangerous: because it makes research look busy while your mind stays closed. How do you handle it: require one serious disconfirming source before any large decision and make yourself explain the other side fairly.
18. Representativeness bias means shallow resemblance gets mistaken for true evidence. What is happening: the story feels familiar, so your brain acts like the conclusion is already proven. A new platform company gets called "the next Amazon" just because revenue is growing fast and the founder sounds visionary, even though the economics, competition, and cash generation look nothing like Amazon.
19. Why is representativeness bias dangerous: because vivid stories can bury base rates and tiny samples can masquerade as proof. WeWork is the right memory hook. Investors heard "technology," saw rapid growth, and started treating it like a great platform business rather than a very exposed office-leasing business.
20. Detection clue for representativeness: one vivid story or tiny sample is doing too much work. Guidance: moderate it by asking, "What is the base rate?" and "Is my sample too small?"
21. Illusion of control bias means thinking you influence outcomes more than you really do. What is happening: familiarity gets mistaken for power. Enron employees who loaded up on Enron stock because they worked there felt safer precisely when they should have been more careful. Knowing the hallways did not mean they controlled the future cash flows.
22. Why is illusion of control dangerous: because confidence rises without real evidence that risk has fallen. How do you handle it: remind yourself that investing is probabilistic and that job proximity, model detail, or effort do not give you command over uncertainty.
23. Hindsight bias means the past suddenly looks obvious because the outcome is already known. What is happening: memory gets rewritten after the fact. After the 2008 financial crisis, many people talked as if the collapse in housing and leverage was completely obvious in advance.
24. Why is hindsight bias dangerous: because fake certainty about the past makes you overconfident about the future. How do you handle it: keep written decision records made at the time, so you can compare what you really knew with what you now claim was obvious.
25. Information-processing errors are less about clinging to old beliefs and more about mishandling the information in front of you right now. Why is this important: now the problem is not stubbornness; the problem is distorted intake or distorted packaging.
26. Anchoring and adjustment bias means starting from an initial number or story and moving too little from it. What is happening: the first number gets too much authority. Suppose you bought Meta at USD 300, watched it fall hard, and now keep saying, "I will think clearly once it gets back to my purchase price."
27. Why is anchoring dangerous: because your purchase price, an old target, or an old high can hijack a fresh valuation decision. How do you handle it: ask whether the conclusion would survive if that anchor had never been seen.
28. Mental accounting bias means splitting money into fake mental boxes even though money is fungible. What is happening: identical money gets treated differently just because it came in through a different door. Salary money gets treated carefully, but a Diwali bonus, an inheritance, or quick profits from an initial public offering suddenly get treated like gambling money.
29. Why is mental accounting dangerous: because portfolio risk is real at the total-wealth level, not at the story level. How do you handle it: put the whole portfolio on one sheet and judge all rupees by the same standard.
30. Framing bias means the same economic choice gets answered differently depending on packaging. What is happening: the wording changes, so the reaction changes, even though the economics did not. Tell a client, "This portfolio has a good long-run expected return," and it may sound attractive. Show the same client, "In a bad year you might lose 18%," and the same portfolio suddenly feels unacceptable.
31. Narrow framing is the small-slice version of framing bias, where one angle is fixated on and the wider picture is missed. Why is it dangerous: because one exciting slice can swallow the full investment case. An investor can fall in love with Apple’s products or Elon Musk’s ambition and stop looking seriously at valuation, position size, and portfolio fit.
32. How do you handle framing problems: restate the question in neutral language, zoom back out to the whole portfolio, and ask whether the answer changes only because the packaging changed.
33. Availability bias means likelihood or importance gets judged by ease of recall, familiarity, or resonance rather than true relevance. What is happening: whatever is loudest in your memory starts pretending to be most important. If the only stocks that come to your mind are the ones discussed all day on business television or social media, your opportunity set is already distorted.
34. Why is availability bias dangerous: because visibility and truth are not the same thing. The artificial-intelligence rush in 2023 and 2024 is the right memory hook. Nvidia, ChatGPT, and every "AI stock" became so visible that many investors started behaving as if the entire market was one giant artificial-intelligence trade.
35. How do you handle availability bias: widen the search process, use broader data, and force yourself to look where the conversation is quieter.
36. Emotional biases are harder because the person may intellectually know the right answer and still emotionally resist it.
37. Loss-aversion bias means losses hurt more than equal gains feel good, which helps explain the disposition effect. What is happening: the pain of admitting a loss becomes stronger than the logic of reallocating capital. Picture two investors. One sells a winner quickly just to lock in pleasure. The other refuses to sell a broken stock because "it is not a real loss until I sell."
38. Why is loss aversion dangerous, and how do you handle it: because emotional pain can replace valuation discipline. The fix is usually adaptation, not preaching. Use written sell rules, deliberate review points, and a fresh fundamental check.
39. Overconfidence bias means unwarranted faith in one’s own ability, forecasts, or edge. What is happening: good outcomes get mistaken for personal genius. A trader who made easy money during the meme-stock and liquidity boom can start thinking he has exceptional stock-picking skill, when part of the truth was simply that almost everything risky was going up.
40. The curriculum splits overconfidence into prediction overconfidence and certainty overconfidence, and it also ties it tightly to self-attribution bias.
41. What is self-attribution bias: taking too much credit for wins and blaming outside forces for losses. Why is it important: it feeds overconfidence. Guidance for overconfidence: partly moderate, partly adapt, with long-run performance review and honest study of losers as well as winners.
42. Self-control bias means choosing short-term comfort over the long-run plan you already know is better, and the curriculum links it to hyperbolic discounting. What is happening: today keeps bullying tomorrow. Think of someone who keeps spending freely on cards in their twenties, postpones investing year after year, and then later tries to "catch up" by taking wild portfolio risk.
43. Why is self-control bias dangerous, and how do you handle it: because the damage compounds quietly for years before it becomes visible. The fix is structural adaptation through written plans, automatic saving, budgets, and systems that do not rely on daily willpower.
44. Status quo bias means sticking with the old setup largely because change feels effortful; endowment bias means overvaluing an asset because you own it; regret-aversion bias means avoiding action because you fear feeling foolish later. What is happening: three different emotions can all produce inaction. A default retirement-plan allocation can sit untouched for years because changing it feels like work. An inherited Reliance position can feel special just because it came from family. A nervous investor can delay buying after a market fall because he is scared of looking stupid if prices fall further next week.
45. Why is this distinction important, and how do you handle it: because the outer behavior looks the same, but the inner motive tells you the real bias. Inertia means status quo, ownership attachment means endowment, and future embarrassment means regret aversion. All three are usually handled better by adaptation through process and reframing.
46. The final learning outcome moves from personal mistakes to market anomalies, which are persistent patterns that simple efficiency stories struggle to explain.
47. Momentum can be partly explained by availability or recency, hindsight, and regret-driven or loss-aversion-driven chasing of recent winners. Bubbles and crashes are the bigger crowd version of the same movie. The dot-com bubble and the housing bubble both had stories people badly wanted to believe, and once enough people were inside that story, overconfidence and confirmation started feeding each other.
48. Value-growth mispricing can be linked to representativeness, halo effect, and overconfidence. A glamorous company with a loved product can get priced as if good business automatically means good stock, while a dull bank or industrial company can get ignored. Home bias is the geographic version of comfort-seeking: many Indian investors massively overweight India, and many American investors massively overweight the United States, because familiar markets feel safer than they really are.
49. The final exam reflex is simple: identify the bias from the revealing sentence, decide whether it is cognitive or emotional, and then decide whether the right response is to moderate it or adapt to it.
