### THE BEHAVIORAL BIASES OF INDIVIDUALS
> Source module: `/home/karma/CFAPractice/mcq/quiz/AI/PDF/portfolio management/module_05_the_behavioral_biases_of_individuals.txt`

###### LOS 5.a: Compare and contrast cognitive errors and emotional biases.
###### LOS 5.b: Discuss commonly recognized behavioral biases and their implications for financial decision making.
###### LOS 5.c: Describe how behavioral biases of investors can lead to market characteristics that may not be explained by traditional finance.

> [!tip] SEE THIS BEFORE EXAM
> - Behavioral finance starts from one brutal fact: people do not invest like calm computers.
> - Cognitive errors usually mean bad reasoning, so the first instinct is **moderate**.
> - Emotional biases usually mean fear, pride, comfort, attachment, or inertia, so the first instinct is **adapt**.
> - Belief perseverance biases: conservatism, confirmation, representativeness, illusion of control, hindsight.
> - Information-processing biases: anchoring and adjustment, mental accounting, framing, availability.
> - Emotional biases here: loss aversion, overconfidence, self-control, status quo, endowment, regret aversion.
> - If the line sounds like "I only like evidence that agrees with me," think confirmation.
> - If the line sounds like "I cannot sell until it gets back to breakeven," think loss aversion.
> - If the line sounds like "I already own it, so it is special," think endowment.
> - If the line sounds like "change is too much effort," think status quo.
> - If the line sounds like "I do not want to feel stupid later," think regret aversion.
> - Market anomalies tied to behavior here include momentum, bubbles, crashes, value-growth mispricing, and home bias.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> - Behavioral biases are systematic patterns, not one-off mistakes.
> - Cognitive errors are usually easier to fix than emotional biases.
> - Belief perseverance means clinging.
> - Information-processing means mishandling.
> - Emotional bias means feeling is driving the decision.
> - Market anomalies can be seen as crowd-level expressions of individual bias.

> [!tip] HOW TO ANSWER BIAS QUESTIONS FAST
> - First ask: bad reasoning or feeling?
> - Then ask: what exact sentence gives the bias away?
> - Then ask: moderate or adapt?
> - Do not classify by outer behavior alone. Classify by the **inner reason**.

1. Behavioral finance studies how people and markets actually behave once real psychology enters the room.
2. What is behavioral finance: the study of decisions distorted by shortcuts, feeling, memory, ego, and social pressure. Why is it used: because traditional finance is cleaner than real life.
3. A behavioral bias is a systematic tendency to think or act in a way that pushes decisions away from rational risk-return logic.
4. What is systematic: repeatable, not accidental once. That is why these biases matter so much in portfolio management.
5. The curriculum cares more about recognizing whether a bias is present than about measuring how strong it is.
6. Financial market participants in this reading include both investors and financial professionals.
7. The first big split is cognitive errors versus emotional biases.
8. Cognitive errors come from faulty reasoning, bad updating, weak information handling, or memory problems.
9. Emotional biases come from impulse, fear, pride, attachment, comfort, temptation, or inertia.
10. That split matters because cognitive errors can often be moderated, while emotional biases are often better adapted to.
11. Cognitive errors split again into belief perseverance biases and information-processing biases.
12. Belief perseverance means clinging to an old belief even when new information should move you.
13. What is cognitive dissonance: the discomfort of new facts fighting old beliefs. Why is it important: many belief-perseverance biases are just attempts to escape that discomfort.
14. Conservatism bias means updating too slowly when new information conflicts with the old story.
15. Detection clue for conservatism: the forecast barely changes even after meaningful new evidence arrives. Guidance: moderate it by forcing the question, "How does this new information change my view?"
16. Confirmation bias means hunting for agreement and downplaying contradiction.
17. Detection clue for confirmation: the investor keeps returning to supportive sources and avoids the contrary case. Guidance: moderate it by requiring disconfirming evidence before large decisions.
18. Representativeness bias means shallow resemblance gets mistaken for true evidence.
19. The two big forms the curriculum emphasizes are base-rate neglect and sample-size neglect.
20. Detection clue for representativeness: one vivid story or tiny sample is doing too much work. Guidance: moderate it by asking, "What is the base rate?" and "Is my sample too small?"
21. Illusion of control bias means thinking you influence outcomes more than you really do.
22. Detection clue for illusion of control: the person talks as if hard work, job proximity, or model detail gives actual control over uncertainty. Guidance: moderate it by remembering investing is probabilistic and by seeking opposing views.
23. Hindsight bias means the past suddenly looks obvious because the outcome is already known.
24. Detection clue for hindsight: "Anyone could see that coming." Guidance: moderate it with written decision records made at the time, not reconstructed memory afterward.
25. Information-processing errors are less about clinging to old beliefs and more about mishandling the information in front of you right now.
26. Anchoring and adjustment bias means starting from an initial number or story and moving too little from it.
27. Detection clue for anchoring: the person keeps circling back to purchase price, past earnings, old highs, or old targets. Guidance: moderate it by asking whether the conclusion would survive if the old anchor had never been seen.
28. Mental accounting bias means splitting money into fake mental boxes even though money is fungible.
29. Detection clue for mental accounting: salary money, bonus money, gains, inheritance, and dividends are treated like different species of wealth. Guidance: moderate it by viewing the whole portfolio on one sheet.
30. Framing bias means the same economic choice gets answered differently depending on packaging.
31. Narrow framing is the small-slice version of framing bias, where one angle is fixated on and the wider picture is missed.
32. Detection clue for framing: wording changed, economics did not, but the answer changed anyway. Guidance: moderate it by restating the problem in neutral language and focusing on future prospects.
33. Availability bias means likelihood or importance gets judged by ease of recall, familiarity, or resonance rather than true relevance.
34. The curriculum highlights retrievability, categorization, narrow range of experience, and resonance as the practical sources.
35. Detection clue for availability: the opportunity set got narrowed because something felt familiar, visible, recent, or personally resonant. Guidance: moderate it by widening the search process and using broader data.
36. Emotional biases are harder because the person may intellectually know the right answer and still emotionally resist it.
37. Loss-aversion bias means losses hurt more than equal gains please, which helps explain the disposition effect.
38. Detection clue for loss aversion: "I will sell when it gets back to breakeven." Guidance: adapt with written sell disciplines and fundamental review, because you cannot make losses stop hurting.
39. Overconfidence bias means unwarranted faith in one’s own ability, forecasts, or edge.
40. The curriculum splits overconfidence into prediction overconfidence and certainty overconfidence, and it also ties it tightly to self-attribution bias.
41. What is self-attribution bias: taking too much credit for wins and blaming outside forces for losses. Why is it important: it feeds overconfidence. Guidance for overconfidence: partly moderate, partly adapt, with long-run performance review and honest study of losers as well as winners.
42. Self-control bias means choosing short-term comfort over the long-run plan you already know is better, and the curriculum links it to hyperbolic discounting.
43. Guidance for self-control bias is adaptation through structure: written plans, automatic saving, budgets, and systems that do not rely on daily willpower.
44. Status quo bias means sticking with the old setup largely because change feels effortful; endowment bias means overvaluing an asset because you own it; regret-aversion bias means avoiding action because you fear feeling foolish later.
45. The exam separates these three look-alikes by inner motive: inertia means status quo, ownership attachment means endowment, future embarrassment means regret aversion. All three usually call for adaptation through process and reframing.
46. The final learning outcome moves from personal mistakes to market anomalies, which are persistent patterns that simple efficiency stories struggle to explain.
47. Momentum can be partly explained by availability or recency, hindsight, and regret-driven or loss-aversion-driven chasing of recent winners.
48. Bubbles and crashes often involve overconfidence, overtrading, confirmation, self-attribution, regret, anchoring, and cognitive dissonance; value-growth mispricing can be linked to representativeness, halo effect, and overconfidence; home bias means preferring domestic or nearby investments because familiarity feels safer than it really is.
49. The final exam reflex is simple: identify the bias from the revealing sentence, decide whether it is cognitive or emotional, and then decide whether the right response is to moderate it or adapt to it.
