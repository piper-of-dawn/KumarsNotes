
> [!tip] LOOK AT THESE BEFORE EXAM
> - Risk is exposure to uncertainty.
> - Risk management is not minimizing risk. It is aligning actual risk with acceptable risk.
> - Governance is the top-down layer.
> - Risk tolerance asks, "How much pain is acceptable?"
> - Risk budgeting asks, "Where will that pain budget be spent?"
> - Financial risks: market, credit, liquidity.
> - Non-financial risks: settlement, legal, regulatory, accounting, tax, model, tail, operational, solvency, health, mortality, longevity, property and casualty.
> - Value at risk is a **minimum tail loss**, not a maximum loss.
> - Conditional value at risk is the average loss beyond the value-at-risk point.
> - Scenario analysis and stress testing exist because models can miss ugly reality.
> - Risk can be modified by avoidance, acceptance or self-insurance, transfer, and shifting.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> - Risk management = define desired risk, measure actual risk, and adjust actual risk toward desired risk.
> - Good risk management reduces surprise and improves decisions.
> - Framework pieces: governance, identification and measurement, infrastructure, policies and processes, monitoring and mitigation, communication, and strategic analysis.
> - Risk tolerance comes before risk budgeting.
> - Risks interact, especially in stress.

> [!tip] HOW TO ANSWER THIS MODULE FAST
> - If it is top-down, think governance.
> - If it is pain boundary, think tolerance.
> - If it is allocation by risk, think budgeting.
> - If it is tail loss, remember value at risk is a threshold, not a cap.
> - If it is method choice, weigh costs and benefits against risk tolerance.

1. Risk management matters because business and investing are really about allocating capital to chosen risks, not directly choosing returns.
2. What is risk: exposure to uncertainty. Why does it matter: outcomes are seldom known with complete confidence.
3. Risk exposure is the extent to which a person or organization is vulnerable to a risk driver through the assets and liabilities it chose to hold.
4. The curriculum likes three related labels here: risk driver, risk position, and risk exposure.
5. What is a risk driver: the underlying uncertain factor, such as an exchange-rate move or rate shock. What is a risk position: the amount of the risky thing held. What is risk exposure: the effect on value if the driver moves.
6. Risk management is the process by which an organization or individual defines the level of risk to be taken, measures the risk being taken, and adjusts the second toward the first.
7. Why is risk management used: to maximize enterprise value, portfolio value, or personal utility while staying inside a tolerable risk zone.
8. Risk management is not about avoiding all risk. Return without risk is usually fantasy, not finance.
9. The "doctrine of no surprises" does not mean prediction. It means management should already know how badly a plausible shock could hurt before the shock arrives.
10. Good risk management does not guarantee no loss after the event; it means most of the real work was done before the event.
11. Because exposures and environments keep changing, risk management is continuous, not a one-time memo or spreadsheet.
12. A risk management framework is the infrastructure, process, and analytics needed to support effective risk management.
13. The framework should be custom to the enterprise rather than one-size-fits-all.
14. The seven core pieces are risk governance, risk identification and measurement, risk infrastructure, policies and processes, risk monitoring and mitigation, communication, and strategic analysis.
15. Governance is the top-level system of structures, rights, and obligations by which the organization is directed and controlled.
16. Risk governance is the top-down guidance that aligns risk activity with the goals of the whole enterprise.
17. Risk identification and measurement asks what can hurt the organization and how large that hurt can get.
18. Risk infrastructure includes data capture, models, systems, scenario engines, reporting capability, and people who can run them.
19. Policies and processes translate governance into daily behavior through limits, escalation rules, due diligence, controls, and checklists.
20. Monitoring, mitigation, and management form the active loop: measure risk, check alignment with tolerance, and act if out of line.
21. Communication matters because risk information is useless if it never reaches decision makers.
22. Strategic analysis is the offensive side of risk management because it helps management decide which risky activities are truly adding value.
23. The enterprise loop is simple in words: goals -> tolerance and budgeting -> strategies and risky activities -> risk measurement -> monitor -> modify if out of line -> report -> feed back into better decisions.
24. What is risk culture: the habit of making risk part of every important decision rather than treating it like compliance theater. Why is it important: better risk culture usually means better decisions.
25. The same framework applies to individuals, just with less bureaucracy and usually more informal execution.
26. For individuals the practical sequence is: define goals, choose investments and identify risks, evaluate exposure, modify risk if needed, implement, and review.
27. Good risk management also brings side benefits the curriculum likes: fewer surprises, better decision discipline, quicker response, fewer operational mistakes, better trust between board and management, and a better outside reputation.
28. Risk governance is the foundation because it sets the context for everything else.
29. Effective governance means the governing body sets goals, priorities, risk appetite, oversight, and clear authority.
30. Enterprise risk management means taking a whole-enterprise view, not optimizing one attractive corner in isolation.
31. The pension example exists to hammer this: looking only at pension assets while ignoring bond-like liabilities can create fake safety and hurt the whole enterprise.
32. The curriculum also explicitly likes three governance hallmarks: visible commitment from the top, a risk management committee, and a chief risk officer (CRO).
33. A CRO is not just a policeman. The role should help build and run the framework and participate in strategic decisions.
34. Risk tolerance is the extent to which the organization is willing to experience losses, opportunity costs, and failure to meet objectives.
35. The risk tolerance decision mixes an inside view and an outside view.
36. The inside view asks what internal shortfalls would seriously hurt or break the organization. The outside view asks which external risk drivers can hit it.
37. Risk tolerance should be decided before a crisis, not invented during a crisis.
38. Risk budgeting comes after risk tolerance and translates appetite into actual allocation by risk metrics or characteristics.
39. Risk budgeting may use standard deviation, beta, value at risk, scenario loss, risk classes, or factor exposures rather than only asset labels.
40. One major benefit of risk budgeting is discipline: every new risk taken must justify the budget it consumes.
41. The three primary financial risks are market risk, credit risk, and liquidity risk.
42. Important non-financial risks include settlement, legal, regulatory, accounting, tax, model, tail, operational, and solvency risk, while individuals also face health, mortality, longevity, and property risks.
43. Risks interact, and this interaction often makes the total damage worse than a simple sum.
44. Wrong-way risk is the clean example: the same move that increases your exposure also weakens the counterparty that owes you.
45. Risk measurement uses different languages for different asset classes, including standard deviation, beta, duration, the Greeks, value at risk, conditional value at risk, and expected loss given default.
46. Value at risk contains a currency amount, a horizon, and a probability, and it tells you a minimum tail loss threshold rather than the maximum loss.
47. Conditional value at risk goes one step deeper by averaging the losses beyond the value-at-risk point, while scenario analysis and stress testing ask what happens under ugly but plausible or deliberately severe conditions.
48. Risk can be modified by prevention or avoidance, acceptance and self-insurance, transfer through insurance, or shifting through derivatives, and the right choice depends on cost, benefit, residual risk, and consistency with risk tolerance.
49. The final exam reflex is simple: identify whether the question is about governance, tolerance, budgeting, risk type, risk metric, or risk-modification method, and then answer from that bucket instead of treating the whole reading like one giant blur.
