#### 1. Types of equity securities

1. Equity security means a claim on the residual value of the business after liabilities have been paid.  
   What is residual value: whatever is left after creditors have been satisfied.  
   Why is equity used: it gives the company permanent capital without a maturity date.

2. Common shares represent basic ownership. You get the most upside if the company does well, but you also stand last in line if the company is liquidated.

3. Common shareholders usually have voting rights, the right to receive dividends if declared, and the right to share in the company’s growth through price appreciation.

4. The dividend point is the first trap in this module: a common dividend is ==not a contractual obligation==. If the board does not declare it, there is no legal breach in the way there would be with missed bond interest.

> [!info] WHY COMMON EQUITY FEELS RISKY
> If you buy Apple common stock, you are not lending Apple money the way a bondholder does. You are taking the messy owner seat. If business explodes upward, you win big. If business struggles, everyone else gets paid before you. That is why common equity carries the highest upside and usually the highest risk inside the capital structure.

#### 2. Voting rights

5. Statutory voting gives one vote per share for each director seat. You cannot combine all your votes on one favorite candidate.

6. Cumulative voting lets you multiply your shares by the number of director seats and then allocate those votes however you want.

7. Suppose you own 30 shares and 9 directors are being elected. Under statutory voting, you cast 30 votes for each seat separately. Under cumulative voting, you get \(30 \times 9 = 270\) votes total and can dump all 270 on one candidate if you want.

8. That is why cumulative voting helps minority shareholders more. It gives them a realistic chance to push one preferred candidate through.

9. Proxy voting means a shareholder authorizes someone else to vote on the shareholder’s behalf. Proxy voting is about who casts the vote. Statutory versus cumulative is about how votes are allocated.

> [!question] STATUTORY VERSUS CUMULATIVE
>
> Problem: You own 100 shares. Five directors are being elected. How many votes can you place on one candidate under cumulative voting?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> 100 \times 5 = 500
> $$
>
> Explanation: Under cumulative voting you can concentrate all 500 votes on one candidate. Under statutory voting you cannot do that.

#### 3. Preference shares

10. Preference shares sit between debt and common equity.  
    What is preference share: an equity security with priority over common shares for dividends and liquidation proceeds.  
    Why does it feel debt-like: dividends are often fixed.  
    Why does it still remain equity: dividends are usually not contractual obligations and the shares can be perpetual.

11. Preference shareholders usually rank above common shareholders for dividends and liquidation, but below all creditors and bondholders.

12. Preference shares generally do not have voting rights unless the issuance terms explicitly say otherwise. So if you see a question trying to link preference shares to normal corporate voting, be suspicious immediately.

13. Participating preference shares receive the fixed preferred dividend and may also receive extra dividends if profits exceed a pre-set level. Some issues also allow extra distribution on liquidation above par value.

14. Non-participating preference shares get the fixed preferred dividend only. No extra profit-sharing, no extra participation in upside beyond the contract.

15. Convertible preference shares can be converted into a stated number of common shares. This gives the investor some upside if the common stock price rises strongly.

16. Convertible preference shares are usually less volatile than the underlying common shares because their dividend stream is more stable, but they still allow some upside through conversion.

17. Cumulative preference shares store missed dividends in arrears. If the board skips today, the unpaid amount stays alive and must be cleared before common shareholders receive dividends.

18. Non-cumulative preference shares do not store skipped dividends. If the board skips the dividend for that period, that cash is gone forever from the investor’s point of view.

19. Callable preference shares let the issuer redeem the shares at a pre-set price. That helps the issuer, not the investor, so callability raises investor risk.

20. Putable or retractable preference shares let the investor sell the shares back to the issuer at a pre-set price. That creates a floor under value and lowers investor risk.

21. From the investor’s point of view, the rough safety ladder is usually: putable is safer than non-callable, and non-callable is safer than callable. Cumulative is safer than non-cumulative.

> [!danger] DO NOT MIX THESE UP
> 1. Participating means extra dividends or extra liquidation participation. It does ==not== mean voting rights.
> 2. Convertible means conversion into common shares. It does ==not== mean extra dividends tied to profits.
> 3. Fixed preferred dividends are usually fixed in amount, but they are ==not== fixed contractual obligations like bond coupons.

> [!question] WHICH PREFERRED IS RISKIEST?
>
> Problem: All else equal, which is riskier for the investor: callable with non-cumulative dividends, non-callable with cumulative dividends, or putable with non-cumulative dividends?
>
> ---
>
> Solution: Callable with non-cumulative dividends is the riskiest.
>
> Explanation: The issuer has the favorable option to redeem, and skipped dividends do not accumulate. That gives the investor the least certainty of future cash flows.

#### 4. Private equity versus public equity

22. Public equity trades on public exchanges and usually has quoted market prices, stronger disclosure requirements, and a more active secondary market.

23. Private equity is issued outside the public exchange system, often through private placements to institutional investors.

24. Private equity is usually illiquid, hard to trade, and harder to value because there is no constantly updated exchange quote.

25. That means private equity investors often need negotiation, patience, and long holding periods. You do not click a button and exit the way you can with a liquid public stock.

26. Private firms usually spend less on exchange listing requirements, regulatory filings, and investor relations than public firms. That is one real advantage of staying private or going private.

27. A management buyout is often most attractive when the company has strong cash flows and undervalued assets. The cash helps service debt, and the undervalued assets may provide room for value realization later.

> [!example]
> Think of a boring manufacturing company that throws off cash every year but the market hates it because growth looks dull. That can be interesting for a buyout group. The steady cash helps pay debt, and unloved assets may be worth more than the market price suggests.

#### 5. Foreign equities and depository receipts

28. Investors can buy foreign equity directly in the foreign market or indirectly through depository receipts.

29. A depository receipt is a local-market security representing an economic interest in foreign shares held by a depository bank.  
    What is depository bank: the financial institution that holds the underlying foreign shares and issues the receipt.  
    Why is depository receipt used: it lets investors trade foreign exposure in a more familiar market and currency.

30. Direct foreign investing exposes you directly to foreign market rules, settlement systems, and currency movements.

31. A depository receipt often makes access easier, but it does not eliminate economic exposure to the foreign business or to exchange-rate movements.

32. A sponsored depository receipt is issued with the foreign company’s involvement. Investors usually get rights similar to direct common shareholders, including voting rights.

33. An unsponsored depository receipt is issued without the foreign company’s direct involvement. In that case, the depository bank usually keeps the voting rights.

34. This is one of the easiest exam traps in the reading, so hammer it into your head: sponsored usually means investor voting rights; unsponsored usually means bank voting rights.

35. An American depository receipt (ADR) trades in the United States. A global depository receipt (GDR) is usually issued outside both the issuer’s home market and the United States, often in large international financial centers.

36. Level I American depository receipts trade over the counter and have the lightest reporting burden. They cannot be used to raise capital in the United States.

37. Level II American depository receipts are exchange listed and have heavier reporting requirements than Level I. They still cannot be used to raise capital in the United States.

38. Level III American depository receipts are exchange listed and can be used to raise equity capital in the United States. They are the most expensive and disclosure-heavy of the three main public levels.

39. Rule 144A receipts are privately placed to qualified institutional buyers. They can help raise capital privately in the United States without becoming a normal public exchange listing.

40. Depository receipt returns are affected by the underlying company, the home market, the exchange rate, and market sentiment. The number of receipts itself is not the main driver of return in the way questions try to make it sound.

> [!warning] DEPOSITORY RECEIPT TRAPS
> 1. Unsponsored does ==not== give the investor the same voting rights as direct common share ownership.
> 2. Sponsored versus unsponsored changes governance rights and reporting involvement, not the basic fact that both can trade like shares.
> 3. Exchange-rate movement still matters. If the euro weakens against the US dollar, a United States investor in a euro-linked equity position gets hurt on translation.

#### 6. Equity return and equity risk

41. Equity return comes from two places: cash income such as dividends and price change.

42. The holding period return formula is:

$$
R = \frac{P_1 - P_0 + D_1}{P_0}
$$

43. If there is no dividend, return is just price change over purchase price. If there is a dividend, add it to the numerator.

44. For foreign investing, investor return also depends on currency movement. The local asset can do fine while the investor still loses after translation back into the home currency.

45. Equity risk is tied to uncertainty about future cash flows and future price. It is not measured by the standard deviation of dividends because many equities pay no dividend at all.

46. Common equity is usually riskier than preference shares because common holders are lower in priority and more dependent on uncertain future price appreciation.

47. Putable preference shares are among the least risky forms listed in most basic exam questions because the investor can put the shares back at a pre-set price.

> [!question] QUICK RETURN NUMERICAL
>
> Problem: You buy a share for USD 50, receive a USD 2 dividend, and sell it for USD 42. What is the holding period return?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> R = \frac{42 - 50 + 2}{50} = \frac{-6}{50} = -12\%
> $$
>
> Explanation: The price loss is USD 8, but the dividend softens the damage by USD 2.

#### 7. Why companies issue equity

48. Companies issue equity mainly to raise long-term capital, fund growth, finance acquisitions, support working capital, or strengthen the balance sheet.

49. Increasing liquidity can also be a motive, especially when a firm wants a broader investor base and easier trading in its shares.

50. Raising equity is not normally described as a direct way to increase return on equity. In fact, if profits do not rise proportionally, new equity tends to push return on equity down because equity, which is the denominator, gets larger.

51. “To ensure the company continues as a going concern” is not the standard normal reason taught for equity issuance. It is more like a distress situation, not the base-case motive.

#### 8. Book value, market value, and return on equity

52. Book value of equity is the accounting value of owners’ claim:

$$
\text{Book value of equity} = \text{Total assets} - \text{Total liabilities}
$$

53. Retained earnings push book value up because retained earnings stay inside equity instead of being paid out.

54. Market value of equity is share price times number of shares outstanding. It reflects investor expectations, not just accounting history.

55. Intrinsic value is the analyst’s estimate of what the equity should be worth based on future cash flows and risk. It is not directly observable on a screen.

56. Management can directly affect book value through operating decisions that drive earnings and retained earnings. Management affects market value only indirectly because market value depends on investor expectations too.

57. Return on equity shows how much net income the company generates relative to the shareholders’ book equity base.

58. The clean formula is:

$$
\text{Return on equity} = \frac{\text{Net income available to common shareholders}}{\text{Beginning or average book value of equity}}
$$

59. Cost of equity is the return investors require to hold the company’s equity. From the company’s point of view, it acts like the minimum return the company must offer to keep equity investors willing to stay.

60. Cost of equity is usually harder to estimate than cost of debt because there is no neat contractual rate sitting in the bond indenture. It depends on risk and investor expectations.

> [!question] BOOK VALUE VERSUS MARKET VALUE
>
> Problem: A company has total assets of EUR 12,000,000 and total liabilities of EUR 7,500,000. It has 100,000 shares outstanding and the market price per share is EUR 52. What is book value of equity?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Book value of equity} = 12,000,000 - 7,500,000 = 4,500,000
> $$
>
> Explanation: Do not touch the EUR 52 share price here. That price is for market value, not book value.

> [!question] RETURN ON EQUITY
>
> Problem: Beginning total assets are GBP 50,000,000, beginning total liabilities are GBP 35,000,000, and net income is GBP 2,000,000. What is return on equity?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Beginning book value of equity} = 50,000,000 - 35,000,000 = 15,000,000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Return on equity} = \frac{2,000,000}{15,000,000} = 13.3\%
> $$
>
> Explanation: The question is testing whether you know the denominator is book value of equity, not market capitalization.

#### 9. DO NOT MAKE THIS MISTAKE

> [!danger] DO NOT MAKE THIS MISTAKE
> 1. Do not say common equity requires periodic dividends. ==It does not.== Common dividends are discretionary.
> 2. Do not say participating preference shares help with voting. ==They do not.== Participating means extra dividends or extra liquidation sharing.
> 3. Do not say preference shares must be callable or putable. Some are, some are not.
> 4. Do not say convertible preference shares give extra dividends when profits rise. That is participating preferred, not convertible preferred.
> 5. Do not say private equity has quoted exchange prices. ==It usually does not.==
> 6. Do not forget that private firms usually have lower regulatory and investor-relations costs than public firms.
> 7. Do not assume emerging-market access means foreign exchange markets became stable. That is not the takeaway.
> 8. Do not give voting rights on unsponsored depository receipts to the investor. The depository bank usually keeps them.
> 9. Do not forget Level III American depository receipts can raise capital, while Level I and Level II cannot.
> 10. Do not measure equity risk using dividend volatility alone. Some equities pay no dividends.
> 11. Do not say issuing equity increases return on equity. Usually it pushes return on equity down unless earnings rise enough too.
> 12. Do not confuse book value with market value. Book value comes from assets minus liabilities. Market value comes from share price times shares outstanding.
> 13. Do not forget management buyout candidates usually need healthy cash flow and undervalued assets, not weak cash flow.
> 14. Do not call preferred dividends contractual obligations. Fixed does not mean legally mandatory like bond interest.
> 15. Do not say the number of depository receipts is the main return driver. Return comes from the underlying shares and exchange-rate effects.

> [!tip] QUICK CHECKS
> 1. If the question asks about voting power for small shareholders, your mind should jump to cumulative voting.
> 2. If the question asks which preferred is safer, ask who owns the option: investor option is safer for the investor; issuer option is worse for the investor.
> 3. If the question gives assets and liabilities, think book value first.
> 4. If the question gives share price and shares outstanding, think market value first.
> 5. If the question mentions a United States investor in a foreign asset, always ask what the currency did.
