> [!tip] SEE THIS BEFORE EXAM
> - Start with the biggest divide: **covered bonds keep the loans on the issuer's balance sheet**, while a true securitization removes the asset pool and puts it into a separate entity.
> - Covered bonds have **dual recourse**. If the issuer fails, investors first go after the ringfenced cover pool and then the issuer's unencumbered assets.
> - Most other ABS have **single recourse**. Your claim is basically on the collateral pool, not on the originator's whole balance sheet.
> - Covered bonds usually have **one bond class**. ABS and CDOs usually have **multiple tranches**.
> - Redemption-regime trap:
>   - **Hard-bullet**: miss the scheduled payment, and default is triggered immediately.
>   - **Soft-bullet**: maturity gets pushed out first; default comes only if payment still fails by the extended date.
>   - **Conditional pass-through**: after original maturity, unpaid amounts get passed through as cover-pool cash is collected.
> - Internal credit enhancement memory list: **overcollateralization, excess spread, subordination**.
> - **Overcollateralization** means collateral value is bigger than bond value. Cushion first, pain later.
> - **Excess spread** means the collateral earns more than the issued securities pay. That extra spread can absorb shortfalls or build reserves.
> - **Subordination** means junior tranches take losses first, so senior tranches get protected.
> - Waterfall trap: **senior gets paid first and loses last; equity/junior gets paid last and loses first.**
> - Rating trap: a securitization tranche can have a **better credit rating than the originator** because the rating reflects both collateral quality and structural protection.
> - Amortizing-vs-non-amortizing trap:
>   - **Auto loans** are amortizing, so principal comes back from the start.
>   - **Credit card receivables** are non-amortizing, so during the revolving period principal is reinvested, not returned.
> - Credit card ABS cash-flow trap: during the **revolving or lockout period**, investors mainly get **finance charges and fees**. Principal comes back only in the later **amortization period**.
> - If a credit card ABS hits **early amortization** or **rapid amortization**, that means principal starts coming back sooner because the structure is trying to protect investors.
> - Solar ABS trap: many deals have a **pre-funding period**, which lets the trust add more qualifying assets after closing.
> - CDO family memory:
>   - **CBO** = backed by bonds
>   - **CLO** = backed by leveraged bank loans
>   - **Structured finance CDO** = backed by other CDOs
>   - **Synthetic CDO/CLO** = created through credit derivatives
> - The prevailing CDO structure is the **CLO**.
> - CLO trap: unlike a plain ABS, the collateral pool is usually **actively managed**. That means you are taking not just collateral risk but also **collateral-manager skill risk**.
> - CLO type trap:
>   - **Cash flow CLO** is the most common.
>   - **Market value CLO** depends on portfolio market value.
>   - **Synthetic CLO** uses credit derivatives.
> - Coverage-test trap: if a CLO fails an overcollateralization or similar test, cash gets diverted away from junior and equity holders toward senior debt. That is the structure **deleveraging itself**.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE THIS
> Core differences:
> - Covered bonds: **on-balance-sheet, dual recourse, one bond class, dynamic cover pool**
> - Non-mortgage ABS: **off-balance-sheet collateral, single recourse, usually multiple tranches**
> - CDOs and CLOs: **off-balance-sheet, multiple tranches, actively managed collateral pool**
>
> Internal credit enhancements:
> - **Overcollateralization**
> - **Excess spread**
> - **Subordination / credit tranching**
>
> Covered-bond redemption regimes:
> - **Hard-bullet** = immediate default if scheduled payment is missed
> - **Soft-bullet** = maturity extension first
> - **Conditional pass-through** = converts into pass-through after original maturity
>
> Non-mortgage ABS:
> - **Credit card ABS** = non-amortizing collateral, revolving period, early amortization risk
> - **Auto loan ABS** = amortizing collateral, principal returned from the start
> - **Solar ABS** = amortizing collateral, green financing angle, may include pre-funding period
>
> CDO and CLO:
> - **Prevailing CDO form = CLO**
> - **Cash flow CLO** = most common
> - **Market value CLO** = tranche value depends on market value
> - **Synthetic CLO** = uses credit derivatives

1. Asset-backed securities are built on one simple idea: take cash flows from a pool of assets and rearrange them into different claims for different investors.

2. That rearranging matters because not every investor wants the same mix of risk, maturity, and cash-flow timing.

3. The curriculum keeps coming back to the same big benefit of securitization: it transfers risk, gives issuers and investors more flexibility, and can make capital allocation more efficient.

4. But the exam will not reward vague admiration for securitization. It will ask who owns the collateral, who has recourse, who gets paid first, and who takes the first loss.

#### Covered bonds

5. A covered bond is a senior debt obligation issued by a financial institution and backed by a segregated pool of assets, usually residential mortgages, commercial mortgages, or public sector assets.

6. The key difference starts immediately: in a covered bond, the loans stay on the issuer's balance sheet.

7. So if a bank issues covered bonds backed by mortgages, those mortgages are still the bank's assets, not assets sold away into a bankruptcy-remote trust.

8. The bank ringfences certain eligible assets into a cover pool, and that pool supports the covered bonds.

9. Investors in covered bonds have dual recourse if the issuer gets into trouble.

10. Dual recourse means they first have a claim on the ringfenced cover pool and then, if needed, a claim on the issuer's unencumbered assets.

11. That dual recourse feature is a huge reason covered bonds usually carry lower credit risk than otherwise similar ABS.

12. Covered bonds usually have one bond class per cover pool, not a stack of senior, mezzanine, and equity tranches.

13. That alone should stop you from confusing covered bonds with a typical securitization waterfall.

14. Another important feature is that the cover pool is dynamic.

15. Dynamic here means the pool is monitored over time, and the issuer must replace prepaid or non-performing assets so the pool keeps generating enough cash flows.

16. A third party also monitors whether the cover pool keeps meeting performance and underwriting standards.

17. Covered bond investors are protected not just by structure, but also by strict eligibility rules for what can go into the pool.

18. A mortgage often has to satisfy a loan-to-value cut-off to stay eligible for inclusion.

19. If a mortgage stops meeting the eligibility standards, it may have to be replaced with another qualifying mortgage.

20. Covered bond transactions also commonly use overcollateralization.

21. Overcollateralization means the collateral value exceeds the face value of the covered bonds, so there is extra cushion before investor payments are threatened.

22. Germany, for example, requires a minimum amount of overcollateralization for covered loan collateral pools, and some issuers voluntarily go above the minimum.

23. All of this is why covered bonds have stayed a relatively stable funding tool over time.

24. The curriculum's bottom line is very clear: covered bonds usually offer lower yields than otherwise similar ABS because they are safer.

25. Green covered bonds are just a covered-bond version of the same financing idea, where the cover pool is tied to environmentally friendly assets such as mortgages on green buildings.

#### Covered bonds vs other securitizations

26. If the exam asks for the cleanest difference between covered bonds and most ABS, think balance sheet first.

27. Covered bonds keep the assets on the issuer's balance sheet, while a true securitization removes the asset pool and transfers it into a separate legal entity.

28. That separate legal entity is typically a special purpose entity, or SPE.

29. In a true securitization, the collateral pool is what mainly stands behind the issued securities.

30. In covered bonds, the securities are still fundamentally debt of the issuer, just with extra asset support layered behind them.

31. That is why the curriculum says covered bonds and ABS are complements, not substitutes.

32. ABS are a way to package and sell exposure to private credit risk.

33. Covered bonds are more like a way for banks to raise long-term funding at a lower cost than unsecured debt.

34. Another testable difference is cash-flow style.

35. Covered bonds typically look more like ordinary bonds: often fixed-rate and tied to a fixed maturity date.

36. ABS more often pass through cash-flow behavior from the underlying assets, including early payments.

37. Another big bank-level difference is capital treatment.

38. When loans remain on the bank's balance sheet, as in covered bonds, the bank still carries that risk and still needs capital against it.

39. A securitization can reduce that balance-sheet burden because the assets are moved off the balance sheet.

40. So if the question is about a bank wanting to free up capital, securitization has an advantage that covered bonds do not.

#### Covered-bond redemption regimes

41. The redemption-regime question is pure memory, so you want a very crisp mental picture.

42. In a hard-bullet covered bond, if payments do not occur according to the original schedule, default is triggered and payments accelerate.

43. This is the fastest route to formal default.

44. In a soft-bullet covered bond, default is delayed by extending the final maturity date, usually by up to about a year.

45. So soft-bullet does not mean "no risk"; it means "give the structure more time before calling default."

46. In a conditional pass-through covered bond, if payments are still not made by the original maturity date, the bond converts into a pass-through security.

47. That means investors are repaid as cash is collected from the cover pool instead of by a fixed bullet maturity.

48. If the exam says "new maturity date," think soft-bullet.

49. If it says "missed payment causes immediate default and acceleration," think hard-bullet.

50. If it says "changes form and pays investors as collateral cash arrives," think conditional pass-through.

#### ABS structures to address credit risk

51. A simple securitization could issue only one class of ABS, but then investors are directly exposed to the default risk of the whole collateral pool.

52. That is why most securitizations add credit enhancement.

53. Credit enhancement is financial support that absorbs losses from defaults in the underlying collateral before those losses hit more protected investors.

54. The curriculum focuses on three internal credit enhancements: overcollateralization, excess spread, and subordination.

55. Overcollateralization means the collateral value is larger than the face value of the issued bonds.

56. If a trust issues EUR1,000 million of ABS but has EUR1,200 million of collateral, the extra EUR200 million is the overcollateralization cushion.

57. That cushion means some defaults can happen in the pool without immediately threatening payments on the bonds.

58. Excess spread is the difference between what the collateral earns and what the issued securities pay.

59. If the underlying loans earn 8% and the ABS pays 6%, the 2% gap is excess spread.

60. That extra spread can absorb collateral shortfalls or build reserves.

61. Subordination, also called credit tranching, means the structure issues multiple bond classes that share losses differently.

62. Some tranches are senior, some are subordinated, and the subordinated ones protect the senior ones.

63. The exam can easily hide subordination by calling it a "senior/subordinated structure" or "credit tranching." It is the same basic idea.

64. External credit enhancements also exist, like financial guarantees, letters of credit, and cash collateral accounts, but this module does not develop them in detail.

> [!question] OVERCOLLATERALIZATION
> Problem: A securitization issues EUR700 million of debt and holds EUR840 million of collateral. What is the overcollateralization ratio, and how much collateral cushion exists?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> Overcollateralization ratio:
>
> $$
> \frac{840}{700} = 1.20
> $$
>
> Collateral cushion:
>
> $$
> 840 - 700 = 140
> $$
>
> Explanation: the structure has EUR1.20 of collateral for every EUR1.00 of issued debt, so the first EUR140 million of collateral loss is cushioned before debt investors as a whole are fully exposed.

#### Waterfall and tranche mechanics

65. Once a securitization has multiple tranches, cash flows and losses follow a waterfall.

66. A waterfall means there is an order to who gets paid first and who gets hit first.

67. Senior tranches sit at the top of the capital structure, so they receive principal and interest before subordinated tranches.

68. Junior or equity tranches sit at the bottom, so they absorb losses first.

69. This is why the same collateral pool can create securities with very different risk profiles.

70. In the Car Loan Trust example from the curriculum, the senior Class A tranche has the largest size, the lowest spread, and the most protection.

71. The most junior Class D tranche has no subordination beneath it, so it is first-loss risk.

72. If collateral losses hit EUR25 million, Class D is wiped out first.

73. If losses rise above EUR25 million, the next junior tranche starts taking the damage.

74. So when the exam gives you tranche sizes and total collateral loss, the solving order is always bottom-up for losses.

75. But for payments in normal times, the cash-flow order is top-down.

76. That one reversal is the whole trick: **payments flow top-down, losses bite bottom-up.**

77. The creation of tranches lets investors choose different combinations of risk, return, and expected maturity.

78. It also means a tranche can end up with a better credit rating than the company trying to raise funds.

79. That is not magic. It happens because the rating reflects both the collateral and the structural protection from credit enhancement.

> [!question] WATERFALL LOSS ABSORPTION
> Problem: A securitization has three tranches: senior A = EUR280 million, subordinated B = EUR60 million, and subordinated C = EUR60 million. If collateral losses are EUR140 million, what happens to each tranche?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> First losses hit the most junior tranche C:
>
> $$
> 60 \text{ lost}
> $$
>
> Remaining loss:
>
> $$
> 140 - 60 = 80
> $$
>
> Next losses hit tranche B:
>
> $$
> 60 \text{ lost}
> $$
>
> Remaining loss:
>
> $$
> 80 - 60 = 20
> $$
>
> The last EUR20 million hits senior tranche A:
>
> $$
> 280 - 20 = 260
> $$
>
> Final values:
> - A = **EUR260 million**
> - B = **EUR0**
> - C = **EUR0**
>
> Explanation: losses always burn through the bottom of the capital structure first.

> [!question] TRANCHE INTEREST
> Problem: You buy EUR3 million of Class B notes paying market reference rate (MRR) + 1.5%. If MRR is 4.0%, what annual interest do you receive if the tranche is still performing?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> Coupon:
>
> $$
> 4.0\% + 1.5\% = 5.5\%
> $$
>
> Interest:
>
> $$
> 3{,}000{,}000 \times 0.055 = 165{,}000
> $$
>
> Explanation: once the tranche is still outstanding and still receiving its promised coupon, you just apply the tranche coupon to its principal amount.

#### Non-mortgage asset-backed securities

80. Non-mortgage ABS are securitizations backed by things like auto loans, credit card receivables, personal loans, receivables, and commercial loans.

81. The first big split here is amortizing collateral versus non-amortizing collateral.

82. Amortizing loans, like auto loans and standard residential mortgages, repay both principal and interest over time.

83. So in amortizing ABS, investors start getting scheduled principal repayments as the loans pay down.

84. Prepayments can also be distributed according to the structure's payment rules.

85. Over time, the number of loans and the total collateral balance shrink.

86. Non-amortizing loans, like credit card receivables, are different because they do not have scheduled principal amortization in the same way.

87. For those pools, the structure often goes through a revolving period first.

88. During the revolving or lockout period, principal that comes in is reinvested into new qualifying receivables so the collateral pool gets replenished.

89. After that comes the amortization period, when incoming principal is no longer reinvested and instead is paid out to investors.

90. So when you read a question stem, the fastest way to orient yourself is to ask: is this collateral amortizing or non-amortizing?

#### Credit card receivable ABS

91. Credit card receivables are classic non-amortizing collateral for ABS.

92. When a credit card issuer extends credit to cardholders, those unpaid balances become receivables from the lender's point of view.

93. Those receivables can be pooled and securitized.

94. Credit card securitization helps the issuer in three ways the curriculum emphasizes: it removes receivables from the balance sheet, lowers the cost of funding, and generates fee income.

95. It also reduces the issuer's exposure to default risk from the credit card debt.

96. The cash flows from credit card receivable ABS come from three places: finance charges, fees, and principal repayments.

97. Finance charges are the interest charged on unpaid balances.

98. Fees include things like late-payment fees and membership fees.

99. During the revolving period, noteholders mainly receive payments from finance charges and fees.

100. Principal that gets repaid during that stage is typically reinvested into additional receivables.

101. That is why the curriculum says there is no principal return to investors yet during the revolving period.

102. Once the revolving period ends, the amortization period begins.

103. At that point, repaid principal is no longer used to buy new receivables and instead starts coming back to ABS holders.

104. Some credit card ABS include early amortization or rapid amortization provisions.

105. These provisions are there to protect the issue if the economics of the transaction start weakening, especially during the revolving period.

106. If repayments are not enough to replenish the pool, or defaults distort the pool too much, the structure can force principal back to investors earlier than planned.

107. This is good for investors because they get their money back sooner when the pool is deteriorating.

108. It is not good for the issuer because the structure stops behaving like a long, stable funding source.

109. Credit card ABS often stack multiple supports together, not just one.

110. The curriculum's example combines subordination, overcollateralization, early amortization provisions, and other tools to make the risk-return trade-off more acceptable to investors.

111. One very testable contrast is credit card ABS versus auto loan ABS.

112. Credit card ABS uses non-amortizing collateral, so principal is reinvested during the revolving period.

113. Auto loan ABS uses amortizing collateral, so investors receive regular principal repayments from the beginning and face prepayment risk from the start.

114. So if the exam says "investors receive only finance charges and fees for a while," you should be thinking credit card receivable ABS, not auto loan ABS.

#### Solar ABS

115. Solar ABS usually securitize solar loans or solar leases tied to home energy systems and related improvements.

116. These structures have become more important as households finance solar installations and other energy-efficiency investments.

117. Solar ABS can appeal to institutional investors for two reasons at once: potential yield and sustainability exposure.

118. The curriculum is very explicit that this ESG angle matters to investor demand.

119. In the example structure, the solar ABS is backed by amortizing home-improvement loans.

120. From the issuer's perspective, securitizing those receivables removes them from the balance sheet, improves capital efficiency, and generates fee income.

121. Solar ABS can qualify as green bonds when proceeds are directed to environmentally friendly projects.

122. The legal structure can differ by jurisdiction, but the collateral is usually the underlying debt and may also be supported by a lien on the installed system, the property, or both.

123. When the solar loans are structured as residential home-improvement loans, the curriculum says the ABS effectively securitizes a subordinated, or junior, mortgage on the property.

124. That sounds dangerous at first, but the module explains why investors still like the space.

125. Solar loan borrowers are often prime borrowers who own their homes and have good payment records.

126. On top of that, structures often include overcollateralization, subordination, and excess spread.

127. Many solar ABS also include a pre-funding period.

128. A pre-funding period lets the trust add more qualifying assets after the deal closes, as long as those assets meet the eligibility criteria.

129. The exam can easily try to make you confuse a pre-funding period with a revolving period.

130. A revolving period is about reinvesting principal in a non-amortizing pool like credit card receivables.

131. A pre-funding period is about allowing post-closing acquisition of additional qualifying assets, which the curriculum highlights for solar ABS.

132. Another reason solar ABS can look attractive is that the underlying payment often replaces a household's old utility bill.

133. That does not remove risk, but it helps explain why default losses may stay relatively contained if borrowers still need to pay for energy in some form.

> [!question] REVOLVING PERIOD VS AMORTIZATION PERIOD
> Problem: A credit card ABS collects CHF20 million of principal during its revolving period. What happens to that CHF20 million? What changes once the amortization period starts?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> During the revolving period:
> - The CHF20 million of principal is **reinvested into new qualifying receivables**.
>
> During the amortization period:
> - Principal is **no longer reinvested**.
> - It is **distributed to ABS holders**.
>
> Explanation: this is the cleanest cash-flow distinction for non-amortizing collateral pools.

#### Collateralized debt obligations

134. A collateralized debt obligation, or CDO, issues securities backed by a diversified pool of one or more debt obligations.

135. CDO is the broad family name, not one single structure.

136. If the pool is backed by corporate or emerging-market bonds, it is a collateralized bond obligation, or CBO.

137. If it is backed by leveraged bank loans, it is a collateralized loan obligation, or CLO.

138. If it is backed by other CDOs, it is a structured finance CDO.

139. If the exposure is built synthetically through credit derivatives, it is a synthetic CDO.

140. The prevailing CDO structure in today's market is the CLO.

141. The curriculum makes another big distinction here: unlike many plain ABS pools, most CDO collateral pools are not static.

142. Because the pool is not static, the deal typically needs a collateral manager.

143. The collateral manager buys and sells debt obligations in the pool to keep cash flows sufficient to meet obligations to bondholders.

144. That collateral-manager role is one of the cleanest ways to distinguish a CDO from a CMO or a simple pass-through structure in exam questions.

145. The cash available to pay CDO bondholders can come from interest on collateral assets, principal repayment when assets mature, and proceeds from selling collateral assets.

146. The economics of the transaction are basically leveraged.

147. The structure wants the collateral pool to earn more than the total funding cost of the issued tranches.

148. Senior and mezzanine holders receive more bond-like fixed returns.

149. Equity tranche holders and the CDO manager are aiming for more equity-like residual returns.

150. That is why the curriculum says the equity tranche uses borrowed funds to try to earn a return above the funding cost.

151. So if the deal works well, the equity tranche should earn the highest return.

152. And if things go badly, the equity tranche is also first in line to get hurt.

#### Generic CLO structure and tests

153. A CLO is the main live version of the CDO idea in this module.

154. CLO collateral is typically a diversified portfolio of leveraged bank loans, especially senior secured loans.

155. The CLO finances that collateral by issuing multiple debt tranches plus an equity tranche.

156. Senior and mezzanine CLO debt can offer yields that are potentially higher than comparable corporate bonds.

157. Equity tranche investors take equity-like risk and want high residual returns.

158. The equity tranche also matters strategically because if it cannot earn a competitive return, the whole structure becomes harder to make viable.

159. The curriculum highlights three common CLO types.

160. Cash flow CLOs redistribute interest and principal cash flows from the collateral across tranches, and this is the most common CLO type.

161. Market value CLOs depend on the market value of the portfolio.

162. Synthetic CLOs create the collateral exposure synthetically through credit derivatives.

163. Another important feature is that a CLO collateral portfolio is often not fully finalized at closing.

164. There is usually a ramp-up period after the transaction closes.

165. During ramp-up, the collateral manager adds more assets to the collateral pool.

166. After ramp-up, the manager may replace loans as long as new assets satisfy the portfolio selection criteria.

167. That alone tells you the pool is unstable and actively managed, unlike a stable mortgage pass-through pool.

168. Recourse in a CLO is usually limited to the collateral pool, with minimal recourse to the original issuers.

169. CLO tranches are protected by both the waterfall and a set of performance tests.

170. One major test is the overcollateralization test.

171. The idea is simple: the collateral pool should remain large enough relative to the outstanding CLO debt.

172. If collateral value falls too much and the test is breached, cash gets diverted away from equity and junior debt and redirected toward senior debt or additional collateral support.

173. The curriculum describes this as the structure deleveraging.

174. That makes sense because the cheapest funding source, usually senior debt, gets paid down first when the test fails.

175. CLOs also face other tests, including industry diversification measures, limits on exposure to non-senior secured loans, borrower diversity tests, single-obligor limits, and limits on how much CCC-rated debt can be included.

176. Those tests are there to stop the collateral pool from quietly drifting into a much riskier mix.

177. The manager therefore matters a lot.

178. The manager is not just an administrator; the manager is acting a bit like an active bond portfolio manager inside the structure.

179. That creates an extra layer of risk compared with a simple ABS where collateral cash flows are just passed through without active management.

180. In other words, with a CLO you are taking collateral risk and manager execution risk together.

> [!question] CLO OVERCOLLATERALIZATION TEST
> Problem: A CLO has USD700 million of debt outstanding and investors want the manager to hold USD840 million of collateral. What is the target overcollateralization ratio, and what broad outcome happens if the ratio falls below its trigger?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> Target ratio:
>
> $$
> \frac{840}{700} = 1.20
> $$
>
> If the ratio falls below the trigger:
> - Cash is diverted away from junior and equity tranches.
> - The structure uses cash to protect senior debt or rebuild collateral support.
>
> Explanation: the test is there to keep enough asset cushion behind the CLO debt. When the cushion weakens, the structure starts protecting senior investors first.

#### Analyst traps and comparisons

181. If the exam asks which structures have unstable collateral pools with ongoing collateral management, think covered bonds, CLOs, and some solar ABS with pre-funding.

182. If it asks which structure has a stable pool, mortgage-backed securities are the more natural answer, not the structures in this module.

183. If it asks which structure keeps collateral on balance sheet, the answer is covered bonds.

184. If it asks which structure has dual recourse, the answer is covered bonds.

185. If it asks which structures usually have single recourse, think non-mortgage ABS and CDOs.

186. If it asks which internal credit enhancement creates multiple bond classes with different loss-sharing rules, the answer is subordination or credit tranching.

187. If it asks which enhancement is simply "collateral value exceeds bond face value," the answer is overcollateralization.

188. If it asks which enhancement is "collateral yield minus security coupon," the answer is excess spread.

189. If it asks which ABS investors receive only finance charges and fees during an early phase of the deal, think credit card receivable ABS during the revolving period.

190. If it asks which structure may use a pre-funding period to add qualifying assets after closing, think solar ABS.

191. If it asks which CDO subtype is most common, think cash flow CLO as the main structure and CLO as the prevailing CDO family member.

192. If it asks which CLO type depends on market value instead of just redistributed cash flows, think market value CLO.

193. If it asks which CLO type relies on credit derivatives, think synthetic CLO.

194. If it asks what makes CLO investing riskier than plain ABS in an extra way, the answer is collateral-manager risk.

195. The manager might fail to earn enough from the collateral pool to support senior and mezzanine investors and still make the structure attractive for the equity tranche.

196. The whole module is really one long lesson in asking four questions: who owns the collateral, who has recourse, who gets paid first, and who takes the first loss.
