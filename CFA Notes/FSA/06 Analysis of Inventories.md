> [!tip] SEE THIS JUST BEFORE EXAM
> 1. Inventory is not just "stuff in the warehouse." It is cash that has not yet become cost of goods sold or cash inflow. Ask this first: ==did this cost help get the product ready for sale?== If yes, it may belong in inventory. If not, expense it now.
>
> 2. **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)** allow **specific identification**, **first-in, first-out (FIFO)**, and **weighted average cost**. **United States generally accepted accounting principles (US GAAP)** allow those plus **last-in, first-out (LIFO)**. **==IFRS DOES NOT ALLOW LIFO.==**
>
> 3. If prices are stable, the cost formula barely matters. If prices are moving, it matters a lot.
>
> 4. In inflation, **FIFO** usually gives lower cost of goods sold, higher ending inventory, and higher profit. **LIFO** usually does the opposite.
>
> 5. In deflation, flip that logic.
>
> 6. **Memorize these numbers, because LCNRV AND LCM is confusing:** original invoice cost is **210**, expected selling price is **225**, selling cost is **22**, normal profit is **12**, and replacement cost is **197**. First calculate NRV: if I sell today, I receive 225, but I pay 22 to sell, so I keep **203**. That 203 is NRV. 
> 7. Under lower of cost and NRV, I compare original cost **210** with NRV **203**, and I carry inventory at **203**. So the write-down is 7
> 8. **For lower of cost or market, memorize the corridor:** the upper bound is NRV, so **203**. The lower bound is NRV minus normal profit, so **191**. Replacement cost is **197**, and because 197 sits between 191 and 203, market is **197**. Now compare original cost **210** with market **197**, and carry inventory at **197**. So the write-down is **13**. 
> 9. Now what happens if net realizable value rises above the cost let's say it's become 220. Now LCNRV is 210 and earlier it was 203 so IFRS would allow you to make a reversal and increase the value of your inventory, but US GAAP will never do it.
> 10. IFRS usually uses **lower of cost and net realizable value**. US GAAP also uses that for many firms, but **LIFO** and retail-method users go into **lower of cost or market** territory. 
> 11. Under **lower of cost or market**, market does not just mean replacement cost. ==It means replacement cost, but capped by net realizable value and floored by net realizable value minus normal profit.==
> 12. Think of it like this. If I normally sell an item for 120, and after selling costs I keep 110, that 110 is my NRV. Now suppose I normally expect 20 profit on this kind of item. Then the lowest market value I should use is 110 minus 20, so 90. So in lower of cost or market, replacement cost is not allowed to fall below 90.
>
> 13. Big reversal trap: IFRS can reverse a prior write-down, but only up to the original cost. **==US GAAP does not allow the write-up back.==**
>
> 14. Inventory write-down hurts profit, current assets, total assets, and equity right now.
>
> 15. Because inventory gets smaller, inventory turnover usually rises after a write-down and days of inventory usually fall. That can make a weak business look "more efficient" if you are not paying attention.
>
> 16. **LIFO reserve** is the bridge from LIFO numbers to FIFO-like numbers.
>
> 17. If finished goods keep rising while sales do not, slow down. That may be growth, or it may be demand weakness hiding in the warehouse.

> [!abstract] ROTE MEMORIZE
> - Allowed methods: IFRS = specific identification, FIFO, weighted average cost; US GAAP = those plus LIFO.
> - IFRS: lower of cost and NRV; reversal allowed up to the previous write-down.
> - US GAAP: no reversal of inventory write-downs.
> - LIFO and retail-method users under US GAAP use lower of cost or market with ceiling and floor.
> - Inflation: FIFO -> lower cost of goods sold, higher inventory, higher profit; LIFO -> higher cost of goods sold, lower inventory, lower profit.
> - Inventory write-down lowers assets and equity and usually hurts margins.

> [!info] HOW TO READ THIS MODULE
> Inventory is cash wearing a product costume.
> The real questions are: what costs belong inside inventory, which cost formula is being used, is inventory overstated, what happens in inflation or deflation, and what are the notes quietly telling me?

## Exam-heavy segments: Inventory module

#### Inventory measurement

1. Inventory includes costs incurred to bring the product to its present location and condition for sale. That sounds dry, but the exam trap is simple: inventory includes product-readying costs, not every cost that happened somewhere near the product.

2. Purchase price, freight-in, import duties, and similar costs usually belong inside inventory because they helped get the item ready for sale.

3. Abnormal waste, unnecessary storage, and other inefficient or non-production costs do not get to hide inside inventory. They are expensed now, because they did not create a legitimate future inventory benefit.

4. Manufacturers may show one inventory number on the balance sheet, but the notes still matter because the notes can break that total into raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods.

5. That breakdown is useful because rising raw materials tells a different story from rising finished goods. Raw materials can mean planned production. Finished goods piling up can mean the company made more than customers wanted.

> [!warning] MISTAKES YOU WILL MAKE
> - Storage costs for finished products are usually excluded from inventory.
> - Do not say every production-related cash outflow belongs in inventory.
> - LIFO is not allowed under IFRS.

#### Cost formulas

6. Cost formulas decide which cost layer leaves inventory first and becomes cost of goods sold. The physical product flow and the accounting cost flow do not have to match.

7. If prices do not change, the choice of cost formula barely changes reported numbers. Once prices move, the method starts shaping profit, inventory, and tax effects.

8. Specific identification tracks the exact cost of the exact item sold. It works best when items are distinct, expensive, and individually traceable, like cars, art, or custom machinery.

9. FIFO assumes the oldest costs leave first, so the newest costs remain in ending inventory.

10. Weighted average cost smooths the layers by averaging them, so cost of goods sold and ending inventory both sit between the FIFO and LIFO extremes when prices are moving.

11. LIFO assumes the newest costs leave first and is allowed only under US GAAP. That one permission difference is one of the easiest IFRS-versus-US-GAAP traps in this topic.

#### Lower of cost and net realizable value

12. Net realizable value means expected selling price minus the costs still needed to complete and sell the inventory. It is basically the amount the firm realistically expects to recover from that inventory.

13. Under lower of cost and net realizable value, compare historical cost with net realizable value and carry inventory at the lower number.

14. So if inventory cost is USD 105 and net realizable value is USD 103, the carrying value becomes USD 103 and the USD 2 shortfall is recognized in income.

15. Under IFRS, inventory is measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value, and a later recovery can reverse a prior write-down, but only up to the amount previously written down and never above original cost.

16. Under US GAAP, many non-LIFO and non-retail-method users also compare cost with net realizable value, but the big memory trap is that US GAAP does not allow the later write-up back.

#### Lower of cost or market

17. Lower of cost or market appears mainly for US GAAP firms using LIFO or the retail method. This is where students get trapped by the word "market."

18. Market here does not simply mean replacement cost on its own. It starts with replacement cost, but then gets capped by a ceiling and supported by a floor.

19. The ceiling is net realizable value. The floor is net realizable value minus normal profit margin. So replacement cost is only accepted if it stays inside that band.

20. After you find that bounded market amount, you compare cost with market and carry the lower number.

> [!question] INVENTORY WRITE-DOWN
> Problem: A company sells digital cameras. Cost is USD 210 per unit, expected selling price is USD 225, selling costs are USD 22, normal profit margin is USD 12, and replacement cost is USD 197. What are the carrying values under lower of cost and net realizable value and under lower of cost or market?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{NRV} = 225 - 22 = 203
> $$
>
> Lower of cost and net realizable value:
>
> $$
> \text{Carry at} = \min(210, 203) = 203
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Write-down} = 210 - 203 = 7
> $$
>
> Lower of cost or market:
>
> $$
> \text{Ceiling} = 203
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Floor} = 203 - 12 = 191
> $$
>
> Replacement cost is USD 197, which sits inside the USD 191 to USD 203 range, so market is USD 197.
>
> $$
> \text{Carry at} = \min(210, 197) = 197
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Write-down} = 210 - 197 = 13
> $$
>
> Explanation: Lower of cost and net realizable value uses the direct cost-versus-NRV test. Lower of cost or market first forces replacement cost through the ceiling-and-floor filter.

#### Periodic vs perpetual inventory systems

21. Imagine the same chocolate shop. You buy **1 chocolate for 10**.  Then you sell **1 chocolate**.  Then you buy **1 chocolate for 20**.
22. Now rank the methods from simplest exam intuition. **First: FIFO.**  FIFO says, “the first chocolate bought is the first chocolate sold.” So the sold chocolate cost **10**, and the remaining chocolate is **20**. Periodic or perpetual usually does not change this logic.
23. **Second: Weighted average.**  Weighted average says, “do not track exact chocolate; blend the costs.” If you have chocolates costing 10 and 20, average cost is **15**. It smooths the answer, so it usually sits between FIFO and LIFO.
24. **Third: LIFO.**  LIFO says, “the latest chocolate is sold first.” This is where periodic versus perpetual becomes tricky.
25. Under **perpetual LIFO**, you ask at the time of sale: “what is the latest chocolate available right now?” Only the 10 chocolate exists, so cost of sales is **10**.
26. Under **periodic LIFO**, you wait until the end. By then, the 20 chocolate also exists, so LIFO says the sold chocolate cost **20**.

#### Inventory write-down effects

24. When inventory is written down, inventory falls, current assets fall, total assets fall, profit falls, and equity falls. The loss usually runs through cost of goods sold or a separate write-down line in income.

25. Current ratio usually falls because current assets are now lower while current liabilities did not get smaller just because the warehouse item lost value.

26. Inventory turnover often rises after a write-down because average inventory is lower, and days of inventory often fall for the same reason. That is why a write-down can create a fake-looking efficiency improvement.

27. Gross margin, operating margin, and net margin usually weaken because cost of goods sold or write-down expense is higher.

28. Total asset turnover can rise mechanically after a write-down because assets are lower, not because the operating machine suddenly got better.

29. Debt-to-assets and debt-to-equity can look worse because assets and equity fell while debt did not.

> [!question] INVENTORY WRITE-UP AFTER PRIOR WRITE-DOWN
> Problem: Using the earlier example, assume the next year net realizable value and replacement cost both rise by USD 10. What happens under IFRS and under US GAAP?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> New net realizable value:
>
> $$
> 203 + 10 = 213
> $$
>
> New replacement cost:
>
> $$
> 197 + 10 = 207
> $$
>
> IFRS:
> - Prior carrying value under lower of cost and net realizable value was USD 203.
> - Original cost was USD 210.
> - The write-down can be reversed, but only up to original cost.
> - So carrying value rises back to USD 210, and the reversal gain is USD 7.
>
> US GAAP:
> - No write-up is allowed.
> - The carrying value stays at the previously written-down amount.
>
> Explanation: IFRS allows the recovery to come back, but only up to what was lost before. US GAAP locks the write-down in place.

#### Inflation and deflation effects

30. In inflation, FIFO pushes older cheaper costs into cost of goods sold first, so cost of goods sold is lower, ending inventory is higher, gross profit is higher, and usually taxes are higher too.

31. In the same inflation setting, LIFO pushes newer more expensive costs into cost of goods sold first, so cost of goods sold is higher, ending inventory is lower, profit is lower, and taxes are usually lower.

32. Weighted average cost usually lands somewhere between FIFO and LIFO in inflation because it smooths the layers instead of choosing one extreme.

33. In deflation, reverse the logic. Older layers are more expensive, so FIFO can produce higher cost of goods sold and lower profit than LIFO.

> [!info] FIFO VS LIFO 
> Imagine a grocery store buys soap at USD 1 early in the year and USD 2 later in the year.
> Under FIFO, the income statement acts like the sold soap cost USD 1, so profit looks fatter and ending inventory looks more current and expensive.
> Under LIFO, the income statement acts like the sold soap cost USD 2, so profit looks thinner but cost of goods sold is closer to current replacement cost.
> That is why inflation turns inventory accounting into an earnings-quality issue, not just an accounting-format issue.

#### LIFO reserve and analyst adjustments

34. If a company uses LIFO, its inventory is usually too low in rising prices, because old cheap costs stay on the balance sheet. So the note gives you the **LIFO reserve**, which tells you how much extra inventory would exist under FIFO.
35. if LIFO reserve **increases**, prices are rising and FIFO inventory is pulling further ahead. For adjustment, subtract the increase from cost of goods sold. Example: Reserve goes from 60 to 80. Increase is 20. Reduce cost of goods sold by 20.
36. If LIFO reserve **decreases**, old cheap inventory may have been sold. That is **LIFO liquidation**. It can artificially increase profit because old low costs enter cost of goods sold.
37. ==If you want a rough FIFO-style inventory number for a LIFO user, add the LIFO reserve to LIFO inventory.==
38. **LIFO inventory is 500.** **LIFO reserve is 80.** This means: under FIFO, inventory would be higher by 80. Why? Rising Prices, you are getting rid of older cheaper inventory under FIFO. 
39. You bought inventory in two batches:
		**Old batch:** 10 items bought at 5 each, so total cost is 50.  
		**New batch:** 10 items bought at 10 each, so total cost is 100.
		Now suppose LIFO inventory shows only **50**, but FIFO inventory would show **100**. That gap of **50** is the **LIFO reserve**.
		Now if next year the reserve becomes **70**, the gap increased by 20. **==That means LIFO pushed more expensive cost into cost of goods sold, so profit looked lower.==**

40. This matters because comparing one company’s LIFO margins with another company’s FIFO margins without adjustment can make an accounting-method difference look like an operating-performance difference.
41. LIFO reserve is usually shown in the **notes to the financial statements**, inside the **inventory accounting policy / inventory disclosure** section.

#### Inventory disclosures

38. The inventory note should tell you the accounting policy and cost formula, carrying amounts by category, the amount recognized as expense, write-downs recognized, and inventories pledged as collateral.

39. Under IFRS, disclosures also include reversals of write-downs and the circumstances behind them. **==Under US GAAP, LIFO users often disclose the LIFO reserve instead, and there is no reversal disclosure because reversals are not allowed.==**

40. The note matters because the balance sheet gives you one inventory number, but the note tells you what kind of inventory it is, how conservative the accounting was, and whether the company may be using inventory accounting to flatter margins or hide weakening demand.

> [!tip] REAL-WORLD HOOK
> Fashion retailers live and die by inventory. A Zara jacket this month is inventory. The same jacket after the season may be a markdown waiting to happen.
> That is why inventory is not "almost cash" by default. Sometimes it is yesterday's fashion sitting under bright store lights.
