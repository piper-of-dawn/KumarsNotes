#### Financial statement analysis framework

1. Financial statement analysis is not just “reading statements.” Exam trick starts there. It’s you interpreting a company’s performance and financial position inside its economic environment, and then turning that into a decision.

2. What is financial statement analysis: using financial reports, plus other information, to judge a company’s **past, current, and possible future** performance and financial position (and yes, risk shows up in this story too). What is financial reports: the company’s disclosure package (statements, notes, required filings, and often management commentary). Why is financial statement analysis used: to support an investment, credit, or other economic decision (buy, lend, rate, value, etc.).

3. And the core question isn’t just “did they make money?” It’s more like: can they earn at least their cost of capital, grow profitably, generate cash, meet obligations, and still pursue opportunities?

4. What is cost of capital: the required return demanded by providers of capital. Why is cost of capital used: it’s the hurdle rate; earning below it means growth can destroy value.

5. Okay, now where do you begin in practice? You start with the company’s financial reports: audited financial statements, required regulatory disclosures, and management commentary. Then you sharpen that with industry and company research.

6. What is a financial report: a package of company disclosures (statements, notes, required filings, and sometimes management’s explanation). Why is a financial report used: it’s your starting map for analysis, not the full territory.

7. CFA can trick you by changing the user of analysis. Same financial reports, totally different questions: debt analysis asks “will I get paid back?”, while equity analysis asks “what’s the upside after everyone else gets paid?”

8. That’s why the framework starts with ==**articulating purpose and context**==. Without the question, ratios are just noise. You can calculate forever and still not know what decision your work is supposed to support.

9. Purpose isn’t “fluff” either, it directly decides your tools, your data, your depth, your report format, your deadline, and what parts of the analysis matter most.

10. A quick self-check before you touch numbers: “If every ratio appeared instantly, what decision could I make?” If you can’t answer, your analysis doesn’t have a spine yet.

11. Once the purpose is clear, you can write the specific questions. Comparing three industry peers usually pushes you toward growth and profitability questions, while a credit decision pushes you toward leverage, coverage, and cash-flow questions.

12. The second step is ==**collecting data**==: you gather the information you need to answer your questions. What is collecting data: assembling the financial statements and other relevant info (industry, economic, management, etc.) before you start “doing math.” Why is collecting data used: missing inputs create confident-looking conclusions that are just wrong.

13. While collecting, you have to understand the business model: how the company makes money from customers, costs, assets, and financing. What is business model: the company’s “money engine” behind the numbers. Why is business model used: without it, ratios look smart but explain nothing.

14. Financial statement data alone can help you screen (like minimum profitability or sales growth), but deeper questions such as “why did this company outperform peers?” need industry, economic, and business context.

15. That’s also why top-down analysis exists: macro conditions shape industry prospects, industry prospects shape company prospects, and your company forecasts get more realistic when you build them inside that environment.

16. Now that you have data, the third step is ==**processing it**==: clean, scale, adjust, and structure it. This is where common-size statements, ratios, growth rates, charts, regressions, simulations, forecasts, sensitivity analysis, and valuation tools help you compare and make sense of things.

17. What is common-size analysis: converting statement items into percentages (like a percentage of sales or a percentage of assets) so you can compare different-sized companies. Why is common-size analysis used: it removes size distortion so comparisons actually mean something.

18. What is a ratio: a relationship between financial statement items used to study profitability, liquidity, leverage, efficiency, or valuation. Why is a ratio used: it compresses information into a comparable signal, but it still needs interpretation.

19. The annoying part is comparability: business models, lease-versus-purchase decisions, accounting policies, and tax jurisdictions can make two “similar-looking” companies economically different.

20. Once processing is done, the fourth step is ==**analyzing and interpreting**==: you convert the processed output into meaning. Big CFA trap here: treating the numerical answer as the final answer, when you still need judgment.

21. Also, forecasts, fair value estimates, free cash flow, interest coverage, and leverage aren’t separate islands. They support real decisions like buy/hold/sell, lend/refuse, or assign a rating.

22. Then you communicate conclusions: present the answer in a format suitable for the task, institution, and audience. For example, external equity reports often include a summary, an industry view, a model, valuation, and risks.

23. Communication needs control because users need enough facts, assumptions, risks, and limitations to judge your recommendation instead of blindly trusting you.

24. This is basically Standard V(B) of Standards of Practice logic: separate fact from opinion, explain the factors that drove your recommendation, and disclose limitations and investment risks so the reader can evaluate your conclusion.

25. Finally, follow-up keeps the process alive. New information changes actual results versus expected results, so forecasts, valuations, ratings, holdings, and recommendations may need revision.

26. Even if you rejected an investment, follow-up can still matter: price may fall, business conditions may change, or new information may make a previously unattractive security worth revisiting.

> [!example]
> Sea Limited reported second-quarter 2022 revenue growth and huge losses, then suspended e-commerce guidance. The story moved fast: numbers came out, analysts compared them with expectations, macro uncertainty entered the frame, and the stock fell. That is financial statement analysis in motion, not a spreadsheet exercise.

#### Roles of financial statement analysis

27. Another trap: this is not only backward-looking. Past numbers are used mainly to form expectations about future performance, future financial position, and risk factors, and then you use those expectations for investment, credit, and other economic decisions.

28. If the user is **management**, they also do financial analysis, but they’re different because they can access internal nonpublic information. External analysts mostly work from public and supplementary sources.

29. So remember: financial statement analysis is decision-making support, not accounting record-keeping. In exam terms, it shows up as choices like equity inclusion, security valuation, setting loan terms, assigning debt ratings, evaluating venture capital and private equity investments, and assessing merger or acquisition candidates.

30. When the user is an **equity analyst**, your mental dashboard leans toward profitability and per-share value, while the **credit analyst** brain leans toward repayment ability, cash flow, leverage, and interest coverage.

31. Profitability is not the same thing as cash flow, and CFA can absolutely trap you there. A company can report accounting profit while cash is trapped in receivables, inventory, or reinvestment needs.

#### Regulatory framework and filings

32. Once you accept that reported profit can look “fine” while cash is getting tight, you naturally care about rules and enforcement. Regulatory authorities require publicly traded issuers to prepare financial reports under specified accounting standards and securities laws, creating minimum disclosure discipline for investors.

33. What is International Organization of Securities Commissions: a global body whose members regulate most financial capital markets and promote investor protection, fair markets, transparency, and lower systemic risk.

34. **==Systemic risk is the risk that trouble spreads beyond one issuer into the wider financial system.==** What is systemic risk: contagion risk, where one entity’s trouble triggers broader system damage.

35. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission regulates the market and requires the filings analysts use: registration statements, annual filings, interim filings, proxy statements, and current reports.

36. The Securities Act of 1933 is the “initial sale disclosure” law, so think new securities being sold to investors. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 is the “ongoing reporting” law, so think public companies staying under disclosure discipline after trading begins.

37. Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 is the post-scandal tightening move: stronger auditor independence, executive certification, internal control reporting, and oversight through the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board.

38. Registration statements matter because they pull together offered-security details, capital structure links, recent audited statements, and business risk factors before investors commit capital.

#### Forms and reports

39. Forms 10-K, 20-F, and 40-F are annual filings. Form 10-K is for United States registrants, Form 40-F is for certain Canadian registrants, and Form 20-F is for other non-United States registrants.

40. Inside annual filings you usually get the business overview, risk factors, financial disclosures, legal proceedings, management information, audited statements, notes, management discussion and analysis, and the audit report.

41. The annual report and the Form 10-K overlap, but they are not the same vibe. The annual report is more polished and shareholder-facing; the Form 10-K is the more legal, regulatory document.

42. If the question smells like voting or governance, think proxy statement or Form DEF-14A: proposals, management ownership, principal owners, director biographies, and executive compensation live there.

43. For interim updates, Forms 10-Q and 6-K are the key names to remember. Form 10-Q is quarterly for United States companies, while Form 6-K is often used by many non-United States companies.

44. If something material happens between periodic filings, think current report. Form 8-K is the current report for United States registrants, and Form 6-K fills much of that role for many non-United States registrants.

#### Notes, supplementary schedules, and management commentary

45. Financial statement notes are not decoration. This is where accounting policies, assumptions, estimates, debt details, contingencies, commitments, segment information, related-party items, and a lot of the real interpretive landmines sit.

46. Supplementary schedules extend the statements with extra breakdowns that do not fit neatly into the main statements but still matter to analysis.

47. Management commentary explains results through management’s eyes, which is useful for strategy, trends, risks, and outlook, but remember the built-in bias: useful does not mean audited.

48. What is management discussion and analysis: management’s explanation of financial condition and operating results. Why is management discussion and analysis used: it connects the accounting numbers to business causes, liquidity, capital resources, and future expectations.

#### Audit report and assurance

49. What is audit report: the independent auditor’s written opinion on whether audited statements fairly present financial position, performance, and cash flows under the relevant accounting standards. Why is audit report used: it adds confidence, but it does not give certainty.

50. What is reasonable assurance: high confidence, not certainty. Audits use sampling and the statements themselves contain estimates, so audit assurance is not absolute assurance.

51. Unqualified, unmodified, or clean opinion is the one you prefer: statements are fairly presented under the applicable standards.

52. Qualified opinion means “mostly okay, but there is a stated problem,” usually a scope limitation or accounting exception. That is why you read the explanation instead of relaxing at the word “qualified.”

53. Adverse opinion means the statements are materially not fairly presented. Disclaimer of opinion means the auditor cannot give an opinion at all, often because the scope was too limited.

> [!warning]
> A clean audit opinion does not mean “the company is safe.” It means the statements are reasonably and fairly presented under accounting rules. Business risk, valuation risk, liquidity risk, and management bias can still remain.

54. Key Audit Matters and Critical Audit Matters highlight hard-judgment areas, higher misstatement risk, significant estimates, or major transactions, but they are not automatically the most important items for investors.

55. Internal control reports can give some comfort because management must assess controls and auditors may confirm them, but you still need skepticism.

#### Segment reporting

56. Segment reporting matters because it stops you from judging the company as one big blob. It helps you see where revenue, profit, loss, assets, and weakness are really coming from.

57. What is an operating segment: a part of the company that earns revenue or incurs expenses, whose results are reviewed by senior management, and for which separate financial information exists.

58. Not every operating segment becomes a reportable segment, and CFA likes that trap. The usual threshold is **10 percent or more of revenue, assets, or profit/loss**.

59. Even after the 10 percent test, reportable segments must cover at least **75 percent of external revenue**. If they do not, the company has to add more segments until that test is met.

60. Segment reporting also helps you judge business quality because one segment may produce most of the revenue but weak profits, while another may produce less revenue but most of the economics.

61. Companies can disclose segment revenue, profit or loss, assets, liabilities if reviewed, interest, depreciation, tax, non-cash expenses, and reconciliations back to consolidated numbers.

#### IFRS and US GAAP

62. IFRS are set by the International Accounting Standards Board, while US GAAP are set by the Financial Accounting Standards Board. If two companies use different systems, do not compare them lazily.

63. Convergence has made the systems more similar over time, but the differences still matter enough to distort comparability, forecasts, and valuation if you ignore them.

64. One broad reason differences show up is style: US GAAP is more rules-based, while IFRS is more principles-based, and that can affect classification and measurement.

65. Interest paid is a classic trap. US GAAP classify it as operating cash flow, while IFRS allow operating or financing classification.

66. Inventory valuation is another trap. US GAAP allow first in, first out; last in, first out; and weighted average, while IFRS allow first in, first out and weighted average.

67. Development costs also differ. US GAAP usually expense them, while IFRS allow capitalization if specified conditions are met.

68. Inventory write-down reversal differs too: US GAAP prohibit reversal, while IFRS allow reversal if specified conditions are met.

69. This is why analysts monitor standards as users, not preparers. The real question is not “how do I book it?” but “how do these rules change the numbers I rely on?”

70. This gets more important when new products or transactions show up, like fintech structures, digital assets, and structured financial instruments, because reporting guidance can be thin and judgment can become the whole game.

#### Other information sources

71. Filings are not the whole map. Issuer sources beyond filings include earnings calls, investor day events, press releases, company websites, company visits, and direct conversations with management or investor relations.

72. Earnings calls are management presentation plus Q&A, so analysts use them to understand differences from expectations, guidance changes, acquisitions, restructurings, and management’s explanation of results.

73. Investor days go deeper on business segments and strategy, but they are still management-framed, so you have to listen with sharper filters.

74. Press releases matter because material events, products, management changes, board changes, mergers, acquisitions, and restructurings often show up there before the fuller filing detail arrives.

75. Company visits and product experience can add ground-level evidence. If you can walk into the store, use the service, or observe operations, the numbers stop feeling abstract.

76. Public third-party sources include analyst reports, industry reports, government and economic data, general and industry news, and even social media customer sentiment.

77. Proprietary third-party sources and proprietary primary research can add an edge: paid data platforms, surveys, conversations, and product evaluations can reveal details that filings miss.

> [!example]
> Picture T-Mobile getting credit upgrades after operational and financial progress. The headline is not just “rating improved.” The deeper story is subscriber growth turning into cash flow, leverage becoming more visible, debt-market access opening, and the company’s financial position changing in lenders’ eyes.

78. If you want one clean mental model under pressure: purpose defines the question, data answers the question, processing makes data comparable, interpretation creates meaning, communication supports decisions, and follow-up keeps the decision alive.

79. And that is the real exam vibe here: CFA will not reward blind ratio memorization as much as it rewards knowing where information comes from, how reliable it is, what bias it carries, and what decision it supports.
