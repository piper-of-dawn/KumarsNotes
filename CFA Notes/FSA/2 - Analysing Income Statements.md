

> [!tip] SEE THIS JUST BEFORE EXAM
> 1. Do not jump straight to margins. First ask: ==is this a revenue-recognition issue, an expense-timing issue, a one-off item, or a dilution issue?==
>
> 2. For revenue, memorize the flow like this: **contract, performance obligations, transaction price, allocate price, recognize revenue.**
>
> 3. Collectability trap: **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)** uses a lower “probable” hurdle than **United States generally accepted accounting principles (US GAAP)**, so the same shaky customer may qualify sooner under IFRS.
> 4. Suppose collectability is **55 percent likely**. Under IFRS, the contract may qualify because it is more likely than not. Under US GAAP, it may not qualify because “likely” is stronger than just above 50 percent. Suppose collectability is **80 or 90 percent likely**. Then both IFRS and US GAAP likely pass. Suppose collectability is **40 percent likely**. Then both fail.>
> 5. If the company is the real seller, think **principal** and gross revenue. If it is just arranging the sale, think **agent** and net commission revenue. If I am the **principal**, I control the product before it goes to the customer. So I record the **full selling price as revenue**.
> 6. Example: I buy a product for 70 and sell it for 100. I report revenue of 100, cost of goods sold of 70, and gross profit of 30. My gross margin is 30 percent.
> 7. If I am the **agent**, I only arrange the sale for someone else. I do not control the product. So I record only my **commission or fee** as revenue.
> 8. Example: customer still pays 100, but my commission is 30. I report revenue of only 30. There is no cost of goods sold for the product. Gross profit is still 30, but gross margin becomes 100 percent. That is the trap: **same economics, different reported revenue and margins**.
>
> 9. **Bill-and-hold** is not “customer paid, so book revenue.” It works only when control has really passed and the seller cannot redirect the product.
>
> 10. Capitalize versus expense is a timing game, not a total-cost game. ==Capitalizing usually makes current profit look better, but future depreciation or amortization comes back to bite.==
>
> 11. Do not say “capitalizing always boosts profit.” It helps only while new capitalized spending is bigger than current depreciation or amortization.
>
> 12. **Capitalized interest** is still interest. For coverage analysis, pull it back in instead of letting it hide inside an asset.
>
> 13. **Discontinued operations** go below continuing operations and are shown **net of tax**.
>
> 14. **==Bad-news trap: expected disposal losses can be recognized earlier; expected disposal gains must wait until the sale is done.==**
>
> 15. **==Change in accounting policy is usually retrospective. Change in estimate is prospective. Do not mix those up.**==
>
> 16. **Basic earnings per share (EPS)** uses weighted average ordinary shares, not just ending shares.
>
> 17. **Stock splits** are applied retroactively to the weighted share count. Do not time-weight the split date like a normal share issue.
>
> 18. For **convertible bonds**, add back **after-tax interest** to the numerator.
>
> 19. For **options and warrants**, only the leftover shares from the treasury stock method are dilutive.
>
> 20. Common-size income statement means every line is divided by **revenue**.
>
> 21. Margin trap: most income-statement margins use **revenue** in the denominator; turnover ratios are a different conversation.

> [!abstract] ROTE MEMORIZE
> - Five-step revenue model: contract -> performance obligations -> transaction price -> allocate -> recognize.
> - Principal = gross revenue. Agent = net commission revenue.
> - Expense timing models: match to revenue, expense as incurred, or capitalize then depreciate/amortize.
> - Discontinued operations: separate, after continuing operations, net of tax.
> - Basic EPS = earnings available to ordinary shareholders / weighted average ordinary shares.
> - Convertible debt dilution: add back after-tax interest and add conversion shares.
> - Treasury stock method: options are dilutive only if exercise price is below market price.
> - Common-size income statement: divide by revenue.

> [!info] HOW TO READ THIS MODULE
> The income statement is the company saying, "Here is how we performed over the period."
> Your job is to ask: did revenue really happen, were expenses timed properly, which items will not repeat, how many shares split the profit, and what do margins actually tell me?

## Exam-heavy segments: Income Statement module

#### Revenue recognition

1. Revenue recognition is really a timing-and-amount question: how much revenue is the company allowed to record, and exactly when is it allowed to record it?

2. The five-step model is the spine here: identify the contract, identify the performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate that price across the obligations, and recognize revenue only when each obligation is satisfied.

3. Do not memorize those five steps as dead words. Read them like a real business story: first check there is a real deal, then figure out what was promised, then figure out what the customer is expected to pay, then split that price sensibly, and only then decide when the company has actually earned it.

4. A subscription example makes the timing trap obvious. If Netflix collects United States dollars 120 for a one-year subscription, that is not day-one revenue just because the cash came in. The cleaner economic story is about United States dollars 10 per month as the service is delivered.

5. Before you even start the five-step model, ask whether IFRS or US GAAP would treat the customer agreement as a real contract at all. If collectability is too doubtful, revenue recognition does not get to start properly.

6. The exam trap is that both systems use the word "probable," but not with the same practical hurdle. IFRS is usually more like "more likely than not," while US GAAP usually wants stronger evidence that collection is likely to occur.

7. So if the customer is weak and payment confidence is only barely above a coin toss, IFRS may be more willing to treat the arrangement as a contract than US GAAP. Same customer, same economics, different accounting doorway.

8. Under the converged standards, incremental costs of obtaining a contract and some costs of fulfilling it are capitalized instead of being expensed immediately. That means a company can look more profitable after adoption even if the business itself did not suddenly improve.

#### Principal vs agent

9. Principal versus agent is one of the easiest ways to get fooled by reported revenue size. If the company controls the good or service before it reaches the customer, it is closer to principal and reports gross revenue.

10. If the company is just arranging the sale for someone else, it is closer to agent and reports only the commission or fee as revenue. Same economic deal can produce totally different revenue and margin optics.

11. Amazon is the clean memory hook. If Amazon sells its own inventory, it reports the full selling price as revenue and the inventory cost as cost of goods sold. If Amazon Marketplace just connects buyer and seller, Amazon reports the commission, not the customer’s full payment.

12. That is why you should never compare net margins across companies without first checking whether one reports gross and the other reports net. The "better margin" may just be a presentation effect.

#### Licensing / SaaS / subscriptions

13. Franchising and licensing create the same kind of trap. A company-owned restaurant records the full restaurant sale, but a franchisor usually records the royalty or fee, not the burger-and-fries revenue earned by the franchisee.

14. Upfront franchise fees are another timing trap. The parent normally does not dump the whole fee into revenue on day one; it often records deferred revenue first and recognizes it over the franchise term.

15. Software forces you to separate what the customer gets now from what the customer keeps receiving later. A software license can support earlier revenue recognition, while support, updates, or a software-as-a-service subscription usually push revenue over time.

16. So traditional Microsoft Office was closer to "you got the software now," while Microsoft 365 is closer to "you keep receiving access and service over time."

#### Long-term contracts

17. Long-term construction and production contracts recognize revenue over time when control passes as work is performed. The key idea is not "long project equals over-time revenue"; the real question is whether the customer is effectively receiving control or enforceable value as the work happens.

18. Progress can be measured using outputs such as units completed or inputs such as costs incurred relative to total expected cost. If a United States dollars 10 million contract is 60 percent complete on a cost basis, you do not wait until final delivery to pretend nothing happened before then.

#### Bill-and-hold sales

19. Bill-and-hold means the customer bought the product but wants the seller to keep it temporarily. Revenue is allowed only if the reason is genuine, the product is specifically identified for that customer, it is ready for delivery, and the seller cannot just redirect it elsewhere.

#### Expense recognition

20. Selling, general, and administrative expense sits on the income statement as normal overhead rather than direct product cost. Selling expense supports the sales effort, while general and administrative expense covers the head-office machine.

21. Expense recognition is about matching cost to the period that consumed the benefit. Put more simply, the company should not show the revenue now and quietly park the related cost somewhere else just to flatter profit.

22. There are three big timing patterns to remember: match the cost to related revenue, expense it as incurred, or capitalize it first and then let depreciation or amortization push it through profit later.

23. The matching model is the cleanest with inventory. Inventory starts life as an asset, and only when the product is sold does that cost move into cost of goods sold.

24. That is why unsold inventory is not yet an expense. It is still waiting on the balance sheet for the period in which the related sale happens.

25. Period costs are different because they support the period as a whole rather than one specific sale. Administrative salaries, ordinary repairs, many information-technology costs, and a lot of routine overhead live here.

26. The trap is thinking "it helped the business" automatically means it should be capitalized. Rent helps the business. Advertising helps the business. Routine maintenance helps the business. That still does not mean they created a separable long-lived asset.

#### Capitalizing vs expensing

27. Capitalization means recording spending as an asset first and expensing it later through depreciation or amortization. Depreciation is the spreading of a tangible asset’s cost over its useful life, while amortization does the same job for many intangible assets.

28. Land is the classic exception because it is usually capitalized but not depreciated, and indefinite-life intangibles are usually tested for impairment instead of being amortized.

29. Capitalize versus expense changes timing, not the total pre-tax cost over the full life. If equipment costs euro 900 and lasts three years, you either take euro 900 now or about euro 300 per year, but the full cost still lands eventually.

30. The analytical punchline is simple: capitalizing usually raises current profit, current assets, and current equity, while immediate expensing does the opposite now and removes future depreciation or amortization later.

31. You only keep getting that "capitalization boost" while new capitalized spending is larger than the depreciation or amortization already flowing through the income statement. Once old assets catch up, the free-looking lift disappears.

32. Depreciation method and useful-life estimates matter because management can shape current earnings through them. Longer useful lives, higher salvage values, or slower depreciation all make current profit look better.

33. Impairment is the opposite kind of signal. It says the carrying value was too high, so profit is hit now even though the cash damage may have happened earlier. That often tells you past reported earnings were more flattering than they should have been.

#### Capitalized interest

34. Capitalized interest is just debt cost tucked into an asset instead of being shown immediately as interest expense. The accounting label changed, but the company still needed financing and still bore that interest burden.

35. If a company builds a refinery for its own use, capitalized interest usually becomes part of property, plant, and equipment and later leaks out through depreciation. If a developer builds apartments for sale, the same idea can sit inside inventory and later flow through cost of sales.

36. For credit analysis, do not let that accounting hide-and-seek fool you. Interest coverage should be based on total interest burden, not just the interest expense line shown this year.

37. Capitalized interest can also flatter operating cash flow because the cash outflow may be classified with the asset instead of sitting in operating cash flow. Total cash did not improve; only the label moved.

38. Software development costs are another classic judgment area. Before technological feasibility, the spending is usually treated like research and development expense. After that point, some costs may be capitalized until the product is ready for general release.

39. A software company that capitalizes aggressively can look stronger on earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, operating cash flow, and even valuation multiples than a similar company that expenses more development immediately.

40. If you want comparability, the usual adjustment is to expense the current development spending, remove amortization of previously capitalized software from expense, and then bring assets, equity, and cash flow classifications back to a more comparable base.

41. Expense estimates are where management can quietly shape earnings without changing the headline business story. Watch bad-debt allowances, warranty estimates, useful lives, salvage values, and any other assumption that moves expense timing.

#### Non-recurring items

42. Nonrecurring items are material events that are unusual in nature or infrequent in occurrence, such as asset-sale gains or losses, impairments, write-downs, write-offs, or restructuring costs.

43. Do not mechanically strip every nonrecurring item out of analysis. If a company seems to have a "special" restructuring charge every other year, that pattern is telling you something about the real economics of the business.

44. A discontinued operation is a narrower and more important category. It is a business component that management has decided to dispose of and that is clearly distinct in operations and financial activities from the rest of the firm.

45. Once a business qualifies as discontinued, its results move out of continuing operations and are shown separately near the bottom of the income statement, net of tax.

46. Net of tax means the tax effect is already baked in, so do not mentally tax the number again when you read it.

47. There is a one-sided conservatism trap here. Expected losses on disposal can be recognized when the disposal plan qualifies, but expected gains wait until the sale is actually completed.

48. For forecasting, discontinued operations should usually be removed from future recurring earnings because that business is not supposed to keep generating profit or cash flow for the company after disposal.

49. Accounting policy changes and accounting estimate changes are not the same thing. A policy change is usually handled retrospectively, while an estimate change is usually handled prospectively.

50. The memory hook is this: policy change rewrites the old movie so periods are more comparable, but estimate change only changes the next scenes because the new information belongs to today and tomorrow, not yesterday.

#### EPS

51. Basic earnings per share measures the earnings available to ordinary shareholders divided by the weighted average number of ordinary shares outstanding during the period.

$$
\text{Basic EPS} = \frac{\text{Net income} - \text{Preferred dividends}}{\text{Weighted average ordinary shares}}
$$

52. **Preferred dividends are removed because earnings per share is trying to isolate what belongs to ordinary shareholders after the preferred claim is taken out.**

53. Weighted average shares means you do not just grab year-end shares. You weight each share count by the fraction of the year for which that share count actually existed.

54. Stock splits are the classic exam trap here. Once a split happens, you restate the share count as if the split had been in place for the whole period, so the weighted average denominator is adjusted retroactively. **Long story short: Just adjust the denominator.** **==Example: Suppose a 2-for-1 stock split takes effect on 1 December and company has 100 shares. The Weighted Avg. Shares would become 200 in the denominator. Everything else remains same.==**

55. Diluted earnings per share asks the what-if question: what would earnings per share look like if dilutive instruments such as convertible bonds, convertible preferred stock, options, or warrants became ordinary shares?

56. For convertible bonds, you assume conversion and add back after-tax interest to the numerator because the company would no longer be paying that interest, but it also would no longer be getting the tax shield from that interest.

$$
\text{Diluted EPS numerator adjustment for convertible debt}
=
\text{After-tax interest saved}
$$

57. For convertible preferred stock, add back the preferred dividends to the numerator and add the extra shares from conversion to the denominator.

58. **Options and warrants use the treasury stock method. This is important** 

	**First figure out if the exercise is economic:**
	- If exercise would be uneconomic because the exercise price is above market price, the instrument is not dilutive and you leave it out.
	- If exercise is economic, assume the options are exercised, 
		- Assume cash comes in (Shares times Exercise Price), call it A 
		- Assume that cash repurchases shares at the average market price (Shares times Market Price), call it B.
		- A and B are different because exercise price is different from market price.
		- Calculate B - A and use the money to buy or sell the shares at market price 
		- Only the leftover shares increase the denominator. Those leftover shares are the true dilution.
		- For written puts, company spends cash and could have bought more shares in market, so the missed extra shares become dilution.
		- For calls/options, company receives cash and buys back shares.
		-  Always remember if the exercise is economic to the option holder then weighted average number of shares must always increase that is dilution would hurt EPS always.

59. Memorise both the numericals below. **I mean truly mug up, the numbers as well.**  You might not have time to rebuild the correct logic during the live CFA exam.

> [!question] NUMERICAL
> Suppose the company has **1,000 written put options** with exercise price **50**, and the average market price is **30**.
> 
> Is exercise economic? Yes
> Put is an obligation to buy. Cash must go out
> Cash that goes out = 50 $\times$ 1000 = 50000
> How much stock this outgoing cash could have been bought at market price = 50000 / 30 = 1667
>  **The company only offered 1000 shares but now it has 1667 shares so the weighted average number of shares increased by 667.**


> [!question] NUMERICAL
> Suppose the company has **1,000 call options**, exercise price **30**, and average market price **50**.
> Exercise is economic. Call option is an obligation to sell
> Cash that comes in = 30 $\times$ 10000 = 30000
> Use this 30000 cash to buy shares at market price =  30000 / 50 = 600
> **The company sold 1000 shares but now also bought back 600 so only 400 shares would increase the denominator that is weighted average number of shares.**

60 . Diluted earnings per share should never be higher than basic earnings per share. If an assumed conversion makes earnings per share go up instead of down, the security is antidilutive and should be excluded.

> [!question] WEIGHTED AVERAGE SHARES
> Problem: A company has 1,000,000 shares outstanding on 1 January, issues 200,000 shares on 1 April, and repurchases 100,000 shares on 1 October. What is the weighted average number of shares for the year?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> 1{,}000{,}000 \times \frac{3}{12} = 250{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> 1{,}200{,}000 \times \frac{6}{12} = 600{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> 1{,}100{,}000 \times \frac{3}{12} = 275{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Weighted average shares} = 250{,}000 + 600{,}000 + 275{,}000 = 1{,}125{,}000
> $$
>
> Explanation: Each share count is weighted by how long it was actually outstanding, not by what the company ended the year with.

> [!question] STOCK SPLIT
> Problem: Using the weighted average shares above, suppose a 2-for-1 stock split takes effect on 1 December. Net income is United States dollars 2,500,000 and preferred dividends are United States dollars 200,000. What is basic earnings per share?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Adjusted weighted shares} = 1{,}125{,}000 \times 2 = 2{,}250{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Basic EPS} = \frac{2{,}500{,}000 - 200{,}000}{2{,}250{,}000} = 1.022
> $$
>
> Explanation: The split is treated as if it had existed throughout the period, so the denominator is restated retroactively.

> [!question] DILUTED EPS WITH CONVERTIBLE BONDS
> Problem: Oppnox reports net income of United States dollars 750,000 and has a weighted average of 690,000 ordinary shares. It also has United States dollars 50,000 of 6 percent convertible bonds that can convert into 10,000 shares. The tax rate is 30 percent. What is diluted earnings per share?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{After-tax interest add-back} = 50{,}000 \times 0.06 \times (1 - 0.30) = 2{,}100
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Adjusted numerator} = 750{,}000 + 2{,}100 = 752{,}100
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Adjusted denominator} = 690{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 700{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Diluted EPS} = \frac{752{,}100}{700{,}000} = 1.0744
> $$
>
> Explanation: You assume the bonds converted, so the interest burden disappears, but only after tax.

> [!question] OPTIONS AND WARRANTS
> Problem: A company reports earnings available to ordinary shareholders of United States dollars 1,200,000 and has 500,000 ordinary shares outstanding all year. It also has 100,000 options with an exercise price of United States dollars 15. The average market price is United States dollars 20. What is diluted earnings per share under the treasury stock method?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Cash from exercise} = 100{,}000 \times 15 = 1{,}500{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Shares repurchased} = \frac{1{,}500{,}000}{20} = 75{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Net new shares} = 100{,}000 - 75{,}000 = 25{,}000
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Diluted EPS} = \frac{1{,}200{,}000}{500{,}000 + 25{,}000} = 2.2857
> $$
>
> Explanation: The company is treated as issuing all option shares and then buying back some of them with the exercise cash. Only the leftover shares dilute earnings per share.

#### Common-size income statement

61. A common-size income statement expresses each income-statement line as a percentage of revenue. It turns raw currency amounts into proportions, which makes cross-company and across-time comparison much easier.

$$
\text{Common-size line item} = \frac{\text{Income statement line item}}{\text{Revenue}}
$$

62. This is why a retailer, a software company, and a luxury brand can all be compared without size fooling you. One company may have huge revenue and thin margins, while another has smaller revenue and thick margins because the business model is different.

63. Gross margin tells you how much of each revenue dollar remains after cost of goods sold. Operating margin tells you how much survives after the core operating cost structure. Net margin tells you what finally reaches the bottom line after everything, including tax and financing effects.

64. The big exam habit is to read margin movement causally. Rising gross margin can mean better pricing, cheaper input cost, or better mix. Falling operating margin can happen even if gross margin is stable if selling, general, and administrative expense is swelling.

65. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization can be useful for comparing operating performance before financing and non-cash depreciation effects, but it can also flatter businesses that need heavy reinvestment. Do not confuse a strong EBITDA margin with strong free cash flow.

66. Common-size analysis also helps spot classification issues. A principal-versus-agent shift, a capitalization policy change, or a big non-recurring item can change margins even when the business reality did not improve as much as the headline suggests.

67. The denominator rule is simple here: when you are doing common-size income statements or income-statement margins, revenue is the denominator unless the ratio itself explicitly says otherwise.

> [!tip] QUICK CHECKS
> - Contract first, revenue second.
> - Gross versus net revenue matters before margin comparison.
> - Capitalization changes timing, not total pre-tax cost.
> - Capitalized interest still counts for debt-burden analysis.
> - Discontinued operations sit below continuing operations and are shown net of tax.
> - Stock splits are applied retroactively to earnings per share denominators.
> - Convertible bonds require an after-tax interest add-back.
> - Treasury stock method dilutes only by the leftover shares.
