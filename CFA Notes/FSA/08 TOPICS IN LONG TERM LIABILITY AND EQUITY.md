> 1. Lease from the lessee side usually means: "I got use of the asset now, and I promised payments later." So think **right-of-use asset + lease liability**.
> 2. Lease from the lessor side usually means: "Did I keep renting out my asset, or did I basically turn it into a receivable?"
> 3. Defined contribution plan means the employer promises the **deposit**. Defined benefit plan means the employer promises the **retirement outcome**.
> 4. Share-based compensation is still salary expense even if cash does not move today.
> 5. The exam keeps asking the same deep question in different clothes: **who bears the risk?**
> 6. Mini numerical. Lease liability opening **100**, discount rate **10%**, lease payment **30**.
>    Interest expense = **100 x 10% = 10**.
>    Principal repayment = **30 - 10 = 20**.
>    Closing lease liability = **100 + 10 - 30 = 80**.
> 7. Mini numerical. Same lease, right-of-use asset amortization **25** per year.
>    Under International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), Year 1 total lease expense = **interest 10 + amortization 25 = 35**.
>    Year 2 interest will be lower because the liability already fell to **80**.
>    That is why IFRS lease expense is front-loaded.
> 8. Mini numerical. Plan assets **90**, defined benefit obligation **120**.
>    Funded status = **90 - 120 = -30**.
>    Negative means **underfunded**, so the company has a net pension liability of **30**.
> 9. Mini numerical. Employer promises to contribute **8** each year to your retirement account.
>    That is a defined contribution plan because the employer promised the **input**, not the final pension.
>    If markets crash, the employee suffers the bad investment outcome.
> 10. Mini numerical. A stock grant worth **60** vests evenly over **3** years.
>     Compensation expense each year = **60 / 3 = 20**.
>     No cash today does not mean no expense today.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> - Lease = right to use a specific asset for a period of time in exchange for payment.
> - Finance lease = feels like buying the asset slowly.
> - Operating lease = feels more like renting.
> - Under IFRS and United States generally accepted accounting principles (US GAAP), the lessee usually records both a right-of-use asset and a lease liability.
> - Under IFRS, lessee expense usually appears as amortization plus interest. Under a US GAAP operating lease, the income statement usually shows one straight-line lease expense.
> - Defined contribution plan = employer defines the contribution.
> - Defined benefit plan = employer defines the pension benefit.
> - Under IFRS defined benefit accounting, service cost and net interest go to profit and loss, while remeasurements go to other comprehensive income.
> - Share-based compensation is measured using fair value at the grant date and then recognized over the vesting period.

#### Lease Basics and Classification

1. **A lease is a contract that gives someone the right to use a specific asset for a period of time in exchange for payment.**  
   What is right to use: You control how the asset is used during the contract period.  
   What is specific asset: One identifiable thing, such as one truck, one machine, one aircraft, or one shop floor.  
   What is payment: Cash or cash-like payments you promise to make for that use.  
   In very normal language, a lease says: "I do not own this asset, but for some time I control it and I must pay for that privilege."

2. Imagine Starbucks needs a shop in Mumbai but does not want to buy the building. It signs a long contract, gets control of that one shop, and pays year by year. That is why lease accounting often feels like debt accounting wearing different clothes.

3. From the user side, called the lessee, a lease often feels like financing. From the owner side, called the lessor, a lease often feels like rent income or a slow-motion sale.

4. For a contract to be a lease, three things must all be true:
   - A specific asset must be identified.
   - The customer must get most of the economic benefits from that asset during the period.
   - The customer must decide how that asset is used.

> [!info] IMAGINE YOU TOOK ONE TRUCK
> Imagine Reliance signs for one specific truck for two years.
> Only Reliance can use that truck.
> Reliance decides where it goes, what it carries, and when it moves.
> That smells like a lease.
> But if Reliance only pays a logistics company to deliver goods and the logistics company can send any truck it wants, that is a service, not a lease.

5. Leasing is popular because it reduces upfront cash pain, can be cheaper than unsecured borrowing, and lets a business use expensive assets without buying them outright.

6. Airlines love leases for exactly this reason. Buying every aircraft with cash would be brutal. Leasing lets the airline fly now and pay over time.

7. **A finance lease is a lease that, in substance, looks like a purchase paid over time.**  
   What is in substance: The real economics matter more than the label printed on the contract.  
   What is purchase paid over time: The lessee is taking most of the asset's life, value, or ownership-like benefit and paying in installments.  
   If that purchase-like feel is missing, the arrangement is closer to an operating lease.

8. A lease is usually treated as a finance lease if any one of these signs is strong enough:
   - Ownership transfers to the lessee.
   - The lessee has a bargain purchase option and is reasonably sure to use it.
   - The lease term covers a major part of the asset's useful life.
   - The present value of lease payments is substantially all of the asset's fair value.
   - The asset is so specialized that the lessor has no easy alternative use for it.

> [!warning] EASY MEMORY TRICK
> If the lessee is taking most of the life, most of the value, or something very close to ownership, call it a finance lease.
> If the lessee is using the asset temporarily and then walking away without taking most of the economics, call it closer to renting.

9. Suppose Apple leases a machine for ten years and the machine itself only lasts ten years. Be honest: that is not normal renting. Apple is basically using up the whole machine, so the deal feels like a purchase over time.

10. Now flip it. Suppose Walmart leases a machine for one year and the machine can last twenty years. That feels much more like renting than buying.

#### Lessee Accounting

11. Under both IFRS and US GAAP, long leases usually go on the balance sheet for the lessee as:
   - a right-of-use asset, and
   - a lease liability.

12. **The right-of-use asset is the economic benefit you now control. The lease liability is the payment promise you now owe.**  
   What is economic benefit: The useful service you can get from the asset during the lease period.  
   What is payment promise: The future cash you committed to pay.  
   The balance sheet is simply showing both sides of the same story.

> [!info] WHY BOTH SIDES SHOW UP
> Imagine you lease a shop for your business.
> The moment you sign a serious long contract, you got something valuable: the right to use that shop.
> But you also created an obligation: you now owe future payments.
> That is why the balance sheet shows both an asset and a liability.

13. The lease liability starts at the present value of future lease payments.

$$
\text{Lease liability at inception} = \text{Present value of future lease payments}
$$

14. After that, the lease liability follows the same old loan logic:

$$
\text{Interest expense} = \text{Opening lease liability} \times \text{Discount rate}
$$

$$
\text{Principal repayment} = \text{Lease payment} - \text{Interest expense}
$$

$$
\text{Closing lease liability} = \text{Opening lease liability} + \text{Interest expense} - \text{Lease payment}
$$

15. Do not panic at those formulas. They are just debt formulas. Early on, the liability is large, so the interest slice is large. Later, the liability shrinks, so more of each payment kills principal.

16. The right-of-use asset is then amortized, usually on a straight-line basis over the lease term unless the facts clearly point to another pattern.

17. Under IFRS, the lessee uses one basic model. The income statement usually shows:
   - amortization expense on the right-of-use asset, and
   - interest expense on the lease liability.

18. That means total lease expense is often front-loaded. Early years look more expensive because the interest part is higher when the liability is still large.

> [!info] WHY EARLY YEARS LOOK HEAVIER
> Imagine Netflix leases equipment and starts with a big lease liability.
> In Year 1, the interest is calculated on that big number.
> In Year 2, the liability is smaller, so interest is smaller.
> Amortization might stay flat, but interest falls.
> That is why total IFRS lease expense is heavier early and lighter later.

19. Under IFRS cash flow presentation, principal repayment usually sits in financing activities. Interest paid can be operating activities or financing activities depending on accounting policy.

20. Under US GAAP, lessee accounting has two income-statement shapes:
   - finance lease shape, which looks very similar to IFRS, and
   - operating lease shape, which keeps the balance sheet recognition but changes the income statement look.

21. Under a US GAAP operating lease, the company still records the right-of-use asset and the lease liability, but the income statement usually shows one straight-line lease expense instead of separate amortization and interest lines.

22. In the background, the liability still follows interest logic. The right-of-use asset amortization is just adjusted so that total lease expense stays flat.

$$
\text{Lease expense} = \text{Interest expense} + \text{Amortization plug}
$$

23. Under US GAAP, the full cash payment for an operating lease is generally shown in operating activities. That is one reason ratios can look different from IFRS even when the economics are similar.

24. This creates classic exam traps:
   - US GAAP operating lease usually gives lower earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) than IFRS.
   - Operating cash flow is usually lower under US GAAP operating lease presentation.
   - Total assets can stay a bit higher early on because the right-of-use asset is not falling in the same visible way as the IFRS split presentation.

> [!tip] HAMMER THIS INTO YOUR HEAD
> Lessee question = "What asset did I gain control over, and what liability did I create?"
> Once you see that, the rest is just debt math plus amortization.

> [!question] LESSEE MINI NUMERICAL
> Problem: A company starts Year 1 with a lease liability of **100**. The discount rate is **10%** and the lease payment at year-end is **30**. The right-of-use asset is amortized by **25** for the year. Calculate Year 1 interest expense, principal repayment, closing lease liability, and total IFRS lease expense.
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Interest expense} = 100 \times 10\% = 10
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Principal repayment} = 30 - 10 = 20
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Closing lease liability} = 100 + 10 - 30 = 80
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Total IFRS lease expense} = 10 + 25 = 35
> $$
>
> Explanation:
> This is just loan logic plus amortization. The payment is partly interest and partly principal. Under IFRS, you separately recognize interest expense and amortization expense.

#### Lessor Accounting

25. **From the lessor side, the question changes to this: am I still mainly renting out my asset, or did I effectively convert it into a receivable?**  
   What is receivable: Money to be collected from the customer in the future.  
   Why is this the question: Because lessor accounting depends on whether the asset is still the star of the story or whether a financial claim is now the main thing left.

> [!info] IMAGINE YOU GAVE AWAY MOST OF THE ECONOMICS
> Imagine Tata Motors owns a machine and gives it to another company for most of the machine's useful life.
> The other company squeezes almost all of the benefit out of it.
> Tata Motors mostly sits back and collects payments.
> At that point the machine matters less than the money claim.
> That is why a lessor finance lease starts to look like a receivable.

26. In a lessor finance lease:
   - the lessor removes the physical asset from the balance sheet,
   - recognizes a lease receivable,
   - recognizes interest income over time, and
   - may recognize a gain or loss at inception if carrying value and receivable value differ.

27. In a lessor operating lease:
   - the lessor keeps the physical asset on the balance sheet,
   - continues to depreciate it, and
   - recognizes lease revenue over time.

28. Cash receipts from leases are operating cash flow for the lessor under both types. Do not overcomplicate that point on the exam.

29. The lessor finance lease logic is basically: "The asset left my balance sheet as an operating asset and came back as a financial asset."

30. The lessor operating lease logic is the opposite: "The asset is still my asset. I am just earning rent from it."

> [!question] LESSOR CLASSIFICATION FEEL
> Problem: A lessor gives one highly specialized machine to a customer for most of the machine's useful life. The present value of lease payments is almost equal to the machine's fair value. Is the lessor still mostly carrying a machine, or mostly carrying a receivable?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
> The lessor is mostly carrying a receivable, so the deal looks like a finance lease.
>
> Explanation:
> The customer has taken most of the machine's economics. The lessor is now mainly waiting to collect money.

#### Deferred Compensation and Pension Logic

31. Deferred compensation simply means this: the employee works now, but some compensation is paid later.

32. Normal salary paid at month-end is easy. Pension promises are hard because the cash often comes many years later and depends on uncertain assumptions.

33. **A defined contribution plan is a pension plan in which the employer promises the contribution amount, not the final pension amount.**  
   What is contribution amount: The amount the company agrees to put into the plan.  
   What is final pension amount: The retirement benefit the employee eventually enjoys after investments and real life do their thing.  
   In plain language: the employer is saying, "I promise the deposit. I do not promise how rich that deposit will make you later."

34. Imagine Infosys says, "We will put 8 into your retirement bucket every year." If markets do well, the bucket grows nicely. If markets collapse, the bucket suffers. Infosys promised the deposit, not your retirement lifestyle.

35. That is why in a defined contribution plan, the employee mainly bears:
   - investment risk, and
   - actuarial risk.

36. Investment risk means the plan assets may earn less than hoped. Actuarial risk means real life may behave differently from the guess, such as different retirement timing or lifespan.

37. Accounting for a defined contribution plan is simple:
   - employer contribution for the period = pension expense,
   - cash paid = operating cash outflow,
   - unpaid promised contribution at year-end = accrued liability.

> [!info] WHY DEFINED CONTRIBUTION IS EASY
> The company promised an input, not an outcome.
> Inputs are easy to count.
> "We paid 100 into the plan" is clean.
> "We promise your retirement income no matter what markets do" is messy.
> That mess is the defined benefit world.

38. **A defined benefit plan is a pension plan in which the employer promises the benefit the employee will receive later.**  
   What is benefit: The pension payout the employee gets after retirement.  
   What is promise: The employer is taking responsibility for the outcome, not just the deposit.  
   In casual language: the company is saying, "Do not worry only about the bucket. I am promising what comes out of the bucket."

39. Imagine a company promises, "After retirement, we will pay you 70 percent of your final salary every year until death." That is a serious promise. To account for it, the company must estimate salary growth, discount rates, lifespan, retirement age, and plan asset performance.

40. That is why defined benefit plans are harder and riskier. The employer mainly bears:
   - investment risk, and
   - actuarial risk.

41. The pension obligation is estimated by discounting promised future payments back to today.

42. Many defined benefit plans also have plan assets sitting in a separate fund. Those assets are there to help pay the promised benefits.

43. The net funded status is:

$$
\text{Net pension asset or liability} = \text{Fair value of plan assets} - \text{Defined benefit obligation}
$$

44. If plan assets are bigger than the obligation, the plan is overfunded. If the obligation is bigger, the plan is underfunded.

> [!info] THINK OF TWO BUCKETS
> Bucket 1 says: "What the company owes retirees."
> Bucket 2 says: "What the company has already set aside."
> If Bucket 2 is smaller, the company still has a hole to fill.
> That hole is the net pension liability.

45. One exam shortcut is brutally useful:
   - defined contribution plan = employer promises the input,
   - defined benefit plan = employer promises the output.

46. Another exam shortcut is just as useful:
   - overfunded plan = possible net asset,
   - underfunded plan = net liability.

> [!question] DEFINED CONTRIBUTION OR DEFINED BENEFIT
> Problem: Your employer says, "We will put 8 percent of your salary into your retirement account every year." Is that defined contribution or defined benefit?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
> It is a defined contribution plan.
>
> Explanation:
> The employer promised only the input, which is the 8 percent contribution. The employer did not promise your final retirement payout.

#### Defined Benefit Accounting and Other Comprehensive Income

47. Under IFRS, the change in the net pension position is usually split into three big pieces: service cost, net interest, and remeasurements.

48. Service cost means employees worked one more year and earned one more slice of future pension.

49. Net interest means time passed, so the pension promise moved simply because the payment date is one year closer.

50. Remeasurements are the surprise bucket. They capture things such as people living longer than expected, salary assumptions changing, or plan assets performing differently from what the accounting expected.

51. Under IFRS, service cost, past service cost, and net interest go to profit and loss, while remeasurements go to other comprehensive income.

52. Under IFRS, those remeasurements are not recycled through profit and loss later. They stay in other comprehensive income.

53. Under US GAAP, the same economic story is chopped into more pieces and smoothed more over time.

54. Under US GAAP, service cost and interest expense go to profit and loss, expected return on plan assets reduces pension expense, and past service cost plus actuarial gains and losses usually begin in other comprehensive income and are amortized over time.

> [!warning] BIG PENSION FEEL
> IFRS says: "Show current service and time cost in profit and loss. Dump the measurement shocks in other comprehensive income."
> US GAAP says: "Spread more of the pain over time."

55. Pension expense is often hidden inside normal operating lines. Factory worker pension cost can flow into inventory and then cost of goods sold. Office worker pension cost can sit inside selling, general, and administrative expense.

56. That is why analysts should never stop at "pension expense" as a single neat line. The pieces can be buried across the statements and the notes.

> [!question] FUNDED STATUS MINI NUMERICAL
> Problem: A defined benefit plan has plan assets of **90** and a defined benefit obligation of **120**. During the year, service cost is **8**, net interest is **4**, and a remeasurement loss of **6** occurs under IFRS. What is the funded status at the start, and which items go to profit and loss versus other comprehensive income?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Funded status at the start} = 90 - 120 = -30
> $$
>
> The plan starts **underfunded by 30**.
>
> Items going to profit and loss:
> - service cost **8**
> - net interest **4**
>
> Item going to other comprehensive income:
> - remeasurement loss **6**
>
> Explanation:
> Under IFRS, the current service and time-passage pieces hit profit and loss. The surprise measurement piece goes to other comprehensive income.

#### Share-Based Compensation

57. Share-based compensation means the company pays employees using shares, share-linked promises, or stock-value-linked promises instead of only cash salary.

58. The idea sounds fancy, but the human story is simple: "Work for us, stay with us, help increase the company's value, and part of your reward will move with the stock price."

59. Companies like this because it can retain employees and reduce current cash pressure. Shareholders like it because it tries to align managers with owners.

60. But there is no free lunch. Share-based compensation is still compensation expense. If a company pays you with stock instead of cash, your work was still not free.

61. **Share-based compensation is usually measured at fair value on the grant date and recognized as compensation expense over the vesting period.**  
   What is grant date: The date the company gives the award promise.  
   What is vesting period: The period you must serve, or the conditions you must satisfy, before the award truly becomes yours.  
   In plain language: the company values the gift when it promises it, then spreads that salary expense over the years you are earning it.

62. If vesting is immediate, expense is recognized immediately. If vesting takes time, expense is spread across that time.

63. **Restricted stock units are promises to give you shares later if you stay long enough or meet stated conditions.**  
   What is restricted: You cannot fully keep the award yet.  
   What is unit: A share promise, not necessarily a share already sitting in your hand.  
   Imagine your boss says, "Stay three more years and these shares become yours." That is the flavor.

64. For stock grants and restricted stock units:
   - expense is based on the share price at the grant date,
   - expense is recognized over the vesting period, and
   - the offsetting entry usually increases additional paid-in capital.

65. This confuses many people, so say it slowly. Compensation expense reduces profit. Lower profit reduces retained earnings. But the credit entry increases additional paid-in capital. So one part of equity goes down and another part goes up.

> [!info] IMAGINE YOUR BONUS IS PAID IN SHARES
> Imagine Microsoft tells an employee, "We will give you stock worth 60 if you stay for three years."
> Economically, that is still salary.
> Microsoft is consuming that employee's work during those three years.
> So Microsoft must gradually record compensation expense even before the employee receives the shares.

66. Performance shares are similar, except the final award depends on targets such as earnings, return on assets, or margin performance.

67. That sounds smart, but it also creates temptation. If pay depends on accounting numbers, managers may feel pressure to massage those numbers.

68. **Stock options give the employee the right to buy shares later at a fixed exercise price.**  
   What is right: The employee can choose whether to exercise.  
   What is exercise price: The fixed amount the employee must pay to buy the shares later.  
   In plain language: "If the stock later rises above this fixed price, the employee wins."

69. Stock options are measured at fair value on the grant date, but unlike a plain stock grant, that fair value usually has to be estimated with an option pricing model.

70. Important inputs include:
   - exercise price,
   - expected volatility,
   - expected life,
   - dividend yield,
   - risk-free rate,
   - expected forfeitures.

71. Higher volatility usually makes an option more valuable. Longer life usually makes it more valuable. Higher dividend yield usually makes it less valuable.

72. Once the grant-date fair value is measured, that amount is expensed over the vesting period for equity-settled awards. Later stock-price changes do not keep resetting the expense.

73. When employees exercise the option, the company receives cash equal to the exercise price and issues shares.

74. Options can expire worthless if the stock price never rises above the exercise price. That is one reason restricted stock units became popular in many firms.

> [!info] WHY OPTIONS CAN PUSH RISK-TAKING
> Imagine you own only stock.
> If the stock crashes, you suffer badly, so you may become cautious.
> Now imagine you hold options.
> If the stock shoots up, you gain a lot.
> If the stock stays below the exercise price, the option just dies.
> That one-sided payoff can tempt managers to chase extra risk.

75. Some share-based compensation is cash-settled. Stock appreciation rights and phantom stock can create a liability instead of only an equity entry.

> [!warning] DO NOT MISS THESE TRAPS
> Stock-based pay is still a real expense.
> No cash today does not mean no cost today.
> Stock options and stock ownership do not create the same incentive pattern.

> [!question] SHARE GRANT MINI NUMERICAL
> Problem: A company grants shares worth **60** on the grant date. The employee must stay for **3** years to receive them. What compensation expense should be recognized each year if the award is recognized evenly?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Annual compensation expense} = 60 / 3 = 20
> $$
>
> Explanation:
> The company is paying for three years of service, not one day of service. So the expense is spread across the three-year vesting period.

#### What Analysts Should Look for in Disclosures

76. This last section is about reading the note like an analyst, not like decoration.

77. Lease disclosures are there to help you judge the amount, timing, and uncertainty of lease cash flows.

78. For lessee lease disclosures, hunt for:
   - carrying amount of right-of-use assets,
   - total lease cash outflow,
   - interest expense on lease liabilities,
   - amortization or depreciation of right-of-use assets,
   - additions to right-of-use assets,
   - maturity analysis of lease liabilities,
   - restrictions, covenants, renewal options, and exposures not fully captured in the liability.

79. For lessor lease disclosures, hunt for the split between finance lease information and operating lease information, plus the pattern of expected receipts over time.

80. Defined contribution plan disclosure is usually lighter because the accounting is simpler and the employer did not promise the final pension outcome.

81. Defined benefit plan disclosure is heavier because the estimate is more judgment-heavy and more dangerous.

82. In a defined benefit note, always look for:
   - the pension obligation,
   - the fair value of plan assets,
   - the net funded status,
   - key assumptions such as discount rate,
   - what hit profit and loss,
   - what hit other comprehensive income.

83. Share-based compensation disclosures should tell you:
   - what kind of awards exist,
   - how they were valued,
   - what assumptions were used,
   - current-period compensation expense,
   - how much unrecognized compensation cost still remains for future periods.

84. If a company says it has a large amount of unrecognized compensation cost related to nonvested awards, that means future salary expense is already waiting in the pipeline.

> [!tip] QUICK CHECKS BEFORE EXAM
> - Lease: ask who controls the asset and who bears the ownership-like risk.
> - Lessee under IFRS: separate amortization and interest.
> - Lessor finance lease: physical asset leaves, receivable comes in.
> - Defined contribution plan: employer promised the deposit.
> - Defined benefit plan: employer promised the outcome.
> - Funded status = plan assets minus defined benefit obligation.
> - Share-based compensation: still salary expense even without current cash payment.
