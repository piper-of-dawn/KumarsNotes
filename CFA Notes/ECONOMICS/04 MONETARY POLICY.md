> [!abstract] SEE THIS BEFORE EXAM
> 1. Central bank = monopoly supplier of domestic currency, banker to the government, bankers' bank, lender of last resort, payments-system referee, and monetary-policy conductor. If you remember only one objective, remember ==price stability==.
> 2. Three main tools = open market operations, policy rate, and reserve requirements.
> 3. Open market purchase = central bank buys government bonds from banks = bank reserves rise = lending capacity can rise = broad money can grow.
> 4. Open market sale = central bank drains reserves = banks have less room to lend = money growth cools.
> 5. Policy rate is the loudest public signal. If it rises, commercial-bank lending rates usually rise with it.
> 6. Transmission mechanism runs through four linked doors: short-term interest rates, asset prices, exchange rate, and expectations.
> 7. Neutral rate is roughly: **trend real growth + long-run expected inflation**.
>
> $$
> \text{Neutral rate} \approx \text{Trend growth} + \text{Inflation target}
> $$
>
> 8. If trend growth is 3% and inflation target is 2%, neutral rate is about 5%. Below 5% is expansionary. Above 5% is contractionary.
> 9. Inflation targeting works only if the central bank has independence, credibility, and transparency. Without those three, the target is just a press release.
> 10. Exchange-rate targeting lets a weak-inflation-credibility country "borrow" credibility from a stronger one, but you pay for that by giving up domestic monetary freedom.
> 11. Monetary policy becomes weak in deflation because cutting rates below zero is hard, banks may not lend, and people may still refuse to borrow.
> 12. Quantitative easing is just very large-scale asset buying when normal rate cuts are exhausted.
> 13. Easy fiscal policy with tight monetary policy usually makes the public sector bigger as a share of the economy. Tight fiscal policy with easy monetary policy usually gives more room to the private sector.
> 14. Quick checks:
> - If the central bank directly changes expectations first and inflation later, you are thinking correctly.
> - If a country pegs its exchange rate, domestic rates must obey the peg when pressure hits.
> - If inflation rises because demand is booming, rate hikes make more sense than when inflation rises because oil got expensive.
> - If the question says "banks do not want to lend" or "people do not want to borrow," think limitation of monetary policy.

##### LEARNING OUTCOME 1

Describe the roles and objectives of central banks.

> [!ABSTRACT] MEMORISE
> - A central bank is the sole supplier of domestic currency in a fiat-money system.
> - It is usually the banker to the government, the bankers' bank, the lender of last resort, the regulator and supervisor of the payments system, and the conductor of monetary policy.
> - It may supervise banks directly, but not always by itself.
> - The big unifying objective is usually price stability, which in practice means controlling inflation and preserving confidence in money.

1. **A central bank matters because fiat money needs a trusted adult in the room.** What is fiat money: money that is not legally convertible into gold or some other commodity, but is accepted because the state declares it legal tender and people trust it. If nobody guards that trust, paper money can turn into fancy wallpaper very quickly.

2. **The central bank is the monopoly supplier of domestic currency.** What is monopoly supplier: the only institution allowed to create the base money of that country. Why is this used: because somebody has to control the foundation of the payment system, and you do not want twenty private issuers all playing god with the currency.

3. **Once money is fiat money, the central bank becomes the guardian of confidence.** What is confidence in money: the belief that the money in your pocket will still buy roughly sensible amounts of goods tomorrow. If that belief breaks, contracts, savings, wages, lending, and pricing all start wobbling.

4. **The central bank is usually the banker to the government and the bankers' bank.** What is banker to the government: the institution that holds government accounts and helps settle government payments. What is bankers' bank: the institution where commercial banks keep reserves and settle with one another. You can think of it as the plumbing layer under the visible banking system.

5. **The central bank is lender of last resort.** What is lender of last resort: the institution willing to lend emergency liquidity to banks when normal funding disappears. Why is this used: because a solvent bank can still die in a panic if everyone asks for cash at once.

> [!example] NORTHERN ROCK SHOWED YOU WHAT A LIQUIDITY PANIC LOOKS LIKE
> In 2007, Northern Rock in the United Kingdom had grown fast by leaning heavily on wholesale funding. Then the United States sub-prime mortgage mess poisoned trust across markets. Funding dried up, depositors saw the smoke, and queues formed outside branches. Around GBP 1 billion was reportedly withdrawn in one day, then another GBP 1 billion over the next two days. This is the point you should tattoo into your head: a bank can get crushed by a funding panic before the slow legal process of "figuring things out" catches up. That is why the lender-of-last-resort function exists.

6. **Liquidity is not the same thing as profitability.** What is liquidity: the ability to get cash quickly without having to dump assets at desperate prices. A bank can own assets that are fine in the long run and still blow up today because it cannot turn them into cash fast enough.

7. **The central bank usually oversees the payments system.** What is payments system: the network of rules, accounts, and settlement processes that let money move safely between banks and across the economy. Why is this used: because if payments stop clearing, modern commerce starts choking immediately.

8. **The central bank may supervise banks, but that role is not always organized the same way.** What is bank supervision: the monitoring of banks' safety, lending standards, capital, and risk-taking. Some countries give that job fully to the central bank, some split it, and some use a separate authority. The exam trap is simple: do not assume central bank automatically equals sole bank supervisor.

9. **The Bank of England story is useful because it shows structure matters.** Banking supervision was removed from the Bank of England in 1997 and given to the Financial Services Authority. After the crisis and the Northern Rock embarrassment, supervision moved back toward the Bank in 2013. The big lesson is not gossip. The lesson is that when supervision, crisis lending, and system oversight are split badly, nobody sees the full fire early enough.

10. **Most central banks also manage foreign-currency reserves and gold reserves.** What are foreign-currency reserves: external assets held to support intervention and confidence. Why is this used: because if the country needs to defend its currency or meet external obligations, reserves are the ammunition.

11. **Gold reserves still matter even in a post-gold-standard world.** This looks old-fashioned until markets get scared. Then people suddenly start acting as if metal has a personality. Large central-bank gold sales can move gold prices because the holdings are huge.

12. **The highest-profile role is monetary policy.** What is monetary policy: central-bank action aimed at influencing money, credit, interest rates, and financial conditions. Why is this used: because by changing financial conditions, the central bank tries to influence spending, output, and inflation without directly ordering people what to buy.

13. **The overarching objective is usually price stability.** What is price stability: an environment where inflation stays low and stable enough that households and firms do not lose faith in money as a store of value and unit of account. Price stability is the grown-up objective behind all the machinery.

14. **Why do central banks care so much about inflation?** Because high and unstable inflation quietly vandalizes everything. It distorts contracts, hurts savers, scrambles planning, and turns ordinary budgeting into survival math.

15. **Do not over-read the objective as "only inflation matters."** Some central banks also care about employment, output, financial stability, or broader confidence. But if the exam asks for the overarching objective in most cases, the safest anchor is still price stability.

##### LEARNING OUTCOME 2

Describe tools used to implement monetary policy and the monetary transmission mechanism, and explain the relationships between monetary policy and economic growth, inflation, interest, and exchange rates.

> [!ABSTRACT] MEMORISE
> - Three primary tools: open market operations, policy rate, reserve requirements.
> - Open market operations directly change bank reserves.
> - Policy rate influences short-term rates first, then broader financial conditions.
> - Reserve requirements mechanically affect banks' ability to create credit.
> - Transmission runs through rates, asset prices, exchange rate, and expectations.
> - The central bank influences inflation only indirectly. It can hit expectations and financial conditions more directly.

16. **Open market operations are the most direct way to change reserves.** What are open market operations: the purchase or sale of government bonds by the central bank with commercial banks or designated dealers. Why is this used: because changing reserves changes banks' capacity to expand credit.

17. **When the central bank buys government bonds, reserves rise.** That means banks now hold more liquid balances at the central bank. If banks then choose to lend and borrowers actually want loans, broad money can expand through the money creation process.

18. **When the central bank sells government bonds, reserves fall.** Banks pay with reserves, their lending room tightens, and money growth tends to cool. This is why open market sales are contractionary.

19. **The central bank can target either reserves or the interest rate on reserves.** The curriculum mentions both ideas because the operational framework differs by country. The exam point is not the plumbing detail. The exam point is that open market operations are how the central bank injects or drains liquidity.

20. **The policy rate is the clearest public signal of monetary stance.** What is policy rate: the official short-term interest rate the central bank uses to guide market rates and financial conditions. Why is this used: because once that anchor moves, banks, markets, and expectations all start repricing around it.

21. **Repo operations are just secured short-term lending dressed in bond clothes.** What is a repurchase agreement: a transaction where one side sells a bond now and agrees to buy it back later at a preset price. Why is this used: because it functions like a collateralized loan, so the central bank can lend short-term money while holding security against the loan.

22. **Commercial banks usually move their own lending rates when the policy rate moves.** They do not want to lend cheaply if their own funding or liquidity backstop just became more expensive. So the central bank changes one rate, and the banking system echoes it.

23. **In the United States, the names differ but the logic is the same.** The federal funds rate is the overnight interbank rate on reserves, and the discount rate is the rate for member banks borrowing directly from the Federal Reserve. Different labels, same macro idea: the central bank is steering short-term money conditions.

24. **Reserve requirements change lending power mechanically.** What are reserve requirements: minimum reserves that banks must hold against deposits. Why is this used: because if banks must park more funds as reserves, they have less room to create loans.

25. **Higher reserve requirements are contractionary.** Banks may need to stop or slow lending while rebuilding reserves. That is why frequent changes are disruptive and why many developed economies do not actively use this tool anymore.

26. **Reserve requirements still matter more in many emerging markets.** The curriculum is making a practical point here: not every system looks like London or New York. Some emerging-market central banks still use reserve requirements as a live policy lever.

27. **The transmission mechanism is the bridge from policy action to inflation.** What is transmission mechanism: the chain through which a central-bank move changes borrowing, wealth, demand, exchange rates, and eventually inflation. Why is this used: because a central bank does not reach into shops and rewrite price tags directly.

> [!info] WHY A RATE HIKE HITS THE ECONOMY EVEN THOUGH MOST PEOPLE DO NOT BORROW OVERNIGHT
> You might look at an overnight policy rate and think, "Why should a factory owner or a homebuyer care?" Fair question. The answer is that the overnight rate is like the first gear in the machine. If the central bank makes short-term money more expensive, banks raise lending rates, bond discount rates rise, stock and property valuations feel pressure, and the currency may strengthen. So even if you never borrow overnight in your life, the price of credit you actually face still gets dragged around by that first gear.

28. **The first transmission channel is short-term interest rates.** If the official rate rises, interbank borrowing rates and bank lending rates usually rise. That makes mortgages, business loans, and other credit more expensive, which tends to slow borrowing and spending.

29. **The second channel is asset prices.** Higher discount rates usually mean lower present values for bonds, equities, and capital projects. When asset prices cool, household wealth and business appetite often cool with them.

30. **The third channel is the exchange rate.** Higher domestic rates can attract capital, which can strengthen the currency. A stronger currency makes imports cheaper and exports less competitive, which tends to reduce inflation pressure and net external demand.

31. **The fourth channel is expectations.** What are expectations: beliefs held by households, firms, and investors about future inflation, rates, growth, and policy. Why is this used: because people do not wait for every economic effect to physically arrive; they react to what they think is coming.

32. **Expectations are usually affected before final inflation is affected.** This is one of the cleanest exam distinctions in the chapter. The central bank can hit expectations, market rates, and asset prices fairly directly. Inflation is the slow final result.

33. **The channels are interconnected, not independent.** A rate hike can push up borrowing costs, push down asset prices, strengthen the currency, and change future-rate expectations all at once. If an option in the exam treats them as separate disconnected pipes, be suspicious.

34. **Why does all this matter for growth?** Because lower borrowing, weaker wealth effects, and a stronger currency reduce demand. Lower demand usually means slower real growth in the short run and lower inflation pressure.

35. **Why does all this matter for inflation?** Because inflation is partly a demand story and partly an expectations story. If households and firms become less aggressive about spending, borrowing, pricing, and wage bargaining, inflation pressure can ease.

36. **Why does all this matter for exchange rates?** Because money chases return, credibility, and safety. If rates rise and policy looks serious, capital can flow in and strengthen the currency. If policy looks reckless, the opposite can happen.

##### LEARNING OUTCOME 3

Describe qualities of effective central banks; contrast their use of inflation, interest rate, and exchange rate targeting in expansionary or contractionary monetary policy; and describe the limitations of monetary policy.

> [!ABSTRACT] MEMORISE
> - Effective central banks need independence, credibility, and transparency.
> - Inflation targeting works best when the target is explicit, symmetric, medium-term, and forward-looking.
> - Targeting around 2 percent helps avoid both destabilizing inflation and accidental deflation.
> - Exchange-rate targeting can import low-inflation credibility, but domestic interest rates must adjust to defend the peg.
> - Expansionary policy means policy rate below neutral or liquidity injection. Contractionary policy means policy rate above neutral or liquidity drain.
> - Monetary policy is limited by uncertain transmission, uncertain neutral rate, liquidity traps, deflation, and the fact that banks and borrowers may not cooperate.

37. **A good central bank is not just smart; it is believable.** The curriculum keeps hammering three words: independence, credibility, and transparency. If those are weak, even good technical decisions lose force.

38. **Independence means the central bank is insulated from day-to-day election temptation.** What is independence: freedom from direct political pressure when making monetary-policy decisions. Why is this used: because politicians often love easy money right before voters show up, even when that creates inflation pain later.

39. **There are degrees of independence.** A government may still appoint the central-bank head. So complete political separation is more aspiration than reality. The useful exam distinction is between operational independence and target independence.

40. **Operational independence means the central bank chooses the tools.** What is operational independence: freedom to set interest rates and use instruments as it judges best to hit the assigned objective. The United Kingdom model is the classic case in the text.

41. **Target independence means the central bank also chooses the target itself.** What is target independence: freedom to define the inflation measure, the inflation level, and the horizon for achieving it. The European Central Bank is the curriculum's example.

42. **Credibility means people believe the central bank will actually do what it says.** What is credibility: the degree to which economic agents trust the institution to stick to its stated policy objective. Why is this used: because expected inflation often becomes partly self-fulfilling through wages, contracts, and pricing behavior.

43. **This is why debt-heavy governments are less credible inflation fighters.** If a government has huge debt, it has a quiet temptation to tolerate inflation because inflation reduces the real value of debt. Markets know this. So the speech may sound noble, but traders hear motive as well as words.

44. **Transparency means explaining the reaction function.** What is transparency: clearly communicating how the central bank reads money, credit, markets, labor conditions, growth, and prices, and how that reading shapes policy decisions. Why is this used: because mystery might look powerful in movies, but in monetary policy it weakens expectation management.

45. **New Zealand matters because it turned inflation targeting into an operating system.** In 1988, Finance Minister Roger Douglas announced that inflation should be brought down from around 6 percent to a 0 to 2 percent range. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 then gave the central bank operational independence to pursue that goal, while keeping accountability and communication obligations.

46. **Inflation targeting is not just "we like low inflation."** What is inflation targeting: a framework where the central bank publicly commits to a specific inflation objective or range and uses policy to steer future inflation toward it. Why is this used: because a clear target gives households, firms, and markets a focal point.

47. **Why do many developed central banks sit near a 2 percent target?** Because 0 percent is too close to deflation danger and 10 percent is obviously not price stability. Around 2 percent is seen as high enough to stay away from deflation and low enough to keep inflation from becoming wild.

48. **The target is forward-looking, not backward-looking.** Current inflation is history; it tells you what happened over the last twelve months. Policy moves today affect the economy with lags, so inflation targeters usually care about future inflation, often around two years ahead.

49. **An effective inflation-targeting framework normally has four features.** It has an independent and credible central bank, commitment to transparency, a broad information set for decisions, and a clear medium-term inflation target with explicit bounds.

50. **Japan is the chapter's warning that credibility cannot be wished into existence.** Japan fought deflation for decades. Some economists argued the Bank of Japan should just announce a positive inflation target and make people believe it. The problem was simple and brutal: if households and firms do not believe you can create inflation, the announcement has weak bite.

51. **The United States Federal Reserve was historically treated as an exception to the pure New Zealand model.** The broad lesson is enough for you: major central banks can differ in formal targeting design, but the same deeper issues of credibility, expectations, and communication still matter.

52. **Exchange-rate targeting is a different way to borrow discipline.** What is exchange-rate targeting: fixing the domestic currency at a specified value or band against another currency and defending that value with intervention. Why is this used: because a country with weaker anti-inflation credibility may import the inflation discipline of a more credible foreign central bank.

53. **The trade-off is harsh: if you target the exchange rate, domestic conditions must bend.** You do not get to say, "I want my peg and I also want completely free domestic interest-rate policy." When the peg is under pressure, the peg wins or the peg dies.

54. **If domestic inflation rises above the anchor country's inflation, the domestic currency tends to weaken.** To defend the peg, the central bank sells foreign reserves and buys domestic currency. That shrinks domestic money supply and pushes short-term interest rates up. In plain English: the peg forces you into tightening.

55. **If domestic inflation falls below the anchor country's inflation, the pressure flips.** The central bank may need to sell domestic currency, which increases domestic money supply and lowers interest rates. In plain English: the peg can force you into easing.

> [!example] BLACK WEDNESDAY IS THE KIND OF STORY EXAMS LOVE
> In 1992, the United Kingdom tried to keep sterling inside the European Exchange Rate Mechanism. Markets concluded the peg was not believable at that price. George Soros became the famous face of the trade against sterling, but the deeper point is bigger than one trader. When speculators think the central bank's promise is weaker than market reality, they attack the promise. Sterling crashed out of the mechanism and the United Kingdom eventually moved toward formal inflation targeting. That is your lesson: credibility is not a decoration on a peg. It is the peg.

56. **Thailand in 1997 tells the same story in a nastier emerging-market form.** The Thai central bank tried to defend the baht, ran through reserves, and when the market learned the cupboard was nearly empty, the devaluation triggered wider debt trouble because so much borrowing had been in foreign currency. Peg failure can become balance-sheet failure very fast.

57. **Dollarization is stronger than a simple peg.** What is dollarization: using the United States dollar as the functional domestic currency instead of keeping your own. Why is this used: because it removes exchange-rate uncertainty against the dollar, but the price is giving up a large part of monetary sovereignty.

58. **Expansionary monetary policy means easier money.** What is expansionary policy: a stance that increases liquidity or lowers borrowing costs to support demand and growth. You usually see it through lower policy rates, reserve injection, or large-scale asset purchases.

59. **Contractionary monetary policy means tighter money.** What is contractionary policy: a stance that reduces liquidity or raises borrowing costs to cool demand and inflation. You usually see it through higher policy rates, reserve drainage, or tighter bank constraints.

60. **The neutral rate is the benchmark for calling policy easy or tight.** What is neutral rate: the interest rate that neither stimulates nor slows the underlying economy. Why is this used: because "high" and "low" mean nothing unless you compare them with some equilibrium benchmark.

61. **The neutral rate has two pieces.** It is roughly the long-run real trend growth rate plus long-run expected inflation. This is elegant on paper and messy in real life.

> [!question]
> Problem: Trend real gross domestic product growth is 3 percent and the central bank's inflation target is 2 percent. Which policy rate is expansionary: 4 percent, 5 percent, or 6 percent?
>
> ---
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \text{Neutral rate} \approx 3\% + 2\% = 5\%
> $$
>
> A policy rate below neutral is expansionary, so **4 percent** is expansionary.
>
> Explanation: 5 percent is the rough "neither gas nor brake" rate. A 4 percent policy rate is below that benchmark, so it is pushing the economy, not restraining it.

62. **Do not treat the neutral rate as a measured laboratory fact.** The curriculum is explicit: estimating it is more art than science. Different economists can disagree, and then they will disagree about whether policy is actually tight or easy.

63. **You must identify the source of the inflation shock before worshipping rate hikes.** If inflation rises because demand is booming, tightening makes sense. If inflation rises because oil prices jumped, the case is trickier.

64. **Demand shock usually makes contractionary policy more defensible.** If consumers and firms are too confident, spending surges, and inflation starts climbing, higher rates can cool the heat.

65. **Supply shock is where central bankers can look foolish very quickly.** If inflation rises because energy prices spike, households are already being hit. Aggressive tightening can deepen the slump while doing little to solve the original supply problem.

66. **One limitation of monetary policy is transmission uncertainty.** Long-term rates may not cooperate with short-term policy moves if markets think the central bank is overdoing it or underdoing it. In that case, the policy message gets warped on the way through the system.

67. **Bond market vigilantes are basically market people who punish policy they distrust.** What are bond market vigilantes: investors who sell long-term government bonds when they think inflation control is slipping, pushing yields higher. Why is this used: because markets can impose discipline when they think the central bank or government is getting sloppy.

68. **A liquidity trap is the nightmare version of weak transmission.** What is liquidity trap: a situation where people are willing to hold extra money balances without pushing interest rates lower or increasing spending, so more money injection loses punch. Why is this used: because in deep fear or deflation, cash just sits there.

69. **Deflation is harder than inflation for conventional monetary policy.** What is deflation: a persistent fall in the general price level. Why is this used: because if prices keep falling, people delay spending, the real value of debt rises, and cutting nominal rates much below zero becomes difficult.

70. **Ben Bernanke became famous for the "helicopter money" line for a reason.** In a 2002 speech, he argued the Federal Reserve had enough instruments to prevent serious deflation, even joking about dropping money from a helicopter. The joke stuck because it captured the core idea: a central bank can create money, but that still does not guarantee behavior will change the way you want.

71. **Quantitative easing is the central bank saying, "Fine, the short rate is stuck, so I will attack the quantity side."** What is quantitative easing: large-scale central-bank purchases of government or other securities to inject reserves and loosen financial conditions when normal rate cuts are exhausted. Why is this used: because once short-term rates hit zero or near zero, the central bank needs another route.

72. **Quantitative easing looks like open market operations on steroids.** Same basic mechanism, much bigger scale, and sometimes a wider set of assets. The Bank of England bought government bonds. The Federal Reserve bought large amounts of mortgage-related securities and Treasuries.

73. **Do not romanticize quantitative easing.** Banks can just sit on excess reserves. Firms can refuse to borrow. Households can stay scared. Monetary policy can supply fuel, but it cannot force the engine to turn over.

74. **There is also a balance-sheet risk.** If a central bank buys risky assets and those assets later create losses, confidence in the institution can suffer. A central bank is special, but it is not magic.

> [!example] JAPAN IS THE CHAPTER'S BIG GHOST STORY
> Japan's Nikkei 225 hit 38,915 in 1989 and had fallen to 7,972 by the end of March 2003. After the asset bubble burst, wealth collapsed, confidence cracked, lending shrank, and deflation took hold. The Bank of Japan cut rates from 8 percent in 1990 to zero by 2001 and also used quantitative easing. But cheap money could not force confidence back into households, firms, and banks. This is the exact lesson the curriculum wants: monetary policy has power, but not absolute power.

75. **The deepest limitation is behavioral.** Central bankers do not control how much households and firms deposit in banks, and they do not fully control whether banks actually create credit. That means they do not perfectly control the money supply either.

76. **Developing economies face extra implementation problems.** If there is no liquid government bond market, open market operations are harder to run. If rapid financial innovation keeps changing what "money" means, policy becomes harder to calibrate. If credibility and institutional strength are weak, transmission gets noisier.

##### LEARNING OUTCOME 4

Explain the interaction of monetary and fiscal policy.

> [!ABSTRACT] MEMORISE
> - Monetary policy and fiscal policy both change aggregate demand, but through different channels.
> - Easy fiscal plus tight monetary policy raises the public share of the economy.
> - Tight fiscal plus easy monetary policy gives more room to the private sector.
> - Fiscal multipliers are larger when monetary policy accommodates the fiscal move.
> - Ricardian equivalence weakens fiscal stimulus if households fully expect future tax increases.
> - Quantitative easing that heavily buys government bonds can look like monetizing the deficit and can blur central-bank independence.

77. **Fiscal policy and monetary policy are teammates, but not twins.** What is fiscal policy: government decisions on spending, taxes, and transfers. Why is this used: because governments can push demand directly by spending more or taxing less. Monetary policy works more indirectly through credit, rates, and financial conditions.

78. **The mix matters because the two policies can reinforce or offset each other.** You do not get the same macro result from every combination. The whole point of this learning outcome is to stop you from treating "stimulus" as a one-word button.

79. **Easy fiscal policy with tight monetary policy usually raises the public share of output.** The government is spending more or taxing less, so public demand rises. But tighter money raises interest rates and squeezes private borrowing and spending. Result: public activity takes a larger share.

80. **Tight fiscal policy with easy monetary policy usually gives more room to the private sector.** Government demand retreats, but lower rates support private borrowing, investment, and spending. So the private share tends to rise.

81. **Easy fiscal and easy monetary policy together are strongly expansionary.** Both sides are pressing the accelerator. Aggregate demand rises, and if the economy is near capacity, inflation risk rises too.

82. **Tight fiscal and tight monetary policy together are strongly contractionary.** Government demand falls and private demand also gets hit by higher rates. That is the full double-brake combination.

83. **Why might policy makers prefer tight fiscal with easy money in some situations?** Because they may want to free resources for private investment and growth while keeping borrowing costs supportive. If the goal is a larger, more dynamic private sector, that mix can make strategic sense.

84. **Why might policy makers instead want easy fiscal policy?** Because sometimes the problem is poor infrastructure, weak public investment, or lack of training and human capital. In those cases, state spending may be doing a job the private sector will not do quickly enough.

85. **Fiscal policy is slower and politically messier than monetary policy.** Big projects take time to plan and execute. Tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular. Central banks with independence can often move faster than parliaments can.

86. **Both policies suffer from data fog.** Policy makers do not observe the economy in perfect real time. Data come late and get revised. So both monetary and fiscal actions are being aimed through a partially dirty windshield.

87. **Ricardian equivalence is the chapter's big warning label on fiscal stimulus.** What is Ricardian equivalence: the idea that people may save a current tax cut because they expect future tax increases to pay for it. Why is this used: because if households fully offset the stimulus by saving more, fiscal policy loses punch.

88. **If Ricardian equivalence holds strongly, policy makers may lean more on monetary tools.** That is the exam implication. If tax cuts are just viewed as delayed tax bills, then fiscal stimulus is less effective than it looks on paper.

89. **The International Monetary Fund model result is simple: fiscal multipliers are much larger when monetary policy accommodates them.** If the central bank immediately hikes rates against fiscal stimulus, part of the stimulus gets crowded out. If the central bank holds rates steady, the multiplier is much bigger.

90. **Government investment tends to hit harder than untargeted transfers when there is no monetary accommodation.** The curriculum's cited research says government spending can have a much bigger output effect than general social transfers in that case, because households may not treat the transfers as permanent.

91. **Targeted transfers to poorer households usually hit harder than untargeted transfers.** This is just economic common sense wearing formal clothes. People with tighter budgets are more likely to spend extra cash.

92. **Quantitative easing makes the policy-interaction debate more uncomfortable.** If the central bank buys large amounts of government bonds while fiscal deficits are large, critics start saying the central bank is effectively financing the government.

93. **That accusation is called monetization of the deficit.** What is monetization of the deficit: financing government deficits through central-bank money creation or heavy central-bank bond purchases. Why is this used: because if markets think the central bank is no longer independent, inflation expectations and long-term rates can react badly.

94. **Credibility and commitment still matter here.** Persistent high deficits can push up real interest rates and crowd out private investment. If people believe the government has no long-run discipline, the stimulus works less well because expected future inflation and rates rise.

95. **The deepest takeaway is not just "use both policies carefully."** It is this: the effect of one policy depends on what the other policy is doing. If you miss that interaction, you will misread both the chapter and the real world.

##### LOOK AT THESE BEFORE EXAM

96. **Central bank buys government bonds repeatedly. What stance is this?** Expansionary. Reserves rise, lending capacity can rise, and money growth can accelerate.

97. **Central bank raises reserve requirements. What stance is this?** Contractionary. Banks must hold more reserves and have less room to create loans.

98. **Central bank raises the policy rate. Which thing is hit more directly first: inflation or inflation expectations?** Inflation expectations. Inflation itself comes later through the transmission mechanism.

99. **Country pegs to the United States dollar and domestic inflation rises above United States inflation. What usually happens?** The central bank must defend the peg by buying domestic currency with foreign reserves, shrinking domestic money supply and pushing short-term rates up.

100. **Question says rates are near zero, reserves are abundant, but growth is still weak. What is the likely concept?** Limitation of monetary policy, often liquidity trap logic or broader transmission failure.

101. **Question says demand boom pushed inflation up. Best policy instinct?** Contractionary policy is more defensible because the source is excess demand.

102. **Question says oil shock pushed inflation up while growth is weakening. Best policy instinct?** Be careful. This is a supply shock, and aggressive tightening can worsen the downturn.

103. **Easy fiscal and tight monetary policy. Which sector grows as a share of the economy?** Public sector.

104. **Tight fiscal and easy monetary policy. Which sector gets more room?** Private sector.

105. **What makes an inflation target believable?** Independence, credibility, transparency, and a clear medium-term framework.
