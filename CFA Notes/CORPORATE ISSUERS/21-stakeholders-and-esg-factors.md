### MODULE 21.1: STAKEHOLDERS AND ESG FACTORS
###### LOS 21.a: Compare the financial claims and motivations of lenders and shareholders.
###### LOS 21.b: Describe a company's stakeholder groups and compare their interests.
###### LOS 21.c: Describe environmental, social, and governance factors of corporate issuers considered by investors.

> [!tip] LOOK AT THESE BEFORE EXAM
> - Debt is a finite, contractual, priority claim. Equity is a permanent, residual claim.
> - From the issuer's perspective, debt is riskier because missed payments can trigger default. From the investor's perspective, equity is riskier because equity gets paid last.
> - Debtholders care most about downside protection, covenant compliance, asset coverage, and timely payment. Shareholders care most about upside, growth, control, and long-run value creation.
> - Stakeholder theory is broader than shareholder theory. It forces you to think beyond only owners and include employees, customers, suppliers, lenders, governments, society, and the environment.
> - ESG factors matter only when they can hit operations, cash flows, risk, valuation, financing access, or reputation in a real way.
> - **Assets are sold for 100. Suppliers are owed 15, employees 10, taxes 5, debtholders 50. What is left for common shareholders?** Quick order: 100 - 15 - 10 - 5 - 50 = **20**. Equity gets the residual.
> - **A project has 50 downside loss and 200 upside gain. Debtholders still receive only their fixed claim, but shareholders capture the upside after debt. Which side usually pushes harder for risky upside?** Answer: **shareholders**.
> - **A pollution cleanup will cost 12 today but avoids an expected fine of 40 and reputational damage later. Is that ESG factor financially material?** Answer: **yes**, because it clearly changes future cash outflows and risk.
> - **A data-privacy failure is expected to cut annual revenue by 8%. Is that a social or governance side note, or part of valuation?** Answer: it is **part of valuation** because it directly hits cash flows.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> - Debt: promised interest and principal, finite maturity, priority claim, usually no voting rights.
> - Equity: no promised repayment, residual claim, voting rights, permanent capital.
> - Debt is usually cheaper than equity because debt investors take less risk and interest is often tax deductible.
> - Common stakeholder groups: shareholders, debtholders, board, managers, employees, customers, suppliers, governments, communities, and the environment.
> - Environmental, social, and governance factors can be risks, opportunities, or both.

1. Debt and equity are two different claims on the same business. What is a claim: a right to receive cash flows or assets from the company.
2. Debt is a financing source with a finite life. What is finite life: there is a promised maturity date when principal must be repaid.
3. Equity is permanent capital. Why is it called permanent: the company usually does not promise to repay shareholders on a fixed date.
4. Debtholders are promised interest and principal on stated dates, so their claim is contractual and ranked ahead of equity.
5. Shareholders get whatever is left after operating costs, taxes, supplier payments, wages, and debt payments. What is residual claim: you get the leftovers, not a fixed promised amount.
6. This is the core contrast you should never forget. Debt gets paid first but has limited upside. Equity gets paid last but has theoretically unlimited upside.
7. From the issuer's side, debt is dangerous because failure to pay can trigger distress, covenant breach, loss of control, or bankruptcy. From the investor's side, equity is more dangerous because equity absorbs the losses first.
8. Debt is usually a cheaper source of financing than equity. Why is debt cheaper: investors demand a lower return when their claim is senior and more protected.
9. Another reason debt can be attractive is that interest expense is often tax deductible, while dividends are generally not.

> [!question] QUICK CLAIM ORDER CHECK
> Problem:
> A company generates cash in a year, but it has to decide the payout order. Who gets paid before common shareholders?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
> Suppliers, employees, governments, and debtholders all stand ahead of common shareholders.
>
> Explanation:
> Equity is the residual claim. So if the business is under pressure, equity can get nothing even while debt still receives partial payment.

10. Debtholders and shareholders also have different motivations. Lenders want the company to stay solvent, stay disciplined, and avoid reckless risk-taking that threatens repayment.
11. Shareholders often prefer more upside, more growth, and sometimes more risk if the expected reward is attractive.
12. That difference creates conflict. If a company is already heavily indebted, shareholders may prefer a risky project because they enjoy much of the upside, while debtholders bear much of the downside.
13. This is why debt contracts often include covenants. What is a covenant: a rule in the debt agreement designed to protect lenders.
14. Common covenant logic is simple: do not let management quietly increase leverage, strip assets out, or pay huge cash distributions if that weakens the lenders' position.

15. A corporation has many stakeholder groups beyond debt and equity investors. What is a stakeholder: any group with a vested interest in the firm's success, conduct, or financial condition.
16. Shareholders provide risk capital and want long-term value creation.
17. Debtholders provide borrowed capital and want timely payment, asset protection, and predictable risk.
18. The board of directors oversees management and is supposed to protect the corporation and capital providers through monitoring, strategy oversight, and control.
19. Managers run daily operations and allocate capital, but they may have incentives that do not perfectly match those of investors.
20. Employees want stable jobs, fair pay, safe working conditions, and credible leadership.
21. Customers want quality, reliability, service, and a fair price.
22. Suppliers want repeat business, timely payment, and stable order patterns.
23. Governments want taxes paid and laws followed.
24. Communities and society care about employment, safety, environmental behavior, and whether the company creates or destroys local trust.
25. The environment is not a poetic add-on here. It matters because physical assets, regulations, pollution costs, climate exposure, and resource use can all hit cash flows.

26. Shareholder theory is the narrower lens. It asks management to maximize shareholder value, while still respecting the law and contracts.
27. Stakeholder theory is broader. It says long-term success often requires balancing the interests of several stakeholder groups, not only the shareholders.
28. The exam point is not that one theory magically erases the other. The real point is that stakeholder conflicts can change risk, valuation, and the cost of capital.

29. Environmental, social, and governance factors are non-financial in label but very financial in effect. What is an ESG factor: a business issue tied to environmental, social, or governance behavior that can materially affect performance, risk, or value.
30. Environmental factors include climate change, pollution, waste, water scarcity, biodiversity, and energy use.
31. Social factors include labor relations, customer safety, product quality, data privacy, community relations, diversity, and human rights.
32. Governance factors include board composition, audit committee quality, executive compensation, bribery controls, shareholder rights, lobbying, and whistleblower mechanisms.
33. An ESG factor is material only when it can significantly affect operations, business model durability, financial results, future cash flows, or the firm's risk profile.
34. Think of materiality like this: if the issue can change revenue, margins, capital spending, legal costs, financing access, or valuation multiples, analysts should care.
35. A coal plant facing carbon taxes and tighter regulation has an environmental issue that can directly alter future cash flows.
36. A bank with weak anti-money-laundering controls has a governance issue that can trigger fines, restrictions, reputational damage, and funding stress.
37. A consumer app with poor data protection has a social and governance issue because privacy failures can drive user exits, lawsuits, and political backlash.

> [!info] WHY ESG IS NOT "JUST ETHICS"
> If a company pollutes, mistreats workers, ignores cyber risk, or designs a board that cannot challenge management, the punishment often arrives through lower sales, higher costs, litigation, tighter regulation, or a higher required return from investors.
> That is why analysts increasingly quantify ESG issues instead of treating them as vague reputation talk.

38. Analysts should not treat ESG as a separate decorative section. The better habit is to ask where the ESG factor enters the valuation.
39. Does it hit growth? Does it hit margin? Does it raise required capital spending? Does it create stranded assets? Does it raise the cost of debt or equity?
40. If the answer is yes, the factor belongs in the core analysis, not in a side note.
