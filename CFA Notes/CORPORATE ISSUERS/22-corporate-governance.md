### MODULE 22.1: CORPORATE GOVERNANCE
###### LOS 22.a: Describe the principal-agent relationship and conflicts that may arise between stakeholder groups.
###### LOS 22.b: Describe corporate governance and mechanisms to manage stakeholder relationships and mitigate associated risks.
###### LOS 22.c: Describe potential risks of poor corporate governance and stakeholder management and benefits of effective corporate governance and stakeholder management.

> [!tip] LOOK AT THESE BEFORE EXAM
> - Principal hires agent. Agent usually knows more. That information gap creates agency costs.
> - Direct agency costs are cash monitoring costs. Indirect agency costs are lost value from bad behavior, weak incentives, or missed opportunities.
> - The board is supposed to monitor managers, set or approve major strategy, and protect capital providers through oversight.
> - Governance mechanisms include board independence, audit and controls, meetings and voting, compensation design, disclosure, covenants, and the market for corporate control.
> - Strong governance lowers perceived risk and often lowers the cost of debt and equity. Weak governance does the opposite.
> - **The company pays 2 million of audit fees to reduce reporting risk. Direct or indirect agency cost?** Answer: **direct**. It is a literal cash monitoring cost.
> - **Management rejects a positive-NPV project worth 15 million because they fear failure will hurt their jobs. Direct or indirect agency cost?** Answer: **indirect**. The value loss is the cost.
> - **Better governance improves the chance of a rating upgrade and lowers borrowing spread from 7% to 6%. Is that just governance theory, or a valuation input?** Answer: it is a **valuation input** because cost of debt falls.
> - **A hostile bidder crosses the poison-pill trigger and other shareholders can buy discounted shares. Does the bidder's ownership percentage usually rise or fall after dilution?** Answer: **fall**.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> - Corporate governance is the system of checks, balances, incentives, and control mechanisms that manages stakeholder relationships and reduces conflicts.
> - Shareholders are principals. Directors and managers are agents.
> - Agency costs rise when information asymmetry is high and accountability is weak.
> - Good governance improves control, trust, operating discipline, and financing access. Bad governance raises risk, waste, fraud exposure, and capital costs.

1. A principal-agent relationship exists when one party hires another to act on its behalf. What is principal: the party whose money or interest is at risk. What is agent: the party expected to act for that principal.
2. The relationship works only if the agent acts loyally and competently. The trouble is that the agent usually knows more than the principal about what is really happening.
3. That information gap is called information asymmetry. Why does it matter: the principal cannot perfectly observe effort, honesty, risk-taking, or hidden side deals.
4. In a corporation, shareholders are principals. They elect directors, and directors appoint managers. So you get a chain of agency relationships, not just one.
5. Conflicts arise when the agent's incentives differ from the principal's incentives. This creates agency costs.
6. Direct agency costs are literal cash costs paid to monitor or constrain behavior. Audit fees and control systems are the clean examples.
7. Indirect agency costs are value losses from bad decisions, weak effort, excessive caution, empire building, or self-protection.

8. The shareholder-board-manager relationship is the main exam battlefield.
9. Managers may put in insufficient effort, avoid hard restructuring choices, or spend too much time on prestige projects that make them look important rather than create value.
10. Managers may also choose the wrong risk level. Too many stock options can encourage reckless risk-taking. Too little equity-linked pay can make management too conservative.
11. Empire building is another classic conflict. What is empire building: management grows the company for size, status, or compensation instead of shareholder value.
12. Entrenchment is related but slightly different. What is entrenchment: managers use power, rules, or influence to protect their positions even when change would help owners.

13. Shareholders and debtholders also clash. Lenders want asset protection, stable policy, and timely payment. Shareholders may push for riskier projects, bigger payouts, or more leverage.
14. This is why lenders negotiate covenants and sometimes security interests. Their goal is to stop value from being shifted away from them after they lend the money.
15. Other stakeholder conflicts also matter. Employees may want stability while shareholders want cost cuts. Communities may want environmental restraint while management wants lower near-term costs.
16. Good governance does not mean every stakeholder gets everything they want. It means the firm has a credible system to manage those conflicts before they destroy value.

17. Corporate governance is the system of rules, controls, incentives, reporting, and oversight used to manage the stakeholder ecosystem.
18. A strong board is one of the first defenses. The board should have enough independence, skill, and authority to challenge management instead of acting as a ceremonial club.
19. An audit committee should be independent and financially literate. Why is this used: it protects reporting quality, internal control strength, and the credibility of financial statements.
20. Compensation design is another core mechanism. Good pay design tries to align management rewards with long-term value creation, not with superficial short-term optics.
21. Disclosure matters because outside investors cannot monitor what they cannot see. Better reporting reduces uncertainty and can reduce required returns.
22. General meetings and extraordinary general meetings are also governance mechanisms. An extraordinary general meeting is commonly called for major matters such as mergers, amendments, or other significant decisions that cannot wait for the regular annual meeting.
23. Shareholder voting rights, proxy contests, and the market for corporate control all matter because they create pressure on managers who underperform or misuse power.
24. A poison pill is a takeover defense that lets other shareholders buy discounted shares if a hostile bidder crosses a trigger threshold. The result is dilution of the bidder and a much more expensive takeover.
25. That does not make a poison pill automatically good. The exam point is that some defenses can protect shareholders from coercive bids, but abused defenses can also protect entrenched managers.
26. Creditors have their own governance tools too. Debt covenants, collateral, restructuring negotiations, and creditor committees all help lenders manage conflict and downside risk.

> [!info] ANALYST QUESTIONS TO ASK
> - Who really controls voting power?
> - Is the board actually independent and relevant to the firm's strategy?
> - Does management pay reward long-term value creation or just short-term optics?
> - Are minority shareholders protected?
> - Are there signs of weak controls, aggressive accounting, or related-party abuse?

27. Weak governance can damage a firm long before bankruptcy appears. Poor oversight can allow fraud, bribery, self-dealing, reckless acquisitions, or hidden leverage to build quietly.
28. Weak stakeholder management can also hurt operations. Suppliers pull back, employees disengage, customers leave, regulators intensify scrutiny, and creditors demand a higher spread.
29. Strong governance creates the opposite effect. It improves discipline, information quality, strategic accountability, and investor trust.
30. Better governance can increase the chance of a credit rating upgrade and reduce the cost of debt because lenders feel more protected.
31. It can also support a higher valuation multiple because equity investors attach a lower risk premium when reporting, incentives, and oversight look credible.
32. The best way to think about governance is simple: it is not a box-ticking exercise. It is the machinery that decides whether a corporation uses capital honestly and intelligently.
