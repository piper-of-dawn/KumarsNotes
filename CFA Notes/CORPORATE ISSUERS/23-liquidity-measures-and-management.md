### MODULE 23.1: LIQUIDITY MEASURES AND MANAGEMENT
###### LOS 23.a: Explain the cash conversion cycle and compare issuers' cash conversion cycles.
###### LOS 23.b: Explain liquidity and compare issuers' liquidity levels.
###### LOS 23.c: Describe issuers' objectives and compare methods for managing working capital and liquidity.

> [!tip] LOOK AT THESE BEFORE EXAM
> - **CCC = DIO + DSO - DPO**
> - Shorter cash conversion cycle usually means cash comes back faster, but "lower is better" is not a universal rule across every industry.
> - Negative cash conversion cycles can happen when customers pay before suppliers need to be paid.
> - Current ratio is broadest, quick ratio is stricter, cash ratio is most conservative.
> - Primary liquidity comes from operations, cash on hand, bank lines, and trade credit. Secondary liquidity usually means painful measures like asset sales, equity issuance, or restructuring.
> - Trade credit terms `a/b net c` mean take an `a%` discount if you pay in `b` days; otherwise pay full amount in `c` days.
> - **DIO = 40, DSO = 30, DPO = 25. Find CCC.** Quick algorithm: 40 + 30 - 25 = **45 days**.
> - **Cash = 20, marketable securities = 10, receivables = 30, current liabilities = 40. Find quick ratio.** Quick algorithm: (20 + 10 + 30) / 40 = **1.50**.
> - **Cash = 15, marketable securities = 5, current liabilities = 50. Find cash ratio.** Quick algorithm: (15 + 5) / 50 = **0.40**.
> - **Trade credit is 2/10 net 40. What is the rough annualized cost of skipping the discount?** Quick algorithm: about **24.8%**. This is expensive supplier financing.
> - **DPO is 50 while DIO + DSO is only 35. Find CCC.** Quick algorithm: 35 - 50 = **-15 days**. Negative CCC means customers effectively fund operations before suppliers are paid.

> [!abstract] MEMORISE
> $$
> \text{Cash conversion cycle} = \text{Days of inventory on hand} + \text{Days sales outstanding} - \text{Days payable outstanding}
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Short-term marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Short-term marketable securities}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{Cost of skipping trade discount} = \frac{\text{discount}}{1-\text{discount}} \times \frac{365}{\text{net days} - \text{discount days}}
> $$

1. Working capital is the world of short-term assets and short-term liabilities. Why does it matter: timing mismatches here can create a liquidity crisis even before the long-run business model breaks.
2. The cash conversion cycle measures how long cash is tied up from paying suppliers until cash is collected from customers.
3. Days of inventory on hand tells you how long inventory sits before being sold.
4. Days sales outstanding tells you how long customers take to pay.
5. Days payable outstanding tells you how long the issuer takes to pay suppliers.
6. Put together, the cash conversion cycle shows how many days the firm must fund its operating cycle with its own cash or financing.
7. A shorter cycle usually means faster cash recovery. A longer cycle means more cash is trapped in inventory and receivables.
8. But do not use one industry's cycle as a benchmark for another. Grocery retailers, luxury manufacturers, software firms, and hotels naturally operate with very different working-capital patterns.
9. A negative cash conversion cycle is possible. What does it mean: the firm receives cash from customers before it has to pay suppliers.
10. Businesses with strong brands, fast sales, and favorable supplier terms can pull this off. That is why some retailers and platforms can look amazingly cash-efficient.

11. Companies improve the cash conversion cycle by collecting faster, holding less inventory relative to sales, or delaying payment to suppliers.
12. That sounds straightforward, but each lever has trade-offs. Cutting inventory too hard can trigger stockouts. Pushing customers too hard can damage sales. Stretching suppliers too far can harm relationships or pricing.
13. Analysts also need to watch for cosmetic improvement. End-of-period factoring, delayed supplier payments, channel stuffing, or temporary inventory reduction can make the cycle look better without improving the business.

> [!question] TRADE CREDIT NUMERICAL
> Problem:
> Supplier terms are `2/10 net 40`. What is the approximate annualized cost of not taking the discount?
>
> ---
>
> Solution:
>
> $$
> \frac{0.02}{0.98} \times \frac{365}{30} \approx 24.8\%
> $$
>
> Explanation:
> Skipping the discount is like borrowing for 30 extra days at a very expensive annualized rate. If bank financing costs much less, taking the discount and borrowing from the bank is usually smarter.

14. Trade credit is supplier financing built into normal operations. What is supplier financing: the seller lets the buyer pay later rather than immediately.
15. It is a primary liquidity source because it is embedded in the operating cycle.
16. Forgoing an early-payment discount is not free. It is an implicit borrowing cost, and it can be shockingly high when annualized.

17. Liquidity is broader than the cash conversion cycle. What is liquidity: the firm's ability to meet near-term obligations without unacceptable disruption or value destruction.
18. The long-run primary source of liquidity for most issuers is cash flow from operations.
19. Other primary sources include cash on hand, marketable securities, committed bank borrowing capacity, and trade credit.
20. Secondary sources are the emergency options. These can include issuing equity, selling major assets, renegotiating contracts, and in extreme cases restructuring or bankruptcy protection.
21. Secondary sources are usually more expensive and often signal stress.

22. Drags and pulls on liquidity are a very clean curriculum distinction.
23. Drags are problems that slow receipts or reduce expected inflows. Obsolete inventory, weak collections, and uncollectible receivables are common drags.
24. Pulls are forces that accelerate outflows or cut off financing. Faster supplier payments, margin calls, reduced credit lines, and tighter trade-credit terms are common pulls.
25. If you remember one intuition, remember this: drags choke the money coming in, pulls force the money going out.

26. Liquidity ratios help compare issuers, but each ratio answers a slightly different question.
27. The current ratio includes all current assets, so it is the broadest measure.
28. The quick ratio removes inventory because inventory is less liquid and may require discounting or time to convert into cash.
29. The cash ratio is the harshest test because it asks whether cash and near-cash alone can cover current liabilities.
30. A higher ratio usually indicates more liquidity, but analysts still need to look at asset quality. A current ratio full of slow inventory is not the same as one full of cash.

31. Net working capital adjusts the picture by focusing on operating current assets and liabilities rather than simply adding every current item together.
32. Net working capital to sales is closely tied to the cash conversion cycle. A longer cycle usually means more working capital is needed for a given level of sales.

33. Firms choose a working-capital policy that can be conservative, moderate, or aggressive.
34. A conservative approach holds more cash, receivables, and inventory relative to sales and relies more on long-term funding. It is safer but costlier.
35. An aggressive approach minimizes those balances and leans more on short-term funding. It is cheaper in good times but riskier if credit dries up or demand becomes unstable.
36. A moderate approach sits in between and tries to balance cost and safety.
37. Early-growth firms, highly cyclical firms, or firms with fragile funding access may prefer more conservative liquidity management.
38. Stable firms with predictable collections and easy access to financing may safely run leaner.

> [!warning] EXAM TRAPS
> - Lower cash conversion cycle is not automatically better if it is achieved by starving inventory or hurting customer relationships.
> - Buying marketable securities with cash usually does not improve liquidity in a meaningful way and may reduce the strictest liquidity measures.
> - Supplier financing helps short-run liquidity but can be very expensive if prompt-payment discounts are forgone.
> - Compare liquidity ratios within the same business model and industry, not across unrelated models.
