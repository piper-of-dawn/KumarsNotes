### MODULE 6: HEDGE FUNDS

> [!ABSTRACT] LOS
> 1. Explain investment features of hedge funds and contrast them with other asset classes.
> 2. Describe investment forms and vehicles used in hedge fund investments.
> 3. Analyze sources of risk, return, and diversification among hedge fund investments.

> [!tip] LOOK AT THESE BEFORE EXAM
> 1. Hedge funds are ==not an asset class==. They are private investment vehicles defined mainly by approach.
> 2. The name is a trap. Modern hedge funds do not just hedge, and they are not restricted to equities.
> 3. Hedge funds are usually private, lightly regulated, flexible, fee-heavy, and often open only to institutional and accredited investors.
> 4. The main engine is not just market beta. Hedge funds try to earn strategy beta and manager alpha while often muting plain market exposure.
> 5. Relative value does ==not== mean “same asset class only.” It often compares related securities across capital structures, issuers, curves, and instruments.
> 6. Event-driven strategies usually like strong corporate activity. Merger waves, restructurings, and catalyst-rich environments help them.
> 7. Market-neutral means the manager tries to keep overall market beta close to zero. It does not mean “no risk.”
> 8. Managed futures, also called commodity trading advisers, are often the low-correlation answer because they trade liquid futures and can do well in strong trends.
> 9. Fund of hedge funds gives smaller investors diversification and manager selection help, but adds another fee layer.
> 10. Hedge fund indexes are messy because reporting is voluntary. Self-reporting, survivorship bias, and backfill bias can all make performance look better than reality.
> 11. Lockup, notice period, liquidity gate, and redemption fee all exist because hedge fund positions may take time to unwind.
> 12. **A hedge fund is private, flexible, fee-heavy, and may use leverage and short selling. Is that a new asset class?** No. It is a different investment vehicle and strategy set, not a separate asset class.
> 13. **Which broad strategy usually has the lowest market correlation: short bias, fundamental growth, or market neutral?** Market neutral is usually built to keep market beta near zero.
> 14. **Why can fund-of-funds returns disappoint even when underlying managers are decent?** Double fees. One manager charges fees, then the fund-of-funds manager charges another layer.
> 15. Do not call hedge funds a separate asset class. ==They are strategy-driven vehicles.==
> 16. Do not assume hedge funds must hold exotic assets. They can hold ordinary public securities and still be hedge funds.
> 17. Do not say hedge funds are offered to the public like mutual funds or exchange-traded funds.
> 18. Do not say hedge funds are more restricted than mutual funds in what they can trade. The opposite is usually closer to the truth.
> 19. Do not say relative value always stays within one asset class. It often crosses instruments and capital-structure layers.
> 20. Do not say event-driven strategies thrive mainly in calm markets with little corporate activity.
> 21. Do not think market neutral means no risk. It mainly means low market beta.
> 22. Do not forget that leverage is common because many hedge fund spreads are too small to be exciting on an unlevered basis.
> 23. Do not forget the extra fee layer in a fund of hedge funds.
> 24. Do not trust hedge fund indexes blindly without checking self-reporting, survivorship bias, and backfill bias.
> 25. Do not mix hedge funds with private equity on funding pattern: hedge funds are usually funded upfront, private equity is usually committed upfront and funded later through capital calls.
> 26. Do not forget that managed futures, also called commodity trading advisers, are often the better answer when the question wants liquid, trend-following, low-correlation hedge fund exposure.

#### WHAT A HEDGE FUND REALLY IS

1. **A hedge fund is a private pooled investment vehicle defined more by investment approach than by what it holds**; what is pooled investment vehicle: many investors put money into one common fund; why this matters: a hedge fund can hold ordinary stocks or bonds and still be a hedge fund because the playbook is what makes it different.

2. **The word “hedge” is partly historical and partly misleading**; hedge funds originally used offsetting long and short positions to protect against big stock-market moves, but modern hedge funds do far more than that.

3. **Modern hedge funds can invest in equities, fixed income, derivatives, foreign exchange, private capital, and real assets**; what is derivatives: contracts whose value comes from something else, such as a stock, bond, rate, or commodity; why derivatives are used: they can create leverage, hedging, or directional exposure more efficiently than cash securities.

4. **The real distinguishing feature is the manager’s flexibility**; hedge funds may use short selling, borrowing, leverage through derivatives, concentrated positions, and niche strategies that many mutual funds cannot or do not use.

5. **The objective is usually high absolute return or strong risk-adjusted return, not simple benchmark hugging**; what is absolute return: trying to make money regardless of whether the broad market is up or down; why this matters: many hedge funds care less about copying an index and more about producing attractive standalone performance.

6. **Hedge funds often appeal to investors because returns can have lower correlation with traditional assets**; what is correlation: how strongly two return series move together; why lower correlation helps: it can improve diversification inside a broader portfolio.

7. **Do not make the beginner mistake of thinking hedge funds always reduce risk at the position level**; many hedge fund tools, such as leverage and short selling, can amplify risk in individual trades even if the total portfolio is designed more carefully.

8. **A hedge fund manager often gets much more discretion than a mutual fund manager**; mutual funds are more heavily regulated and offer broader public access, while hedge funds are typically private and lightly regulated.

9. **Hedge funds are usually available only to institutional and accredited investors**; what is accredited investor: an investor allowed to access certain private offerings because of income, wealth, or sophistication requirements.

> [!warning] HAMMER THIS INTO YOUR HEAD
> Hedge funds are not “alternative” because they only hold weird assets.
>
> They are alternative because the structure, fee design, liquidity terms, leverage, shorting, and trading philosophy can make the risk-return profile very different from just owning the underlying securities directly.

#### HOW HEDGE FUNDS DIFFER FROM OTHER INVESTMENTS

10. **Hedge funds differ from mutual funds because mutual funds are public, more regulated, more liquid, and usually more constrained**; hedge funds are private, more flexible, and typically have tougher redemption terms.

11. **Hedge funds differ from exchange-traded funds because exchange-traded funds are public exchange-traded vehicles, usually low fee, highly transparent, and benchmark-oriented**; hedge funds are private partnership-style vehicles with broader discretion.

12. **Hedge funds differ from private equity because hedge funds usually have shorter holding periods and invest in more liquid instruments**; private equity usually makes long-term, control-oriented investments in private operating companies.

13. **Private equity is funded over time through capital calls, while hedge funds are usually funded upfront**; this is one of the easiest exam comparisons to mix up.

14. **Hedge funds often charge performance-based fees, and many also require or expect manager co-investment**; why manager co-investment matters: it can improve alignment between manager and investor.

15. **Some hedge funds use a high-water mark so the manager cannot charge performance fees merely for recovering old losses**; what is high-water mark: the previous highest net asset value level that must be exceeded before a new incentive fee can be charged.

16. **The investment universe is large**; hedge funds may focus on one asset class, mix many asset classes, or even move across regions depending on opportunity.

17. **Not every hedge fund uses leverage or short selling all the time**; some do, some do not, and the curriculum is careful about that point.

#### THE FIVE BROAD HEDGE FUND STRATEGY FAMILIES

18. **The curriculum groups hedge funds into five broad families**: equity hedge, event-driven, relative value, opportunistic, and multi-manager.

19. **This classification matters because it helps with benchmark choice, portfolio construction, and understanding where the return is supposed to come from**.

#### EQUITY HEDGE STRATEGIES

20. **Equity hedge strategies focus on public equity markets using long and short positions**; what is long position: you profit if the asset price rises; what is short position: you profit if the asset price falls.

21. **Most equity hedge strategies are bottom-up**; what is bottom-up: start from company-level analysis first, then industry, then market. A top-down approach does the reverse by starting from macro or market views.

22. **Fundamental long/short means buying undervalued companies and shorting overvalued ones**; why this is used: the manager wants alpha from valuation correction rather than simple market direction.

23. **Most long/short equity funds keep a long bias**; what is long bias: the portfolio still has net positive exposure to equity markets even though short positions are used too.

24. **Fundamental growth means buying companies expected to deliver strong growth and shorting companies expected to suffer weak or negative growth**; the spread between growth and weak-business expectations drives the trade.

25. **Fundamental value means buying undervalued and unloved companies where a turnaround could improve revenue, cash flow, and valuation**.

26. **Short bias means the fund is primarily betting against overvalued companies**; managers often use fundamental, technical, and quantitative tools and may even look for hidden accounting or business problems.

27. **Short-bias funds can help in market stress but have struggled over long bull-market periods**; this makes intuitive sense because it is hard to stay heavily bearish during decades when equities mostly grind upward.

28. **Market-neutral means long undervalued securities and short overvalued securities while keeping net market beta near zero**; what is beta here: sensitivity to the overall market.

29. **Market-neutral does not mean return-free or risk-free**; it means the manager tries to remove plain market direction so the trade depends more on security selection.

30. **Market-neutral often needs leverage to make the return meaningful**; if you strip out a lot of market exposure, the raw spread may be small, so leverage is often used to amplify it.

> [!question] MARKET NEUTRAL INTUITION
>
> Problem: Why can a market-neutral fund still be risky if its market beta is close to zero?
>
> ---
>
> Solution: Because the manager is still making valuation, spread, timing, leverage, and liquidity bets.
>
> Explanation: Zero or near-zero beta only says the fund is trying not to ride the whole market up or down. It does not mean the long positions are definitely right, the short positions are definitely wrong, or leverage will behave politely during volatility.

#### EVENT-DRIVEN STRATEGIES

31. **Event-driven strategies try to profit from corporate events that change valuations**; what is corporate event: a merger, restructuring, special distribution, spin-off, bankruptcy, or another catalyst that changes the company’s financial path.

32. **These strategies are usually bottom-up and often use both long and short positions in equity, preferred stock, debt, and options**.

33. **Merger arbitrage usually means buying the target company and shorting the acquirer after a deal is announced**; the manager wants to earn the spread between the target’s current price and the announced takeover value.

34. **That spread exists because the deal may fail, get delayed, or get repriced**; legal, regulatory, financing, or strategic problems can kill the transaction.

35. **Shorting the acquirer also expresses the risk that the buyer may be overpaying**.

36. **Merger arbitrage often uses leverage because the raw spread is usually modest**; that helps returns when the deal closes cleanly and hurts badly when the deal breaks.

37. **Distressed or restructuring strategies focus on companies near bankruptcy or deep stress**; the manager may buy senior debt trading far below par, expecting recovery through restructuring or liquidation.

38. **Some distressed funds buy a fulcrum security**; what is fulcrum security: the claim in the capital structure most likely to convert into new equity during restructuring.

39. **Special situations focus on catalyst-rich cases such as repurchases, special distributions, rescue finance, spin-offs, and issuance-related opportunities**.

40. **Activist strategies involve building enough equity ownership to push for change**; the fund may seek board influence, restructuring, asset sales, management changes, or capital distributions.

41. **Activist hedge funds are different from private equity because they mainly operate in public equity markets rather than buying full control of private companies**.

42. **Event-driven strategies tend to be long biased overall, though merger arbitrage is usually the least long-biased among them**.

43. **They tend to like strong corporate activity and healthier economic environments, not sleepy markets with no deal flow**.

> [!example]
> Carl Icahn’s involvement with Hertz is the clean activism example from the curriculum. He built a very large stake, pushed for changes in board and management, and tried to create value through operational and strategic direction. The story also shows the painful side of activism: even a powerful activist can still lose badly if industry stress and financing weakness crush the company before the thesis pays off.

> [!question] MERGER ARBITRAGE CORE LOGIC
>
> Problem: A target stock trades below the announced takeover price right after a deal is announced. Why does that gap usually exist?
>
> ---
>
> Solution: Because the market is pricing the risk that the transaction may be delayed, blocked, repriced, or abandoned.
>
> Explanation: If the deal were guaranteed, the target would jump straight to the final consideration value. The remaining spread is the market charging for deal uncertainty.

#### RELATIVE VALUE STRATEGIES

44. **Relative value strategies try to profit from pricing discrepancies between related securities**; what is pricing discrepancy: a temporary mismatch in value between instruments whose prices should move more closely together.

45. **The expectation is that the short-term mispricing will converge toward a more normal relationship over time**.

46. **Convertible bond arbitrage looks for mispricing between a convertible bond and its component parts**; what is convertible bond: a bond that can be converted into the issuer’s common equity under stated terms.

47. **A common version is to buy the convertible bond and short some of the issuer’s common stock**; the manager is trying to isolate mispricing between the bond-plus-option package and the equity.

48. **This strategy can be sensitive to bankruptcy and credit risk even if it looks clever and market-neutral on paper**.

49. **Fixed-income relative value can compare sovereign versus corporate spreads, different issuers, different points on the same issuer’s yield curve, or even different parts of the same capital structure**.

50. **Asset-backed, mortgage-backed, and high-yield relative value strategies compare higher-yielding securities and their derivatives, often looking for security or quality mispricing**.

51. **Multi-strategy relative value means the manager is not married to one narrow trade type**; capital can move across strategies and instruments as opportunities change.

52. **The exam trap here is simple: relative value does not mean “one asset class only”**.

#### OPPORTUNISTIC STRATEGIES

53. **Opportunistic strategies are usually more top-down and macro-sensitive**.

54. **Macro strategies trade expected movements in big economic variables**; what is macro here: broad economic forces such as interest rates, currencies, inflation, commodity trends, and policy shifts.

55. **Global macro funds may trade fixed income, equities, currencies, and commodities using both long and short positions**.

56. **Macro strategies usually like volatile environments because big policy or growth shifts create directional opportunities**.

57. **Managed futures funds are actively managed directional funds focused mainly on futures markets**.

58. **Managed futures funds are also called commodity trading advisers**; what is commodity trading adviser: the older label for managers that historically focused on commodity futures, though many now trade much more than commodities.

59. **Commodity trading advisers often use models built around trend and momentum across different horizons**.

60. **They can diversify portfolios especially during strong trends and extended market stress**.

61. **They can also suffer in mean-reverting markets where apparent breakouts keep failing**.

62. **Their diversification benefit may be weaker than before because many now trade more financial products such as stock-index and bond futures**.

> [!info] WHY MANAGED FUTURES CAN DIVERSIFY
> A normal long-only stock fund usually suffers when equities fall hard. A managed futures fund can go long or short futures and does not need to fall in love with one direction. If the manager catches a strong downtrend early, falling markets can actually help the strategy rather than destroy it.
>
> That is why managed futures often show up as the answer when the exam wants low correlation, liquid markets, and a strategy that can work during stress.

#### MULTI-MANAGER STRATEGIES

63. **The multi-manager category is mainly the fund of hedge funds idea in this reading**.

64. **A fund of hedge funds pools investor money and allocates it across multiple underlying hedge funds**.

65. **The main appeal is diversification across managers, strategies, regions, and styles**.

66. **This structure also helps smaller investors who do not have the resources to source, analyze, and monitor individual hedge funds directly**.

67. **The main pain point is double fees**; one layer is charged by the underlying hedge funds and another by the fund-of-funds manager.

68. **Fund of hedge funds can also offer lower minimums, somewhat shorter lockups, and better exit liquidity than direct investment in some underlying funds**.

69. **Even with the extra fee burden, investors may still choose them because the manager can offer due diligence, diversification, and access to closed or hard-to-reach hedge funds**.

#### HEDGE FUND INVESTMENT FORMS

70. **A common hedge fund legal setup is a private limited partnership or limited liability company**; what is general partner: the manager or controlling party that runs the fund; what is limited partner: the investor providing capital but not running day-to-day decisions.

71. **The fund documents define the legal and operational relationship between manager and investor**; these include the private placement memorandum, partnership agreement, or articles of incorporation.

72. **Many hedge funds are set up as perpetual legal entities even though, in reality, funds close and liquidate regularly**.

73. **The master-feeder structure is a classic direct hedge fund form**; what is feeder fund: a fund that gathers investor capital and sends it into another fund; what is master fund: the central pool that actually runs the investment strategy.

74. **The main reason for the master-feeder structure is tax and operational efficiency across different investor types and jurisdictions**.

75. **An onshore feeder and an offshore feeder can both send money into one master fund, letting the manager run one investment pool instead of many separate copies**.

76. **Side letters are custom agreements for specific investors**; why side letters are used: to address legal, regulatory, tax, operational, or reporting needs without rewriting the full fund documents for everyone.

77. **For larger investors, a fund of one or a separately managed account can offer more control**.

78. **A separately managed account means the investor owns the account or assets more directly while delegating day-to-day management to the hedge fund manager**.

79. **Separately managed accounts offer more customization, transparency, control, and often better liquidity**.

80. **The trade-off is operational complexity and higher governance demands**.

81. **Separately managed accounts can also create incentive problems because the manager may not be personally invested in that specific pool and may allocate the most liquid trades there**.

82. **Indirect hedge fund investment is designed to make hedge fund exposure easier for smaller institutions or larger retail-style investors who want help with manager selection and diversification**.

83. **Fund of hedge funds is the main indirect structure in the curriculum**.

84. **Hedge fund replication exchange-traded products are another indirect route**; these try to mimic hedge fund return styles without actually investing in hedge funds directly.

85. **Replication exchange-traded products usually use liquid publicly traded debt, equity, and derivatives to imitate hedge fund-style return patterns**.

86. **They usually offer lower fees, better liquidity, and more transparency than actual hedge funds, but they usually fall short of pure hedge fund flexibility and return potential**.

#### LIQUIDITY, FEES, AND INVESTOR TERMS

87. **Hedge funds often have liquidity constraints because positions may be concentrated, leveraged, or hard to unwind quickly**.

88. **A lockup period is the minimum period before an investor can redeem**.

89. **A notice period is advance warning required before redemption**.

90. **A liquidity gate partially limits withdrawals so the manager can unwind positions over more time**.

91. **A redemption fee may be charged, often for the benefit of the fund rather than the manager, to protect remaining investors from the costs of someone leaving early**.

92. **A soft lockup means there is still an early exit path, but it is expensive**.

93. **Hedge fund fee structures are relatively high**; the classic phrase is “two and twenty,” meaning a management fee plus an incentive fee.

94. **The curriculum also notes newer pressure-driven structures such as “one or thirty”**; here the manager receives the greater of a smaller management fee or a larger incentive fee tied to outperformance.

95. **Fees exist partly because hedge funds are less transparent and more manager-dependent**; investors are effectively paying for skill, flexibility, sourcing, and risk management.

> [!question] FUND OF HEDGE FUNDS FEE DRAG
>
> Problem: Why can a fund of hedge funds underperform a direct hedge fund investment even when the underlying managers are solid?
>
> ---
>
> Solution: Because the investor pays fees at the underlying hedge fund level and then pays another layer at the fund-of-funds level.
>
> Explanation: The extra diversification and due-diligence service are real, but they are not free. If the gross return is decent but not spectacular, double fees can eat a painful share of what the investor keeps.

#### SOURCES OF HEDGE FUND RETURN

96. **Traditional portfolios often earn most of their return from market beta**; hedge funds try harder to shift the return mix toward strategy beta and alpha.

97. **Market beta means broad return you could largely capture with market index exposure**.

98. **Strategy beta means the return pattern associated with a specific hedge fund style applied systematically across markets**.

99. **Alpha means manager-specific return from selection, timing, execution, or other skill**.

100. **The curriculum’s main idea is that hedge funds try to reduce ordinary market exposure and make more of the return come from idiosyncratic opportunities**; what is idiosyncratic: specific to the security, event, spread, or manager skill rather than the whole market.

101. **This does not mean market risk disappears**; it means the manager is trying to rely less on it than a standard long-only fund does.

102. **Common drivers of hedge fund returns include market inefficiencies, volatility, spread behavior, catalyst resolution, and manager skill in finding and trading these opportunities**.

103. **Fees reduce the return actually reaching the investor**; this matters because headline strategy returns and investor net returns can diverge sharply.

#### BENCHMARKING AND INDEX BIASES

104. **Benchmarking hedge funds is hard because reporting is limited, strategies differ, and positions may be illiquid or opaque**.

105. **Most hedge fund indexes are built from voluntarily reported manager data**.

106. **That creates self-reporting problems because non-reporters are invisible and poor performers may have less reason to keep showing up**.

107. **Selection bias can appear because funds are grouped into strategy buckets inconsistently**.

108. **Survivorship bias happens when failed or stopped-reporting funds disappear from the index, making the average look healthier than reality**.

109. **Backfill bias happens when a successful fund starts reporting later and then adds its earlier good returns into the database**.

110. **Equal weighting can also distort reality because many hedge fund indexes do not weight by assets under management**.

111. **The curriculum treats fund-of-funds composite indexes as a somewhat fairer way to compare hedge fund performance because they are more investable and can reduce some distortions**.

> [!danger] DO NOT MAKE THIS MISTAKE
> A “good-looking” hedge fund index does not automatically prove hedge funds were amazing.
>
> First ask who reported.
>
> Then ask who died and disappeared.
>
> Then ask whether good old returns were backfilled after success arrived.

#### RISK, RETURN, AND DIVERSIFICATION

112. **Hedge fund risk-return analysis is more complicated than for plain stock or bond funds because of leverage, illiquidity, strategy complexity, and limited disclosure**.

113. **Historically, hedge funds have often been valued for diversification more than for raw upside alone**.

114. **Market-neutral, relative value, and event-driven strategies can be especially useful during equity downturns or when stock correlations fall**.

115. **Their performance tends to weaken when correlations are high and equities rise together in a uniform way, because relative opportunities become scarcer**.

116. **Managed futures can help during strong trends and extended stress, especially when other strategies struggle**.

117. **The diversification case for hedge funds became more visible after the dot-com unwind and then evolved again after the 2008 crisis**.

118. **After 2008, investors demanded much more due diligence, transparency, leverage discipline, and operational control**.

119. **The reading is clear that hedge funds still have a role in institutional portfolios mainly because of diversification and risk-adjusted return potential, not because they always beat equities in every period**.

120. **Adding hedge funds to a traditional portfolio can reduce overall standard deviation and improve the Sharpe ratio, though the benefit varies over time**.

121. **The diversification story is not permanent magic**; later periods showed higher correlation with equities and weaker absolute return, which made some allocations less attractive.

122. **The manager due-diligence burden is high**; investors must assess strategy, transparency, liquidity, reporting, manager experience, incentives, and operational discipline.

123. **Fraud risk matters**; light regulation and large minimum investments can create room for abuse, which is why operational due diligence is not optional.

> [!example]
> The post-2008 world forced hedge fund investors to grow up. The crisis, along with fraud episodes such as Bernie Madoff, made institutions much more demanding about transparency, leverage control, operational systems, and proof that the manager actually knows what risks are being taken. The lesson is simple: a clever strategy is not enough if the structure around it is weak.

> [!tip] QUICK CHECKS
> 1. If the question asks “vehicle or asset class?”, your reflex answer should be “vehicle and strategy set.”
> 2. If the question asks “why private?”, think flexibility, lighter regulation, negotiated terms, and fee-heavy active management.
> 3. If the question asks “which strategy likes corporate catalysts?”, think event driven.
> 4. If the question asks “which strategy hunts mispricing between related securities?”, think relative value.
> 5. If the question asks “which strategy often helps during strong trends and stress?”, think managed futures or commodity trading advisers.
