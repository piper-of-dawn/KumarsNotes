# Evaluating Quality of Financial Reports
*CFA Level II · Learning Module 5 · Five-minute revision*

## 1. Reporting quality and accounting manipulation

**1. Earnings smoothing can be perfectly GAAP-compliant.**

A company deliberately understates profits during good years and overstates them during bad years. You classified this as non-GAAP accounting.

But the question explicitly said the company complied with GAAP. Accounting standards permit estimates and judgment, which management can exploit to smooth earnings.

**Remember:** GAAP compliance doesn't guarantee faithful reporting. The classification was *GAAP-compliant earnings management*.

**2. Repeated asset-disposal losses tell you something about earlier years.**

A company repeatedly sells equipment at substantial losses. You identified poor earnings quality but missed poor reporting quality.

Suppose equipment is worth €60 but remains on the books at €100. Selling it exposes a €40 loss. ==The more important question is why depreciation hadn't already reduced its carrying value.== Management may have understated depreciation for years, inflating operating earnings.

**Both qualities suffer:** The historical financial statements were biased, and apparently sustainable operating earnings were overstated.

**3. Shorter depreciation lives aren't aggressive accounting.**

A company reduces an asset's estimated useful life. You treated this as the warning sign instead of a persistent increase in operating margins.

Shorter life → higher annual depreciation → lower current earnings.

That's conservative, not aggressive. Extending useful lives would have the opposite effect.

**Exam rule:** A suspicious trend in operating margins warrants investigation; shorter depreciation lives generally reduce reported profits.

**4. Early revenue recognition isn't always visible in receivables.**

A company previously recognized subscription revenue over three years. It now recognizes most of it in year one.

You focused on days sales outstanding, even though DSO barely changed.

The actual change was in **unearned revenue**, a liability representing payments received before services are earned.

Accelerating recognition reduces this liability and increases current revenue without requiring new cash.

**Look at accounting policy changes, not just ratios.**

## 2. Cash flow: genuine cash, misleading conclusions

**5. Strong operating cash flow can be manufactured temporarily.**

A company reports CFO exceeding operating income, rising payables, and falling receivables and inventory. You considered both observations suspicious.

But CFO exceeding operating income alone isn't necessarily bad.

The real warning is the working-capital combination:

- Pay suppliers later → cash retained.
- Collect or sell receivables → cash increases.
- Reduce inventory → less cash tied up.

CFO improves without necessarily improving profitability.

**Ask where the cash came from, not merely how much appeared.**

**6. A widening earnings–CFO gap is a reporting-quality warning.**

You identified the company with stable, low-volatility CFO as potentially having poor reporting quality, rather than the company whose earnings increasingly diverged from CFO.

Stable, sustainable operating cash flows are normally favorable.

When earnings continually grow faster than CFO, increasingly large accruals may be supporting reported profit.

**CFA interpretation:** ==The widening gap suggests possible earnings manipulation and therefore potentially poor *reporting quality*.== It's a warning, not proof.

## 3. Balance-sheet adjustments

**7. Equity-method accounting can artificially elevate profit margins.**

A parent company owns 49% of a profitable business. Its income statement includes its share of the investee's profit but excludes the investee's revenue.

You expected return on sales to increase after adjusting for comparability.

Imagine reported profit of €20 and sales of €100: margin = 20%. Add €100 of investee sales without changing the profit numerator: margin falls to 10%.

**The denominator was missing.** Including the relevant investee sales reduces the calculated margin.

**8. Constructive capitalization never uses undiscounted payments on one side.**

A take-or-pay contract requires €10 million of future payments, worth €8 million today.

You added €8 million to assets, €10 million to liabilities, and removed €2 million from equity.

But €2 million isn't an immediate loss. It's the difference between future nominal payments and present value.

The analytical adjustment is:

**Assets +€8m | Liabilities +€8m | Equity unchanged**

You're recognizing the present value of contractual benefits and obligations to reveal hidden leverage.

## 4. Scores, thresholds, and interpretation

**9. Beneish: Less negative means more suspicious.**

Three companies have M-Scores of −1.54, −2.60 and −1.86. You chose −1.86 as the highest manipulation risk.

But **−1.54 is the highest number**.

A higher M-Score indicates greater estimated manipulation probability.

| M-Score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| −2.60 | Lower estimated risk |
| −1.86 | Below the warning cutoff |
| −1.54 | Flagged as potentially manipulative |

**Cutoff: −1.78. Higher is worse.**

**10. Improving Altman scores don't necessarily resolve uncertainty.**

A company's Z-Score improves from 1.82 to 2.14. You concluded that bankruptcy probability had decreased.

The direction is favorable, but both values are in the **grey zone**.

| Altman Z-Score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Below 1.81 | Financial distress |
| 1.81–3.00 | Uncertain |
| Above 3.00 | Relatively safer |

The exam wanted *bankruptcy risk remains uncertain*.

**Never replace a threshold-based classification with a directional inference.**

## 5. Corporate governance

**11. Propping can be inappropriate even though money enters the public company.**

You identified tunneling alone as an inappropriate related-party transfer.

Tunneling takes resources **out** of a public company and transfers them to a manager-owned private entity.

Propping moves resources **into** the public company from the manager-owned entity.

Why would management do that? To keep a struggling company alive, preserve control, or retain the opportunity to extract private benefits later.

**Both can involve inappropriate transfers.** Direction differs; neither is necessarily evidence of generosity.

## 6. Other traps worth remembering

| Concept | Five-second memory |
|---|---|
| **Reporting vs earnings quality** | Truthfulness of statements vs sustainability and adequacy of actual returns |
| **Normal vs discretionary accruals** | Actual accruals − predicted normal accruals = abnormal residual |
| **Earnings persistence** | Cash component persists longer; abnormal accruals accelerate mean reversion |
| **Accounts receivable turnover** | Credit sales ÷ average AR; DSO = 365 ÷ turnover |
| **Cost capitalization** | Current profit ↑, assets ↑; future amortization ↑ |
| **Core pretax earnings** | Add back genuinely non-recurring expenses; retain recurring expenses |
| **Beneish vs Altman** | Beneish: lower better. Altman: higher better |
| **Merton vs hazard** | Merton uses market-based option logic; hazard models default over time |
| **Auditor's opinion** | Historical and generally lagging; internal controls can also be audited |
| **Deferred tax allowance understated** | Deferred tax assets ↑, tax expense ↓ |
| **Pension discount rate reduced** | Pension liability ↑, equity ↓, leverage ↑ |
| **IFRS interest paid moved to financing** | Reported CFO ↑; total cash unchanged |

### Three questions to ask before answering

**1. Did the business actually improve, or did its accounting numbers merely improve?**

**2. What exactly moves—the asset, liability, income, cash flow, numerator, or denominator?**

**3. Does the evidence establish a conclusion, or merely raise suspicion?**
