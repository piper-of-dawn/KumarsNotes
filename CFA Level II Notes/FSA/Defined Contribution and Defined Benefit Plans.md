# Defined Benefit


Under both IFRS and US GAAP, the balance sheet generally reports only the net pension liability.

NET LIABILITY = PV of DBO -  PV of Plan Assets


The net pension liability rises when the present value of liabilities increases or the present value of assets falls. Under IFRS, we use the same discount rate for both, so manipulating it cannot alter the net liability position on the balance sheet.




> The mechanics work like this: each month or quarter, the employer contributes a set amount, which flows into and increases plan assets. To pay retirees, the employer sells plan assets and uses the proceeds for benefits. Assets decline, and benefit obligations decline with them. The operating cash outflow is simply her employer contribution.

Net interest Expense = (DBO - PA) ⨯ r, where r is the assumed discount rate. The actual return on plan assets does not affect net interest expense.

Under IFRS, we cannot use different discount rates to calculate interest on obligations and assets, but US GAAP permits it.

Remember under IFRS, service costs are operating expense, net interest expense are financing expense and remeasurements, actuarial gains/losses go to OCI.

1. What is the difference between long term and post employment benefits?  Sabbatical and graduate benefits are long-term benefits, whereas defined pension is a post-employment benefit.
2. Grant is when the company promises it, vesting is when the employee earns it, and settlement is when the company actually pays.
3. **Expense is generally recognized when employees earn compensation, not when the company pays it.**

# Share Based Compensation

Suppose HSBC grants me 100 stocks @ $90 per stock to be vested over 3 years. Straight-line expense = $3000 every year

|Year|Actual Share Price|Compensation Expense|
|----|------------------|--------------------|
|2026|88                |3000                |
|2027|80                |3000                |
|2028|110               |3000                |

Compensation Expense decreases pre-tax profit by $3000 every year. ==**This does not reduce the cash taxes paid today.==** HSBC will create a DTA of tax rate ⨯ 3000. 

This 3000 is added to the share-based compensation reserve under equity on the liabilities side of the balance sheet, leaving total equity unchanged.

**Now what happens during settlement at 2028, when I indeed vest the stock.**
- Share price is $110. Total Value of Shares awarded = 110 ⨯ 100 = $11000.  
- No compensation expense is recognised by HSBC. $9000 is already accounted for. 
- Share-based compensation reserve is moved to additional paid-in capital within the equity subhead.

**The tax treatment is the only part with teeth.**  Now in the tax accounting HSBC would deduct the market value of shares vested i.e. $11,000 from its pre-tax income. So its tax base would be reduced by $11,000. **This will generate a real cash tax windfall of tax rate ⨯ 11,000.** 

==**The DTA created will disappear and excess tax windfall will go to the income statement under US GAAP and directly to equity under IFRS.**==
