## Different Types of Publicly Traded Real Estate Securities

### REIT

1. REITs dodge corporate income tax, but the trade-off is a forced payout: they have to distribute at least 90% of their earnings to shareholders.
2. No more family-owned command centers: the company needs at least 100 owners, and no five can hold more than 50% of it in US.
3. Another major drawback of REITs is that they can move in step with stocks (high correlation with equities), leaving your diversification with less room to work.

### REOC

4. If you can't form an REIT, you end up with an REOC: the real estate world's broker-and-builder outfit. It skips the REIT constraints, but the tax bill comes as corporate income tax.

### Commercial MBS

5. Mortgage-backed securities turn a pile of mortgages into a cash-flow buffet: investors are betting on what the borrowers pay back. Bank -> SPE -> Investors.

### Mortgage REIT

6. These guys borrow money, buy a pile of MBS, and pocket the spread. 

## Three Approaches to Valuation

### NOI Approach (Direct Capitalisation Method)

1. NOI is the EBITDA of real estates. It is a hard cash based approach
2. $$ \text{Cap Rate} = \frac{\text{NOI}}{\text{Property Value}} $$. It is the RRE of real estates. Given a discount rate and a growth rate, the calculation is simply R minus G: the discount rate minus the growth rate.
3. To calculate NOI, strip out non-cash rent and fixed Capex
4. Adjustment for acquisitions means adding the missing rental income from properties a REIT purchased partway through the year. Add it to NOI. Adjust for growth.
5. This gives pro-forma NOI. Divide by cap rate to get property value.

6. Add hard assets (anything except Goodwill and DTA) and subtract liabilities to get NAV.

### Use of Funds from Operations (FFO) Approach

1. You're working backward up the income statement, from net income to NOI or EBITDA: add back depreciation and losses on asset sales, then subtract gains.
2. ==Interest costs are NOT added back, they remain deducted from FFO. This is the main difference between FFO and NOI.==

### Adjusted Funds from Operations Approach

3. This is what FFO looks like after you run it through the cash register. Remove non-cash rent adjustment and remove Fixed Capex (plumber and electrician fees).
4. Since adjusted funds from operations subtracts fixed capital expenditures and sticks to hard cash, it's a sturdier stress test of whether a REIT can keep going. 
5. FFO multiple is Price / FFO. Two REITs might have identical P/FFO multiples but very different debt levels, because FFO does not care about leverage. While calculating NAVPS through NOI, we subtract debt.
6. FFO already deducts interest expenses, but the multiple doesn't explicitly tell you how heavily leveraged a REIT is. Higher leverage → greater financial risk → potentially lower justified P/FFO.
