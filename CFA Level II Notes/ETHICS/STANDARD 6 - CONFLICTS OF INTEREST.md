## Variant: Conflicts, trading priority, and the payment nobody mentioned

**Abstract:** *A conflict asks who could influence judgment; priority asks who got the trading opportunity first; referral disclosure asks who gets paid for the introduction. Test the recipient, timing, and remedy separately.*

> Your father manages a recommended fund, your parents hold client accounts, your employer approves an outside arrangement, and a referral payment arrives a year later. Which details change disclosure duties, and which merely distract you?

A conflict is not a confession of corruption. It is a reason another person might reasonably question your judgment. “But I know I'm objective” asks the client to solve the conflict by trusting the person who has it. Convenient arrangement. Incomplete answer.

**VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts — tell the exposed person**

> [!NOTE]
> Avoid conflicts where reasonably possible; otherwise give full, fair, prominent disclosure in plain language of matters reasonably expected to impair objectivity or interfere with duties to clients, prospects, and employer.

**Case 1.** Katz recommends a suitable hedge fund run and substantially owned by her father. She tells her employer and agrees to follow its advice about whether the client needs to know. Is that the strongest available answer?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The material family relationship needs client-facing disclosure too. Employer guidance cannot waive the client's entitlement to understand the recommendation's conflict. Suitability does not eliminate that duty.

**Change one fact:** assigning an independent analyst can avoid part of the conflict, but the firm's relevant relationship may still require disclosure. Conversely, automatically rejecting the best-fitting manager merely because a relative works there is not the universal requirement.

**Case 2.** A firm's analyst covers a bank in which her employer acquires a significant ownership stake. A report says only, “Our firm may have business relationships with issuers.” Is that enough?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Generic possibilities conceal a specific existing relationship. Explain the ownership conflict prominently; consider reassignment or discontinuing coverage where appropriate rather than assuming disclosure solves every problem.

The textbook often distinguishes **best practice** from the minimum response: avoiding a conflict can be preferable, while full disclosure may be the minimum in the stated circumstances. Do not turn every “should consider reassignment” into an automatic ban on issuing research.

**Case 3.** A broker helps a seller facing foreclosure by buying the property herself after other prospects cannot be reached. She waives her commission and sells later at a profit. Does the profit prove the original purchase was unethical?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Later profit alone does not establish earlier exploitation. But acting as agent and buyer creates a conflict: assess contemporaneous disclosure, informed agreement, price fairness, and the facts actually supplied.

**From your mistakes: Kozniak.** The stored key says no violation. The shortened stem does not fully spell out disclosure or agreed terms, so it cannot support “waiving commission cures self-dealing.” The sound lesson is that a later gain does not itself prove client harm, while actual agency conflicts still require proper treatment.

> [!NOTE]
> Disclose relevant personal/family interests, issuer and banking relationships, outside roles, and compensation incentives to those affected; update material changes. No cash payment or proven bias is necessary for a conflict to exist.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 165–170; VI(A), Examples 1–7. Katz and Kozniak are mistake-file cases, not named examples in this PDF.*

**VI(B) Priority of Transactions — identify the account, then the order**

> [!NOTE]
> Client and employer transactions have priority over transactions in which you are the beneficial owner. Clients must have a fair opportunity to act on recommendations before proprietary or personal advantage is taken.

**Case 4.** Peek fails to report an investment-club holding under his employer's annual disclosure policy. The club has not traded for a year and no transaction is planned. Which breach is least established: misrepresentation, conflicts, or transaction priority?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Transaction priority.** No competing trade or displaced client opportunity is shown. Nondisclosure may still create misrepresentation and conflict problems. One missing duty does not make the entire conduct acceptable.

**Case 5.** One manager buys scarce IPO shares for an account in her husband's name before buying for her fund. Another excludes her parents' regular fee-paying account from a suitable allocation solely because they are relatives. Does putting every family account last solve both?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The husband's beneficial account must not take priority over the fund. Genuine family client accounts deserve ordinary fair treatment; disclose and apply personal-trading controls if the manager also has beneficial ownership.

This corrects a dangerous shortcut in the old note: managing or controlling a client's account does not automatically turn it into your personal beneficial account. If that were true, every discretionary portfolio manager would personally own an alarming amount of the economy. Determine the economic interest and account relationship.

**Case 6.** A manager recommends a stock but sells some personal shares to pay school fees, after clients have had their opportunity and after appropriate employer notification and clearance. Is selling against a buy recommendation automatically prohibited?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Personal liquidity, taxes, or risk needs can differ from clients'. Do not misuse confidential information, harm clients, or bypass priority and firm controls. The opposite trade direction alone decides nothing.

**Case 7.** An analyst buys personally before publishing a favorable recommendation. The firm has no personal-trading policy. Is the policy gap a defense?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Client priority exists independently of an internal manual. The analyst's breach and any supervisor's inadequate-control breach must be assessed separately.

> [!NOTE]
> Blackouts, preclearance, beneficial-holdings disclosure, and duplicate confirmations are recommended safeguards, subject to applicable requirements. They protect priority; paperwork approval does not excuse an actual breach of it.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 170–175; VI(B), Examples 1–5; personal liquidity, practice question 57 and solution. Peek and the policy-gap case come from [[mistakes]].*

**VI(C) Referral Fees — disclose before the client commits**

> [!NOTE]
> Disclose referral compensation or benefits **paid or received** to the employer and affected clients or prospects as appropriate. Before the client enters the service agreement, explain the arrangement's nature and estimated value.

**Case 8.** Arun is paid by a pension sponsor to select a manager. He also has an undisclosed pre-existing agreement for a fee from the chosen manager. Payment arrives a year later, after the sponsor praises the results. Has the delayed payment made the original incentive harmless?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The hidden arrangement existed when the recommendation was made. Later satisfaction, delayed payment, or retrospective disclosure cannot undo that earlier nondisclosure.

**Important correction to the mistake key:** its answer says Arun should refuse the payment. That can be the appropriate response to collecting a benefit under the undisclosed arrangement; it does **not** establish that every disclosed referral payment must be refused. The supplied textbook's analogous Arrow case identifies VI(C) nondisclosure and notes that applicable law may separately prohibit some consultant payments. Do not invent such a law when the stem does not supply it.

**Case 9.** A trust officer gets an internal bonus for referring clients to the same bank's brokerage department. Another receives investment research rather than cash for referrals. Does either arrangement escape VI(C)?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Neither does merely because of its form.** Internal compensation and noncash benefits can influence a referral. Disclose the relevant terms and estimated value rather than relying on one logo or the absence of cash.

**Case 10.** An adviser arranges, with employer and school permission, for part of the advisory fee to support a school when parents become clients. The arrangement is discussed when the IPS is developed before engagement. Is the charitable element necessarily improper?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The textbook permits the properly authorized and disclosed school arrangement. Charitable purpose is neither a ban nor an exemption; the permissions, incentives, and client disclosure matter.

> [!NOTE]
> Describe whether compensation is one-time or ongoing, fixed or performance-linked, cash or noncash, and its estimated value. Recommended procedures include employer updates at least quarterly on approved programs' compensation.

**Three separate checks:** client disclosure explains your incentive; IV(B) consent handles an outside benefit conflicting with your employer; I(B) can still require refusing a benefit that reasonably compromises objectivity. One completed form does not answer all three questions.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 175–179; VI(C), Examples 1–5, especially internal referrals, the school arrangement, and Arrow.*
