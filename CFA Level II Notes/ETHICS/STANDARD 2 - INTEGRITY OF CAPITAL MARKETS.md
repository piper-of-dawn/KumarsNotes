## Variant: Information advantage versus manufactured market signals

**Abstract:** *For II(A), test the information's materiality and public availability. For II(B), identify the misleading distortion and intent. A profitable informational advantage is not automatically inside information or manipulation.*

> A short seller pays industry experts, builds a bearish thesis, shorts a stock, and publishes a report intended to make investors reconsider its price. Which extra fact would actually establish a violation?

The market is supposed to react to useful information. Otherwise research would be an unusually expensive diary. The exam's job is to make legitimate information gathering look suspicious and a prohibited shortcut look like clever analysis.

**II(A) Material Nonpublic Information — test the inputs**

> [!NOTE]
> Information must be **both material and nonpublic** to trigger II(A)'s trading restriction. A reasonable investor's interest, likely price impact, source reliability, and specificity inform materiality.

Material does not mean “guaranteed to change the price.” Nonpublic does not mean “nobody else knows.” A private briefing can contain fifty analysts and still be selective disclosure.

**Case 1.** An analyst combines public capacity data, ordinary retailer observations, and nonmaterial supplier comments to infer a major earnings decline. Another receives the unreleased earnings figure from the CFO and adds exactly the same analysis. Can both invoke mosaic theory?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Only the first.** A material conclusion can come from permitted inputs. Adding your own research to an already material nonpublic input does not make that input usable.

Mosaic theory concerns how you built the conclusion. It is not a blender into which you drop one forbidden fact and enough annual reports to improve the texture.

**Case 2.** A trader receives a vague earnings rumor from another trader with no known issuer connection. His portfolio manager already placed an independently justified order. A second trader receives specific trial results from a researcher working on an unreleased medical study. Must both stop all trading merely because the information is private?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The first message may be market noise, as in the textbook's Nadler example. The second has a specific, credible inside source and potentially material content. Assess each source and fact.

**Change one fact:** if the first trader knows the sender has an ongoing issuer relationship, the materiality and nonpublic-information assessment changes. “It was only a text” tells you the delivery method, not whether you may trade.

**Case 3.** An analyst learns credible, significant, undisclosed information about a founder's health. She proposes either confirming the diagnosis before issuing a sell report or urging management to disclose it publicly. Which is the appropriate route?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Encourage proper public dissemination and refrain from acting on the information.** Confirmation may establish reliability, but does not create permission to publish a recommendation based on MNPI.

**From your mistakes: Opdyke.** The problem is not that asking a question is inherently forbidden. It is that “confirm, then use” skips the public-dissemination requirement. Do not replace this with a blanket rule that every medical rumor is automatically material: assess importance and credibility in the stated circumstances.

**Case 4.** A prominent analyst develops a sell recommendation entirely from permitted information. Before publication, a television producer learns the forthcoming change and shorts the stock. The recommendation itself contains no company secret. Is the producer safe?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No, if the unreleased recommendation is material.** Advance knowledge of influential research can itself be MNPI, even when the analyst's underlying research process was legitimate.

> [!NOTE]
> The restriction reaches trading, recommendations, and causing others to act. Losses, family recipients, expert-network disclaimers, and lawful access for another purpose do not excuse misuse.

**When is information public?** Seek broad dissemination to the market, rather than selective disclosure to clients, analysts, or a restricted online group. After dissemination, allow a reasonable opportunity for the market to receive it; the standard does not impose one universal waiting period or require every investor to read it.

> [!NOTE]
> Receiving MNPI does not make you a public-disclosure authority. Seek compliance guidance and encourage issuer disclosure; do not announce confidential information or issue a trade call as a supposed cure.

A firm may also need a restricted list, information barriers, access records, and personal trading controls. Ringing compliance is useful because it changes how the information is handled, not because the phone call blesses the trade.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 64–76; II(A), Examples 3–12, especially mosaic theory, the producer, Nadler's rumor, and expert networks.*

**II(B) Market Manipulation — find the false signal**

> [!NOTE]
> II(B) prohibits price distortion or artificial trading volume with intent to mislead market participants. Identify both the conduct creating the distortion and the misleading intent; price impact alone is insufficient.

**Case 5.** A short seller publishes a well-supported negative report, discloses relevant conflicts, and expects the price to fall. Another circulates misleading allegations and coordinates a publicity campaign to generate artificial selling pressure. Is wanting a lower price enough to convict both?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Legitimate bearish research can promote price discovery. The second scheme supplies the misleading mechanism. Do not infer manipulation solely from a short position and hoped-for price decline.

**From your mistakes: Cardinal.** The stored answer flags the publicity campaign. Its explanation goes too far if read as “publish after shorting, intend a price decline, therefore manipulate.” Test deceptive distortion. Also assess II(A) separately if the research uses **material** nonpublic information; paying lobbyists or learning something not yet public does not by itself settle that test.

**Case 6.** An exchange temporarily discounts trading fees to attract volume to a new futures contract. It fully discloses the program. Another advertises its launch volume as organic demand while concealing an arrangement designed to manufacture that impression. Same violation?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The textbook permits the fully disclosed launch incentive. Concealing manufactured liquidity can mislead users about the market they are entering. The discount itself is not the deciding fact.

**Case 7.** A manager repeatedly trades a thin stock between two controlled funds to display activity and draw outside buyers. Both funds would benefit from a more liquid exit. Does the client benefit justify the scheme?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The apparent liquidity and price signal are artificial. A client-serving objective does not authorize misleading the rest of the market.

**Change one fact:** a genuine portfolio reduction in an illiquid market can move prices without being manipulative. Ask whether the trade serves a legitimate strategy or manufactures a signal to deceive others.

**Case 8.** An analyst exaggerates an earnings forecast to provoke a company response, intending to create volatility. She says she only wants management to clarify the truth. Is that a defense?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** A misleading forecast designed to induce artificial volatility violates II(B); an inadequate research basis can also violate V(A). A candid range of supported outcomes would be different.

> [!NOTE]
> Watch for false rumors, deceptive orders, sham volume, manipulated model inputs, and cornering an underlying instrument to exploit a related derivative. Neither personal profit nor a successful price move is necessary for a violation.

**Two standards can apply to one story.** Trading on confidential trial results is an information-use problem under II(A). Inventing trial results to move the stock is a misleading-market-signal problem under II(B). Doing both does not make you versatile in a useful way.
