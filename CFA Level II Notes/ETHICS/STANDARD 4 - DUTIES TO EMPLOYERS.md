## Variant: Leaving, earning extra, and supervising without outsourcing responsibility

**Abstract:** *For loyalty, track timing and ownership. For outside benefits, identify the conflict and obtain the required consent. For supervision, distinguish an imperfect outcome from an inadequate control system.*

> An employee prepares a competing business, teaches for a fee, takes a model to a new employer, and accepts responsibility for a department with weak controls. Which actions depend on permission, timing, or facts still missing?

Your employer does not own your future. It usually does own the client records you were planning to email to that future. Most IV(A) questions become easier once you stop treating those as the same thing.

**IV(A) Loyalty — preparation, competition, and property**

> [!NOTE]
> While employed, act for the employer's benefit without misusing confidential information, diverting business, or depriving it of your skills. Employment continues until departure is effective, not merely until you decide to leave.

**Case 1.** Jacobs teaches an unrelated weekend course for a fee and prepares marketing materials for a future firm using her own resources. She has not solicited clients, competed for a mandate, or impaired her current work. Is either activity automatically prohibited?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Payment alone does not prove a competing or conflicting activity. Preparation can comply. Check actual competition, interference, confidentiality, and employer policies rather than assuming all outside work is forbidden.

**Change one fact:** submitting a competing proposal for a mandate your employer is pursuing is actual competition, even if the future firm has no office yet. Starting paid consulting in services your employer offers also requires employer consent before providing those services.

**From your mistakes: Davidson.** His paid trust consulting competes with his employer; doing it at home does not remove the need for consent. Meetings arranged after his departure are a separate action. Do not infer that those meetings used a stolen list merely because a list appears earlier in the story. Equally, independently assess any improper compilation or removal of employer records. An answer about the meetings is not an acquittal of every action in the vignette.

> [!NOTE]
> Preparing to compete is different from competing. Before providing competing independent services, disclose their nature, duration, and compensation and obtain employer consent to the terms; assess IV(B) written-consent duties where applicable.

**Case 2.** Piedmont brings an original model he created privately before joining his former firm. Another analyst takes the latest employer-developed version of a model she wrote while employed. Both find the files on their home computers. Does location determine ownership?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Piedmont's pre-employment original can remain his absent contrary rights or agreements. Employer work product does not become personal property because a copy is at home or its author worked weekends.

Do not invent a licensing contract or a universal rule of intellectual-property law. The useful facts are origin, employment purpose, resources, ownership terms, and permission. Your skills and general knowledge travel with you. Your employer's records need their own ticket.

**Case 3.** After leaving, one analyst contacts former clients using public sources, with no agreement prohibiting it. Another uses a CRM export saved before departure. Both clients willingly move. Are the clients' choices sufficient?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Post-departure solicitation can be permissible; unauthorized use of employer records remains a breach. Voluntary client migration does not retrospectively authorize taking the list.

**Case 4.** Webb updates LinkedIn after employment ends and after her old firm has notified clients. The platform automatically alerts her mixed network. Her agreement prohibits solicitation, but not all contact. Is the notification necessarily solicitation?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The textbook permits the factual status update on these facts. A targeted invitation to transfer assets, or an agreement prohibiting all contact, would change the analysis.

The mistake-file version omits some of the textbook's safeguards. Keep them in view. Automatic delivery is not a general exemption: inspect content, timing, applicable agreement, and any firm information in the network. Discuss mixed business/personal networks before departure as the guidance recommends.

> [!NOTE]
> Factual departure notice may comply if consistent with employer procedures; pre-departure solicitation generally does not. Loyalty does not require illegal obedience or sacrificing every personal obligation. Client-protective whistleblowing can be justified.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 116–127; IV(A), Examples 1–15, especially preparation, public-source contact, whistleblowing, and Webb (Example 14, printed p. 126).*

**IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements — awareness is not consent**

> [!NOTE]
> Do not accept outside gifts, benefits, or compensation competing with or reasonably expected to conflict with your employer's interests without **written consent from all parties involved**. Retrievable email can count.

**Case 5.** A client proposes a luxury holiday for every future year in which the account earns 15%. You email the terms to your boss, who does not respond. You accept after the target is achieved. Is the email sufficient?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** This is a prospective incentive arrangement requiring consent before acceptance. Sending information is disclosure; silence is not documented approval. Achieving the target does not change the original arrangement.

**Case 6.** After a strong year, an existing client spontaneously offers a gift, with no prior promise or future condition. You disclose it before acceptance, consistently with firm policy. Is it necessarily the same IV(B) failure as Case 5?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The textbook's I(B) retrospective client-gift example permits acceptance with appropriate disclosure. Do not impose the prospective incentive rule mechanically; assess the facts, conflicts, and firm policy.

The origin and terms matter. Under I(B), disclose client gifts before acceptance where possible, and afterwards if prior notification was impossible. A gift can still be too compromising, prohibited by policy, or part of an ongoing compensation arrangement; retrospective timing is not a blanket exemption.

**Case 7.** A director receives free family gym membership instead of cash. She recommends the company's shares where suitable and says nothing to her employer because she is “unpaid.” Does the absence of cash resolve IV(B)?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Membership is a benefit for services. Noncash compensation can create the same divided loyalty as cash; suitability does not replace the necessary disclosure and consent.

> [!NOTE]
> Disclose the nature, approximate value, and duration of proposed benefits, with terms confirmed by the payer. Revisit material changes. Permission for an outside arrangement does not authorize unsuitable trades, favoritism, or compromised research.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 127–129; IV(B), Examples 1–3; compare I(B), Example 8, printed pp. 41–42.*

**IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors — reasonable systems, actual enforcement**

> [!NOTE]
> Make reasonable efforts to prevent **and detect** breaches by anyone under your supervision or authority, including people outside the CFA Program. Delegating tasks does not eliminate oversight responsibilities.

A code of ethics states principles. Compliance procedures explain who checks what, when, how problems are escalated, and what stops a breach from continuing. A framed code without those mechanisms is a Potemkin village: an impressive compliance façade with very little behind it.

**Case 8.** Inkster is offered a supervisory role where misleading advertisements and inadequate procedures make effective supervision clearly impossible. She plans to accept, tell senior management, and fix things later. What should she do instead?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Decline supervisory responsibility in writing until reasonable procedures exist.** This follows when she clearly cannot discharge the role, rather than whenever any imperfection exists in a firm's controls.

The mistake-file explanation overstates this as automatic liability for every outstanding violation the instant she joins. The textbook's test is whether reasonable supervision is possible and whether she fulfills her own duties. Prior wrongdoing is not automatically inherited like office furniture.

**Case 9.** A junior employee front-runs a recommendation. One supervisor had reasonable controls, training, monitoring, and enforcement, but the employee evaded them. Another knew required personal-trade forms were missing and allowed unrestricted activity. Are both necessarily in breach?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Employee misconduct does not establish automatic supervisor liability. The second supervisor ignored a known control failure; assess the first supervisor's reasonable efforts on their facts.

**From your mistakes: Musa and Kassim.** Kassim cannot defend front-running by pointing to absent firm policies. Musa's supervisory failure depends on her responsibility and failure to establish or enforce adequate controls, not merely her compliance job title or discovering a violation. Where those failures are established, both can violate separate standards.

**Case 10.** A supervisor discovers possible personal trading abuse, reports it upwards, and gets a written promise from the employee not to repeat it. She leaves the employee's activities unchanged while awaiting the investigation. Enough?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Assess the extent promptly and prevent recurrence through appropriate restrictions or increased monitoring. Reporting, warnings, and assurances alone do not finish the supervisory job.

> [!NOTE]
> Use documented procedures, recurring training, monitoring, enforcement, and suitable incentives. Limited firm-wide authority requires escalation and work within your powers; if supervision clearly remains impossible, decline that responsibility in writing.
