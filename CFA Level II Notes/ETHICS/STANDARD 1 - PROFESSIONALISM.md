## Variant: Professionalism when the sensible excuse is still wrong

**Abstract:** *Identify the rule that actually applies, then separate independent judgment, truthful communication, honest conduct, and competence. The trap is usually a respectable excuse that solves a different problem.*

> An analyst has legal advice, employer approval, a grateful client, and a profitable recommendation. Which of those facts actually establishes compliance with Standard I?

Ethics questions rarely announce, “Here comes a corrupt person.” More often, someone has a reasonable explanation and has quietly skipped the one obligation that matters. Read the facts before deciding whether you like the person.

**I(A) Knowledge of the Law — whose rules apply?**

> [!NOTE]
> Identify **applicable** law first; then follow the stricter requirement of that law and the Code and Standards. Nationality and headquarters do not automatically settle jurisdiction.

“Stricter” means the higher obligation or tighter restriction for the conduct in question. A permissive market does not switch off CFA duties. Equally, a stricter law somewhere on Earth does not become applicable just because you have heard of it.

**Case 1.** A manager works in Country B, where applicable law prohibits personal IPO participation. Her home country allows it, and her employer approves a purchase after clients receive allocations. Can she buy?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Applicable law prohibits it. Client priority and employer approval address other issues; neither creates an exemption from that prohibition.

**Change one fact:** if Country B permits participation, the legal prohibition disappears, but personal trading, allocation, and conflict requirements still need checking. “Legal” gets you through one door.

**Case 2.** You discover omitted losing accounts in a marketing composite. Counsel says a regulator would struggle to prove intent. You alert compliance and continue using the brochure while it investigates. Have you done enough?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** You now know the presentation is misleading. Seek correction and dissociate from its use; uncertainty about enforcement is not permission to participate.

Dissociation means separating yourself from the offending activity: stop distributing the material, remove your name, refuse the assignment, or leave the role if lesser measures cannot work. It does not automatically mean resigning from the entire firm immediately.

> [!NOTE]
> Know the laws relevant to your responsibilities and stay current. External reporting is not universally required by I(A); check whether applicable law requires it. Internal escalation alone cannot justify continuing misconduct.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 23–32; I(A), Examples 1–5 and 8.*

**I(B) Independence and Objectivity — who is paying, and for what?**

> [!NOTE]
> Reject benefits reasonably expected to compromise your own or another person's objectivity. You do not need proof that the benefit successfully changed the decision.

**Case 3.** Two managers receive the same expensive tennis tickets and lodging. One receives them from a broker to whom she directs commissions. The other receives them from an existing client as an unsolicited thank-you for completed performance, and discloses the gift to her employer under its policy. Same answer?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The broker's substantial gift threatens independent broker selection. The disclosed, retrospective client gift can comply. A prospective performance reward needs separate IV(B) consent analysis.

The ticket price is identical. The incentive is not. The textbook deliberately pairs these situations because “expensive gift = violation” is a memorized shortcut that stops halfway through the facts. Client gifts still need disclosure and scrutiny for favoritism toward that client.

> [!NOTE]
> Disclose client gifts to your employer before acceptance when possible; if prior notification is impossible, disclose accepted benefits afterwards. Prospective conflicting compensation arrangements require the separate IV(B) written consent.

**Case 4.** An issuer offers a disclosed flat research fee plus a bonus if your report attracts investors. Your analysis is genuinely favorable. Is the bonus acceptable because it will not change your conclusion?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The payment structure reasonably threatens objectivity. Use an appropriate fixed fee independent of conclusions or investor response, preserve independence, and disclose issuer sponsorship.

**Case 5.** A company arranges basic lodging and necessary charter transport to a remote site. A competing manager offers an educational holiday with its president. Both trips contain useful information. Must both be refused?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Necessary, modest business travel can comply; the manager-funded hospitality may taint selection. Educational content does not make a holiday independent due diligence.

**From your mistakes: Sato and Peters.** A friendship, an introduction, and a later job offer look suspicious. They do not, on their own, establish a kickback. Use the stated merit-based selection facts. However, do not turn this into “no explicit quid pro quo, no possible violation”: reasonably compromising benefits and material undisclosed relationships can create problems without a confessed bargain.

> [!NOTE]
> Promising **research coverage** can be acceptable; promising a favorable rating is not. Pressure from bankers, salespeople, issuers, or your boss cannot determine your investment opinion.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 32–45; I(B), Examples 1–10 and 13–16. Sato/Peters is a mistake-file case, not a named example in this PDF.*

**I(C) Misrepresentation — an accurate detail can support a false impression**

> [!NOTE]
> Do not knowingly mislead through statements, omissions, attribution, or presentation. Check what a reasonable reader will understand, rather than whether each isolated sentence can survive an argument.

**Case 6.** A manager advertises that risky bonds “will return up to 12%.” When conditions change, she returns all client capital. Another describes 12% as a conditional estimate with meaningful downside disclosure. Have both guaranteed returns?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Read the whole representation. An unsupported promise is misleading; a properly qualified forecast need not be. Returning capital later does not erase the earlier promise.

**From your mistakes: Zapata.** The stored answer targets the promised return. Do not learn that the words “up to” always create a guarantee, or always avoid one. They are not a legal force field. A real product guarantee may be described accurately, including its guarantor, scope, and conditions; protection of one feature does not guarantee every investment outcome.

**Case 7.** You modify another analyst's model and test it extensively. You credit the original model, identify your changes, and present your test results. A colleague copies the model's explanation, changes a few words, and calls it original. Who has the attribution problem?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **The colleague.** Modification does not erase authorship. You may claim your own implementation and testing while crediting the original idea and borrowed material.

There are useful limits to the attribution rule. Widely recognized factual information from established statistical services does not generally require citation in the same way as borrowed analytical ideas or writing. Firm-authorized research can also remain in use after its author leaves. That does not let an employee pretend to have personally done someone else's work.

> [!NOTE]
> Verify third-party information before passing it on; correct errors once discovered. Clearly distinguish actual from simulated results, and avoid valuation shopping, stale information, or benchmarks that create a misleading comparison.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 46–56; I(C), especially plagiarism guidance and Examples 1–4, 10, and 14–16.*

**I(D) Misconduct — separate bad conduct from disapproved lifestyles**

> [!NOTE]
> Dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or conduct reflecting adversely on professional reputation, integrity, or competence can violate I(D), including conduct outside work. A legal charge is neither necessary nor sufficient by itself.

**Case 8.** One analyst is arrested during peaceful civil disobedience. Another falsifies an insurance claim at home. Neither incident concerns a client portfolio. Are both outside I(D)?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Personal fraud reflects on integrity. Peaceful civil disobedience need not do so merely because it breaks a law. Identify what the conduct demonstrates about professional trustworthiness.

**Change one fact:** drinking socially is not automatically misconduct. Being intoxicated while making professional decisions can reflect adversely on competence. The relevant fact is impaired professional conduct, not whether you approve of the person's evening.

> [!NOTE]
> An honest error is not automatically deceit, though competence or diligence may still be deficient. Do not use the Professional Conduct Program to settle personal or competitive grievances.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 57–59; I(D), Examples 1–5.*

**I(E) Competence — the charter does not install every skill**

> [!NOTE]
> Maintain the knowledge, skill, and diligence needed for your actual role. Competence is ongoing and role-specific; CFA Institute does not prescribe one mandatory continuing-education program here.

**Case 9.** A successful equity analyst is promoted to derivatives manager. She identifies her knowledge gaps, obtains training and specialist support, and limits her activity until capable. A colleague accepts the same role and immediately recommends structures he cannot explain, using a vendor model. Is accepting the promotion the violation?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Not by itself.** The first analyst manages the transition responsibly. The colleague acts without the competence needed for his responsibilities; a vendor's expertise does not automatically become his own.

Users need enough understanding of model assumptions, limitations, and outputs for their duties. Developers and supervisors need deeper knowledge appropriate to their responsibilities. You need not build every engine, but you do need to notice when the recommended journey requires wings.

> [!NOTE]
> A loss does not prove incompetence, and a profit does not prove competence. Ask what the professional knew, learned, checked, and could responsibly do **when acting**.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 60–63; I(E), Examples 1–6. The supplied 2026 textbook includes I(E); older four-part summaries of Standard I are incomplete.*

**Before choosing an answer:** name the obligation, identify the fact establishing a breach, and check whether the proposed remedy addresses that same obligation. Approval, disclosure, good intentions, and good results are different things. None is a universal substitute for the others.
