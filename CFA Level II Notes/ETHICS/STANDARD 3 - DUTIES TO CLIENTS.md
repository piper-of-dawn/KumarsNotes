## Variant: Protecting the right client without inventing extra prohibitions

**Abstract:** *Identify the person or mandate you serve, then test prudent process, fair treatment, suitability, honest performance, and confidentiality separately. Consent to one thing does not waive every other duty.*

> A pension sponsor wants one decision, a beneficiary needs another, a client insists on a trade, and a charity could benefit from a disclosure. Who gets to authorize what?

The hardest client-duty questions let someone do something decent for the wrong person. The sponsor pays the invoice. The charity does good work. The client is enthusiastic. All useful background. None answers the question until you identify the duty.

**III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care — who actually owns the benefit?**

> [!NOTE]
> Act for clients' benefit with reasonable care and prudent judgment, placing their interests before your employer's or your own. In pension management, protect participants and beneficiaries rather than sponsor management.

A trust's governing documents, the relationship, and your role determine the duties. Do not turn “look through the fee payer” into “a trustee can never be the client.” For a pooled fund, follow its mandate; an adviser selecting the fund must assess the individual client's needs. An execution-only trader's care centers on executing the client's instructions diligently, rather than automatically acquiring an advisory mandate.

**Case 1.** A sponsor urges its pension manager to oppose a takeover. The manager independently concludes that opposition benefits participants. Another manager opposes it solely to protect sponsor executives. Are both disloyal because the sponsor wanted the same vote?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Only the second is shown to put the sponsor first.** The first reaches an independently supported beneficiary-focused decision. Agreement with management is not evidence of surrendering judgment.

**From your mistakes: Harris.** A retirement-committee member cannot justify knowingly favoring the employer by saying he preserved corporate confidentiality. But protecting beneficiaries does **not** authorize trading or tipping on MNPI. Seek a compliant resolution through appropriate counsel/compliance; the vignette's two duties must both survive the answer.

**Case 2.** A domestic-equity manager uses client commissions for offshore research unrelated to any managed strategy. Another receives irrelevant research incidentally from the broker offering the lowest commission and best execution, without paying extra. Same misuse of soft dollars?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Paying client brokerage for irrelevant services is different from receiving incidental research at no extra cost while achieving best execution. Identify what the client actually pays for.

**From your mistakes: Steyn.** The offshore database fails the stated domestic-equity benefit test. Do not learn “research is always fine” or “conference attendance is always fine.” Examine the service: relevant investment research may qualify; education, travel perks, software for running the firm, and unrelated services do not become client research because they are useful to you.

> [!NOTE]
> Client brokerage is a client asset. Seek best execution, judge research costs against investment benefit, and disclose practices. Directed brokerage needs appropriate disclosure of execution consequences; it does not authorize churning or self-benefit.

Proxy voting deserves a similar cost-benefit judgment: vote in clients' interests, but not necessarily every proxy regardless of expense. Safeguard and segregate client assets; when controlling them, follow the guidance on at least quarterly itemized reporting and encourage custody-statement reconciliation.

> [!NOTE]
> Prudence means a careful process appropriate to the client's objectives and constraints. It does not mean the lowest-risk asset, a guaranteed profit, or permission to sacrifice the client after disclosing a conflict.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 82–91; III(A), Examples 1–11; LM2 practice solutions 25–29, printed pp. 215–216.*

**III(B) Fair Dealing — fair access, then personal service**

> [!NOTE]
> Treat clients fairly and objectively in recommendations and investment actions. Different disclosed service levels can exist; preferential access to an important recommendation or scarce investment is a separate question.

**Case 3.** A firm emails its new recommendation to all relevant clients, then phones premium clients to discuss it. Another firm phones premium clients first and emails everybody else after those clients have traded. Both disclose that premium clients receive calls. Which process complies?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **The first can comply.** Extra follow-up after fair dissemination is allowed. Calling it a service tier does not cure selective advance access in the second process.

**Case 4.** A bond issue is heavily oversubscribed. The manager gives every eligible client the minimum tradable lot, then allocates the remainder using a consistent objective method. Another assigns attractive fills after seeing which trades performed best. Must the first allocation be exactly pro rata?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** The textbook permits a fair minimum-lot adjustment. Allocating after observing outcomes to favor selected clients is a different process and violates fair dealing.

**Case 5.** A parent is a normal fee-paying client, with no beneficial ownership by the manager. To avoid favoritism, the manager excludes the parent's otherwise eligible account from a hot issue. Is that prudent conflict management?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** A genuine family client deserves fair treatment. Deliberately disadvantaging that client is not the cure for favoritism. A manager's own beneficial account requires personal-trading controls.

> [!NOTE]
> Allocate through a systematic, disclosed, consistently applied process. For the textbook's oversubscribed IPO cases, pro rata allocation among eligible clients is the normal answer; do not substitute “any method I disclosed.”

**A recommendation changes before an order arrives.** If a client orders against a recent change they may not have received, ensure they know the change before executing. Do not silently override the client. Fair dealing gets them the relevant information; suitability and your agreed mandate govern the next decision.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 91–99; III(B), Examples 1–10, especially Examples 6–7; practice solutions 34–37, printed p. 217.*

**III(C) Suitability — the whole portfolio and the actual mandate**

> [!NOTE]
> In an advisory relationship, reasonably inquire into experience, objectives, risk tolerance, and constraints; assess investments in total-portfolio context. Review and update the IPS at least annually and before material changes to recommendations or decisions.

An investment policy statement (**IPS**) records objectives and constraints. It is a working description of the client, not a souvenir from the account-opening meeting. Risk willingness and financial capacity are distinct; a windfall can increase capacity without automatically changing willingness.

**Case 6.** Two clients want the same volatile stock. One has a diversified, wealthy portfolio and a small speculative allocation. The other needs the proposed investment amount for near-term living expenses. Does the security's risk classify both trades the same way?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Total-portfolio fit and liquidity needs differ. A risky security can have a suitable limited role; a respectable security can be unsuitable for a particular client.

**Case 7.** A client insists on a purchase outside the IPS. It is small enough not to affect the overall portfolio materially. Another request would substantially alter the portfolio's risk. Is “the client insisted” sufficient documentation for both?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Discuss the mismatch. A limited trade may proceed after the client understands its effect; a material change requires revisiting the IPS, and unresolved incompatibility may require a separate account or reconsidering the relationship.

This is more nuanced than “obey every client order” or “refuse every unsuitable request.” Establish the advisory relationship, significance, informed discussion, and whether the governing mandate can properly change. An execution-only relationship is different.

**Case 8.** A zero-dividend growth stock looks exceptionally attractive to the manager of a fund explicitly requiring dividend-paying investments. Can a small position be justified because the fund remains mostly income-oriented?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Not if it falls outside the permitted mandate.** Expected return and small size do not authorize undisclosed style drift. Check what the stated mandate actually allows.

> [!NOTE]
> A pooled-fund manager follows the fund's mandate; the adviser recommending that fund evaluates the client's suitability. If a client withholds information, explain the limits and assess using available facts rather than inventing a complete financial picture.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 99–107; III(C), especially Examples 1–8 and guidance on unsolicited requests.*

**III(D) Performance Presentation — permission, credit, and context**

> [!NOTE]
> Make reasonable efforts to present performance fairly, accurately, and completely. Correct arithmetic does not cure cherry-picking, misleading attribution, inconsistent methodology, or omitted context.

**Case 9.** Paper has written permission to show the results of a fund managed by five people. He presents them as evidence of his own success without explaining the team. Which problem remains: unauthorized use or misleading attribution?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Misleading attribution.** Permission solves the use-of-records issue. It does not give one manager the other four managers' contribution. Explain the team, role, and relevant performance context.

That is why “he had written permission” is such an effective distractor. It is a correct answer to a question you were not asked.

**Case 10.** Chen shows the available multi-year history and clearly identifies recent figures as unaudited. He does not claim GIPS compliance. Assuming the presentation is accurate and otherwise fair, must he obtain an audit before showing it?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** III(D) does not universally require GIPS adoption, an audit, or verification. A false claim of GIPS compliance would create a separate problem.

**Case 11.** A firm changes how it treats an external cash flow immediately before an acquired holding jumps in price, solely to capture the gain. The new method is mathematically defensible. Does that settle compliance?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Opportunistically deviating from established policy distorts comparison. A genuine methodology change needs fair, consistent treatment and appropriate disclosure, not selective application to the winning transaction.

> [!NOTE]
> Clearly identify simulated results, relevant periods, gross/net fee and tax treatment, prior-firm context, and material limitations. Retain terminated portfolios in historical results where relevant; a disclaimer cannot repair a misleading main claim.

A short presentation can be acceptable when fair on its own and supported by detail available on request. Analysts promoting the success of their recommendations are also covered. The requirement follows the performance claim, rather than the job title.

*Textbook anchor: LM2, printed pp. 107–112; III(D), Examples 1–8; practice questions 39–40 and solutions, printed pp. 217–218. Chen is from [[mistakes]].*

**III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality — sending information toward clients is different from sending client information out**

> [!NOTE]
> Protect current, former, and prospective clients' confidential information. Exceptions concern client permission, disclosure required by law, or information concerning client illegal activity; applicable law still governs what you may disclose.

**Case 12.** Townsend mentions a charity's fundraiser and her board role in the newsletter she already sends clients. Another adviser gives the charity a spreadsheet of wealthy clients and their contact details. Both want to raise money. Who has the confidentiality problem?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **The adviser sharing client information.** Townsend sends the appeal to clients; she does not disclose their information to the charity. The textbook's question tests III(E), despite the mistake file's I(B) label.

**Case 13.** An adviser tells a charity that a prospective donor recently received a large inheritance, hoping the charity and client will both benefit. No account has been opened yet. Does the absence of a formal relationship remove confidentiality?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **No.** Prospects are protected too. A charitable motive and a hoped-for benefit do not replace the client's permission.

**Case 14.** An adult child says a client is being exploited and requests account details. Another adviser uses a secondary contact previously authorized by the client, within that authorization. Are both disclosures justified by concern?

> [!NOTE]- Answer and decisive fact
> **Only the authorized route has an established permission basis.** Concern does not give relatives automatic access. Check applicable law, authority, scope, and firm procedures before acting.

> [!NOTE]
> Share internally only with authorized colleagues working on the client's matter. Cooperation with CFA Institute's Professional Conduct Program is not barred by III(E), unless applicable law prevents disclosure.

If a client posts private details to a group platform despite reasonable warnings, the adviser is not automatically in breach. Examine safeguards and the response. Conversely, if illegality is suspected, do not broadcast it to anyone who seems helpful: seek legal/compliance guidance on the applicable reporting and confidentiality rules.
